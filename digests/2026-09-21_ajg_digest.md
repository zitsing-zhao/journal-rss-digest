- [Strategic Inversion: How Refugees Reconfigure Everyday Resistance under Benevolent Oppression](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/00031224261476317?ai=2b4&mi=ehikzz&af=R)
  - Authors: Rekha Krishnan, Aloysius Marcus Kahindi, Rajiv Krishnan Kozhikode
  - Abstract:
    What does everyday resistance look like among dominated populations experiencing paternalistic care rather than extraction? Research on everyday resistance emerged from contexts where power extracts surplus from the oppressed. In this scholarship, the backstage is a space where the dominated cultivate hidden transcripts to cope with oppression, while the frontstage, where performances are enacted to those in power, is primary. Inversions of this order, when they occur, are momentary. Using ethnographic fieldwork in two Tanzanian refugee camps, we show that under benevolent oppression—control justified by paternalistic care rather than extraction—dominated populations respond through “strategic inversion”: a reordering of dramaturgical space where the backstage is primary and the frontstage remains tactical. Refugees moved the center of social life backstage, into a thriving underground economy, and kept up frontstage displays of helplessness to shield it. Within this alternative social order, competition and cooperation operated as two routes to heshima (dignity/deference). Competition resisted the uniformity of the refugee label, and cooperation rejected the helplessness it presumed. Refugees sustained this inversion through compliance flooding , rule-following performances so dense that officials could not tell forbidden from permitted activity; embedded enforcement , as guards became accomplices in maintaining the clandestine economy they policed; and collective coordination , where communities maintained alert systems and consistent performances to shield backstage activities. Our analysis extends research on everyday resistance by demonstrating that under benevolent oppression, subordinates actively build and experience empowerment, rather than merely imagining alternative moral orders or experiencing them briefly in bounded rituals.

- [Organizational Consolidation: How Organizations Reflect or Refract Societal Hierarchies](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/00031224261475238?ai=2b4&mi=ehikzz&af=R)
  - Authors: Brent Hoagland, Hana Shepherd
  - Abstract:
    To understand how organizations distribute resources, we need to understand how they come to evaluate their members as worthy. This article introduces “organizational consolidation”: the degree to which ascriptive statuses—race, gender, class—align with the context-specific criteria that actors use when evaluating one another. Building on relational inequality theory and inhabited institutionalism, we argue that organizational consolidation shapes which attributes stand out, how actors categorize one another, and how combinations of attributes yield recognition or marginalization. When consolidation is high, local meaning-making is more likely to reproduce patterns of recognition that track ascriptive statuses; when it is low, alternative patterns become possible. We illustrate the value of these tools in a small, racially diverse U.S. high school where consolidation is low: teachers evaluate students through combinations of race, gender, and organizationally relevant characteristics, rather than any single attribute. By formalizing organizational consolidation as a measurable structural condition, we provide a diagnostic for comparing how organizations reflect or refract broader social hierarchies—shifting focus to how and under what organizational conditions inequality takes the specific forms it does.

    ABSTRACT This meta‐analysis quantifies the effectiveness of workplace interventions to promote employees' pro‐environmental behaviour. We synthesised m = 400 effects from k = 52 studies using random‐effects meta‐analytic models with the state‐of‐the‐art robust variance estimation (RVE) procedure to account for dependencies between effect sizes.     While conventional software is programmed for specific tasks, AI learns and refines its capabilities through a two-stage process: pre-training and fine-tuning. Using an interactive experiment, we predict and find that how employees react to their manager's allocation of training resources depends on the prevailing fairness norms in the work environment.   - Authors: Richard Mills, Robin Cubitt, Chris Starmer
  - Published: 2026-09-18
  - Abstract:
    Abstract We report an extensive, independent replication study (n=2,374) of the Mere Urgency Effect (MUE) (Zhu et al. 2018). The MUE is a surprising tendency for individuals to be lured into sub-optimal time allocation by the framing of a dominated option that makes it appear ‘urgent’. This effect arises even though the apparent urgency is illusory. We replicate the MUE in a base design closely following the procedures of Zhu et al. (2018) but with new participants. - [Turning Uncertainty into Opportunity: Preference Uncertainty Can Be Beneficial When Consumers Have Outcome Uncertainty](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucag037)
  - Authors: Xiang Wang, Amin Shiri, Chris Janiszewski
  - Published: 2026-09-15
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Consumers can experience two types of uncertainty when purchasing a product. Preference uncertainty refers to the uncertainty associated with the horizontal product benefits one desires (e.g., “How strong do I want my coffee to be?”). Outcome uncertainty refers to the uncertainty associated with the actual benefits the product will deliver (e.g., “How strong will the coffee be?”). Ideally, retailers should strive for outcome certainty (i.e., standardize the strength profile of their coffee [coffees in their line]), and help consumers achieve preference certainty (i.e., help consumers learn their ideal coffee strength), so that consumers can purchase the appropriate product with confidence (i.e., achieve a preference-outcome match). - [Time-Duration Asymmetries in Consumer Decision-Making](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucag039)
  - Authors: Jiabi Wang, Kristin Donnelly
  - Published: 2026-09-15
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Consumers routinely make decisions about short time periods, such as waiting for a table or deciding whether to pay for faster delivery. Such periods are often expressed by either a duration or a clock time (e.g., if it is 6:00 PM now, “Your table will be ready in 30 minutes” vs. “Your table will be ready at 6:30 PM”). Eight experiments (N = 16,604) show that these logically equivalent frames change how long a period feels, with downstream consequences for consumer decisions. Relatively short time periods feel longer when framed by duration rather than by start and end time (i.e., clock times or calendar dates), but this pattern reverses as periods get longer. We propose that this occurs because duration frames evoke compressive scalar representations shaped by diminishing sensitivity, whereas start-end frames situate time within structured categorical systems that attenuate compression. This research offers a unified framework for understanding how subtle differences in temporal framing shape perceptions of time and subsequent decisions.
