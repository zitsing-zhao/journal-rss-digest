    ABSTRACT We develop and empirically test a model of information acquisition in capital markets. In the model, investors are uncertain about the returns to acquiring private information before they acquire it. - [Widely Used Measures of Overconfidence Are Confounded with Ability](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05184)
  - DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.2024.05184
  - Authors: Stephen A. Spiller
  - Affiliations: Anderson School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles, California 90095
  - Published: 2026-11-24
  - Crossref journal: Management Science
  - Abstract:
    Measures of overconfidence are used both to assess biases in people’s beliefs regarding their own abilities and to identify correlates of those biases. Proposed correlates of overconfidence include narcissism, social status, (low) task anxiety, savings, planning, and many more. Research on these correlates typically explicitly or implicitly claims that the associations with overconfidence are not attributable to ability (i.e., latent inputs enabling successful task performance, including inputs that vary across people, states, or contexts). I argue that several typical approaches used to measure overconfidence are not sufficient to support such claims. This argument follows from two underappreciated properties of these measures of overconfidence. First, performance is an imperfect measure of ability: accounting for performance does not sufficiently account for ability. Second, self-evaluations of performance should reflect ability in addition to performance: because performance is ambiguous, people should incorporate prior beliefs about their ability. These properties imply that multiple commonly used measures of overconfidence are confounded with ability. I support these analytical results by reexamining previously published findings. In the first analysis, I find that overconfidence predicts subsequent performance, consistent with measures of overconfidence serving as a signal of ability. In the second set of analyses, I find that some purported associations between overconfidence and other constructs could be attributed to ability instead. I close with recommendations to recognize and reduce the potential for mistaken inferences. When research indicates that differences in overconfidence are associated with other behaviors, beliefs, or evaluations, researchers should account for the possibility that differences in ability could provide a sufficient explanation.   - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Correctional work is difficult, dangerous, and hot. Many US prisons lack air‐conditioning, resulting in sweltering conditions for both incarcerated people and the street‐level bureaucrats tasked with their supervision. We examine the effect of working in the summer heat without air‐conditioning on correctional officer turnover using personnel records of nearly 60,000 Texas Department of Criminal Justice (TDCJ) employees across 68 prisons between 2010 and 2023.     Abstract Research Summary Entrepreneurs can observe the same evidence yet reach different conclusions about an opportunity.     Purpose Innovation in supply chains depends on technical coordination between buyers and suppliers, yet close coordination may constrain a firm's ability to search beyond established supplier interfaces.     Abstract Do race or merit make an international expert? While existing research has uncovered epistemic biases related to gender and professions, racial bias in international organisations’ expert selection has not been captured. We argue that signalling whiteness is central to the certification of expertise because expert status is built on racialised structures of epistemic power. Examining individual-level data from 2,053 applications and selection outcomes across several stages in 124 decision rounds between 2011 and 2024 for the United Nations’ Special Procedures, we show that expert selection is subject to racial bias when candidates are chosen for in-person interviews and how markers of whiteness and merit overlap. Our research informs scholarship on racism in international relations, the production of expertise in international organisations, and human rights politics.

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Arsine (AsH 3 ) is a toxic gas widely used in semiconductor manufacturing and metal refining, yet existing occupational exposure limits (OELs), including the US OSHA permissible exposure limit of 200 µg/m 3 (50 ppb) and the nonregulatory American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) threshold limit value of 16.0 µg/m 3 (5 ppb), lack modern, science‐based derivations. Although AsH 3 is not classified independently by International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), its primary in vivo metabolites, inorganic arsenic (iAs) species, are Group 1 human carcinogens. This study conducted an occupational risk assessment deriving recommended exposure limits (RELs) for both sub‐chronic hemotoxicity (REL non‐cancer ) and carcinogenic risk (REL cancer ). Benchmark dose (BMD) modeling was applied to continuous endpoints from a 90‐day inhalation bioassay. Circulating red blood cell count reduction in male rats was the most sensitive endpoint. The Hill model provided the optimal fit, yielding a BMD lower confidence limit (BMDL) of 0.050 ppm. Adjusting for occupational exposure duration (BMDL adj = 0.038 ppm) and applying an uncertainty factor of 100 established an REL non‐cancer of 1.17 µg/m 3 (0.38 ppb). Restricting risk to this threshold yielded an REL cancer of 0.2 µg/m 3 (0.06 ppb). Legacy international standards exceed these health‐protective REL cancer by one to three orders of magnitude, underscoring an urgent need to modernize workplace safety regulations across global semiconductor supply chains.
