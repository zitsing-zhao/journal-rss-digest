  - Affiliations: College of Business Administration, Capital University of Economics and Business Beijing China; School of Management Science and Engineering, University of Jinan Jinan Shandong China; Ivey Business School, University of Western Ontario London Ontario Canada; International Business School Suzhou, Xi'an Jiaotong‐Liverpool University Suzhou Jiangsu China; Department of Supply Chain Management, Broad College of Business Michigan State University East Lansing Michigan USA
  - Published: 2026-08-12
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Operations Management
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Carbon emissions trading schemes (CETS) are a prominent market‐based instrument for addressing climate change, yet their effects on corporate disclosure remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on institutional theory, we develop a pressure–capability–cognition framework to examine how CETS influence firms' environmental and social (E&S) disclosure and how institutional pressures and internal organizational factors shape heterogeneous disclosure responses. Exploiting the staggered implementation of China's CETS between 2013 and 2024 as a quasi‐natural experiment, we employ a multi‐period difference‐in‐differences design using 16,348 firm‐year observations from 1682 listed firms between 2010 and 2024. We report three principal findings. First, CETS significantly increase the level of firm E&S disclosure, indicating that the influence of CETS extends beyond environmental reporting to social responsibility disclosure. Second, institutional pressures generate distinct disclosure strategies. Under coercive participation, CETS exhibits a marginally significant positive effect on social disclosure but no significant effect on environmental disclosure, consistent with a legitimacy compensation mechanism whereby firms offset environmental legitimacy deficits through low‐cost, highly visible symbolic reporting. By contrast, under non‐coercive participation, CETS significantly promotes both environmental and social disclosure, suggesting that firms use disclosure as a strategic signaling device. Third, operational efficiency negatively moderates the CETS–disclosure relationship, whereas managerial environmental focus positively moderates this relationship, revealing that internal capabilities and managerial cognition condition the effectiveness of institutional pressures in shaping disclosure outcomes. These findings contribute to sustainable operations management research by demonstrating how institutional pressures interact with operational capabilities and managerial cognition to shape corporate sustainability disclosure.

    Online Rack Placement in Large-Scale Data Centers: Online Sampling Optimization and Deployment Data centers have grown into major components of global supply chains. This paper develops, deploys, and assesses an optimization algorithm to improve how large-scale data centers place incoming server racks dynamically while balancing space, power, cooling, and reliability constraints. Poor placement decisions can leave valuable resources stranded, leading to high costs and reduced operating resilience. This paper formulates a large-scale online discrete optimization model and develops a new online sampling optimization (OSO) algorithm that anticipates future demand by repeatedly simulating future arrivals and reoptimizing decisions over time. Using postdeployment data, the paper shows that adoption of the tool reduced power stranding by one to three percentage points. At Microsoft’s scale, these improvements translate into substantial financial savings and meaningful reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, demonstrating the real-world impact of optimization in cloud infrastructure management.

    Abstract Research Summary Under what conditions do headquarters replace the general manager (GM) of an underperforming subsidiary? We argue that translating performance feedback into managerial intervention requires an inferential step the behavioral theory of the firm has only partially theorized. Headquarters must attribute shortfalls to managerial agency, and the diagnosticity of performance cues is shaped by organizational separateness within multinational enterprises (MNEs).   - DOI: 10.1002/job.70120
  - Authors: Matthias Felix Sinnemann; Matthias Michael Weiss; Julia Backmann
  - Affiliations: Universität Münster Münster Germany; Zeppelin Universität Friedrichshafen Friedrichshafen Germany
  - Published: 2026-08-12
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Organizational Behavior
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Teams hold the potential for creative performance, yet diverse perspectives may also lead to the premature rejection of unconventional ideas. Because such rejection can prevent teams from realizing their creative potential, understanding what motivational processes drive idea rejection during brainstorming is critical. Drawing on the Motivated Information Processing in Groups model (MIP‐G), we identify the reward structure offered to teams as an antecedent of idea rejection and examine how individual, team, and mixed rewards affect idea rejection and, ultimately, team creativity. We propose that individual rewards foster proself motivation, increasing team idea rejection; team rewards activate prosocial motivation, reducing it; and mixed rewards balance both motives. In an experimental lab study using behavioral video coding of 80 teams comprising 290 participants, we find that individual rewards significantly increase team idea rejection. Contrary to our expectations, team rewards also lead to elevated rejection, whereas mixed rewards reduce rejection compared to individual and team rewards. Exploratory post hoc analyses indicate that team idea rejection varies as a function of teams' level of information elaboration. Moreover, rewards are associated with differences in the novelty of rejected ideas, and novel ideas tend to be rejected in later stages of the ideation process. In contrast, we find no evidence that the manner of rejection, whether collaborative or competitive, systematically influences team creativity. These findings show that integrating proself and prosocial motives through mixed rewards may support creative collaboration in teams more effectively than emphasizing either motive alone.

  - Crossref journal: Journal of Organizational Behavior
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT The rise of performance excellence cultures and intensifying competitive pressure have made stress bragging a common workplace phenomenon. Although existing research has focused on stress bragging among peers, its effects when enacted by leaders remain poorly understood. Grounded in attribution theory and cognitive theories of repetitive thought, this study investigates how subordinates' attributions of leaders' stress bragging (performance improvement vs. impression management) shape their cognitive and behavioral responses. The results of Study 1 (a scenario‐based experiment with 294 employees) and Study 2 (a two‐wave survey of 332 employees) reveal that these attributions yield divergent outcomes. These findings underscore the pivotal role of attributional processes in explaining the double‐edged effects of leaders' stress bragging on both work‐related and interpersonal outcomes.
