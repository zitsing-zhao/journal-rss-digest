3. Reducing Risk Through Trade Credit: Theory and Evidence from Dual-Channel Suppliers
Jie Ning; Sammi Y. Tang; Jing Wu; Jiaqi Zhu
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.03142
Department of Operations, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio 44106; Department of Management, University of Miami, Coral Gables, Florida 33146; CUHK Business School, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong; Tilburg School of Economics and Management, Tilburg University, Tilburg 5037AB, Netherlands
Abstract:
Trade credit is traditionally viewed as increasing supplier risk, because delayed payments expose suppliers to buyer uncertainties. Using a stylized game-theoretic model, we show that this conventional wisdom can be overturned when a supplier is dual channel: operating a direct channel that sells to end customers in addition to an indirect channel that sells to an industry peer who serves the same market. Compared with zero trade credit provision where the supplier requires cash on delivery (COD), we observe empirically that high trade credit provision can lead to lower risk for the dual-channel supplier. We propose a possible mechanism for this risk mitigation effect—pooling: When the market overlap between the supplier and its peer buyer is sufficiently high, the random split of the overlapping market creates negative association between the supplier’s direct channel sales and the buyer’s trade credit payment. Trade credit does not always mitigate risk for dual-channel suppliers. If the supplier’s debt level is high, our theory predicts that trade credit exacerbates risk. We find empirical evidence consistent with our theoretical predictions using data on U.S. manufacturing firms that sell to their industry peers. The positive association between trade credit provision and bankruptcy risk is dampened or even reversed once a supplier begins selling to peer firms. This risk mitigation effect is stronger among suppliers with lower leverage and greater market overlap with their buyers. For highly financially constrained suppliers, trade credit provision is associated with an increase in bankruptcy risk after the supplier becomes dual channel. This paper was accepted by Jayashankar Swaminathan, operations management. Supplemental Material: The online appendices and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03142 .
4. Inferential Theory for Pricing Errors with Latent Factors and Firm Characteristics
Jungjun Choi; Ming Yuan
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.04971
Department of Computer Science and Statistics, University of Rhode Island, Kingston, Rhode Island 02881; Department of Statistics, Columbia University, New York, New York 10027
Abstract:
We study factor models that combine latent factors with firm characteristics and propose a new framework for modeling, estimating, and inferring pricing errors. Following Zhang (2024), our approach decomposes mispricing into two distinct components: inside alpha, explained by firm characteristics but orthogonal to factor exposures, and outside alpha, orthogonal to both factors and characteristics. Our model generalizes those developed recently such as Kelly et al. (2019) and Zhang (2024), resolving issues of orthogonality, basis dependence, and unit sensitivity. Methodologically, we develop estimators grounded in low-rank methods with explicit debiasing, providing closed-form solutions and a rigorous inferential theory that accommodates a growing number of characteristics and relaxes standard assumptions on sample dimensions. Empirically, using U.S. stock returns from 2000 to 2019, we document strong evidence of both inside and outside alphas, with the former showing industry-level comovements and the latter reflecting idiosyncratic shocks beyond firm fundamentals. Our framework thus unifies statistical and characteristic-based approaches to factor modeling, offering both theoretical advances and new insights into the structure of pricing errors. 5. Echoes of Discord: The Effects of Global Conflicts and Disputes on Entrepreneurship
Astrid Marinoni
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.04559
Scheller College of Business, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, Georgia 30308
Abstract:
Mixed-nationality founding teams bring together heterogeneous, hard-to-replicate bundles of human capital and country-specific ties through their cross-national networks, providing a distinctive foundation for building successful ventures. Yet these connections may be vulnerable to nonmarket shocks. This study examines whether geopolitical conflicts and disputes between the countries of origin of immigrant founders affect the formation of new ventures. Leveraging data on the founders of start-ups established in Great Britain and global information on conflicts and disputes, I find that such tensions reduce the likelihood that nationals of hostile countries cofound firms together. The evidence is more consistent with social mechanisms and, particularly, the erosion of interpersonal relationships than with diminished access to cross-country resources or the loss of entrepreneurial opportunities. Start-ups of both low and high quality are affected, including in sectors that depend heavily on skilled human capital, such as high-tech and business services. The negative effect is substantially attenuated for nationality pairs that are heavily colocated across local areas and for founders with prior social exposure to individuals from the opposing nationality, indicating that interpersonal ties can buffer against geopolitical shocks. Conflicts do not affect the exit of existing firms, and the decline in mixed-nationality entrepreneurship is not offset by alternative team configurations such as solo ventures or those formed with conationals, natives, or individuals from third-party countries. This paper was accepted by Alfonso Gambardella, business strategy. Funding: I gratefully acknowledge funding from the Institute for Leadership and Social Impact at Georgia Institute of Technology. 6. Correcting the Unobserved Textual Confounding Factors in Regression by BERT and Partially Linear Regression
Mengke Qiao; Ke-Wei Huang
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.04758
Culverhouse College of Business, The University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa, Alabama 35487; School of Computing, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119077
Abstract:
In the last decade, there has been a surge of text-mining applications in the information systems literature. In these applications, researchers typically construct independent variables from documents by various text-mining techniques. The most prominent example could be using online review sentiments to investigate online word-of-mouth issues. Typically, most documents contain information richer than the independent variable, and researchers may construct control variables from the same documents to alleviate endogeneity issues. However, researchers need to manually craft these variables, and this process may not guarantee that all the important confounding factors are extracted and included in the regression model with a correct functional form. If useful textual information is overlooked and a valid identification strategy is not available, the omitted variable bias may occur. To solve this challenge, we propose a novel model that utilizes text embedding based on the state-of-the-art neural network language model (i.e., bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)) to automatically create a new control variable vector for controlling latent confounding factors in the absence of a valid identification strategy. Moreover, our approach can be considered a semiparametric approach in that our model contains a linear term of the key independent variable and an unknown functional term of the textual control variables. Our approach extends the semiparametric framework, double machine learning, by introducing a novel Neyman-orthogonal loss function in an integrated deep neural network to minimize the estimation bias. Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of controlling textual control variables by text embedding and improving the estimation performance by the novel loss function. This paper was accepted by Anindya Ghose, information systems. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.04758 .
7. Throttling Equilibria in Auction Markets
Xi Chen; Christian Kroer; Rachitesh Kumar
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2023.0008
Columbia University, Computer Science Department, New York, New York, United States; Columbia University, IEOR, New York, New York, United States; Carnegie Mellon University, Tepper School of Business, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, United States
Abstract:
Throttling is a popular method of budget management for online ad auctions in which the platform modulates the participation probability of an advertiser in order to smoothly spend her budget across many auctions. In this work, we investigate the setting in which all of the advertisers simultaneously employ throttling to manage their budgets, and we do so for both first-price and second-price auctions. We analyze the structural and computational properties of the resulting equilibria. For first-price auctions, we show that a unique equilibrium always exists, is well-behaved and can be computed efficiently via tatonnement-style decentralized dynamics. In contrast, for second-price auctions, we prove that even though an equilibrium always exists, the problem of finding even an approximate equilibrium is PPAD-complete, there can be multiple equilibria, and it is NP-hard to find the revenue maximizing one. We also compare the equilibrium outcomes of throttling to those of multiplicative pacing, which is the other most popular and well-studied method of budget management. Finally, we characterize the Price of Anarchy of these equilibria for liquid welfare by showing that it is at most 2 for both first-price and second-price auctions, and demonstrating that our bound is tight.
8. Embedded in Two Worlds: How Incumbents’ Brokerage Affects Adaptation When Old and New Supply Networks Coexist
Jongyoun Baek; Andrew V. Shipilov; Nathan R. Furr
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.18580
INSEAD, EFE, Fontainebleau, Île-de-France, France; INSEAD, Strategy, Fontainebleau, France
Abstract:
The development of new technology architectures often opens up new markets within an existing industry. Incumbents face the decision of whether to enter the new technology market and how to do so. As incumbents attempt to enter the new market, they can become embedded in two buyer-supplier networks, one for the old and the other for new technologies. We argue that brokers have an advantage in this process because they have developed capabilities to integrate suppliers of new technology components or develop unique component combinations for the new technology products. In a study of the U.S. automotive industry between 2012 and 2022, we find that brokerage in the old technology network is positively associated with successful entry into the new technology (i.e., number and performance of new technology models). Surprisingly, brokerage in the old technology network had a stronger positive impact than brokerage in the new technology network on incumbents’ entry into the new technology market for electric vehicles.
9. Conspiracy theory communication framing and support for antidemocratic behavior
Frances Cayton; Courtney Blackington
American Journal of Political Science
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1111/ajps.70092
Center on Democracy, Development, and the Rule of Law Stanford University Stanford California USA; Department of Political Science University of Pittsburgh Pittsburgh Pennsylvania USA
Abstract:
Abstract Many politicians use conspiracy theories (CTs) to win electoral support and justify antidemocratic policies. How do individuals evaluate this strategy? We theorize and show that variation in CT communication frames affects individuals’ willingness to endorse antidemocratic policies and politicians. Politicians who explicitly appeal to a CT earn greater support for antidemocratic behavior than those using implicit appeals. However, this effect primarily occurs for novel CTs lacking partisan cues. Over time, as CTs become more politicized, they lose their mobilizational capacity and can reduce support for CT‐wielding politicians—even among co‐partisans. Three preregistered survey experiments surrounding the 2023 parliamentary and 2025 presidential elections in Poland, and survey data from 21 countries, provide empirical support for our theory. Ultimately, we show that CTs alone are not always enough to justify attacks on democratic norms and institutions to the public.
10. Do Politicians’ Errors in Understanding Public Opinion Persist When They Encounter Individual Constituents?
JACK LUCAS; LIOR SHEFFER; NOAM TITELMAN
American Political Science Review
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-27 | DOI 10.1017/s0003055426102238
University of Calgary; Tel Aviv University
Abstract:
When thinking about public opinion, politicians commonly exhibit three general perceptual errors: a tendency to overestimate their constituents’ conservatism ( conservative bias ), a tendency to hold more accurate knowledge of privileged constituents than constituents in general ( privilege bias ), and a tendency to overestimate how much their constituents share their views ( projection bias ). Studies documenting these patterns invariably rely on assessing politicians’ perceptions of aggregated public opinion. In practice, however, politicians heavily prioritize and rely on the opinions of individual constituents, raising concerns about the external validity of these findings. Thus, in this article, we explore whether politicians’ common perceptual errors persist when they think about specific individual constituents, rather than aggregate public opinion. We assign more than 1,400 Canadian politicians to predict the ideology and issue positions of more than 4,000 randomly generated citizen profiles that vary by a host of salient sociodemographic and political factors. We then compare their evaluations to estimates of these citizens’ real-life positions drawn from a general population survey. We find that politicians do not exhibit conservative bias or privilege bias when making inferences about individual constituents. However, even when thinking about individual constituents, projection bias persists, and the perception errors it creates are amplified when politicians are more similar to their constituents. We discuss the implications of these findings for theories of political representation.
11. Blood is Still Thicker Than Water: A Response to Lin’s Reappraisal and Reflections on Historical Political Economy
YUHUA WANG
American Political Science Review
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-27 | DOI 10.1017/s0003055426102135
Harvard University
Abstract:
A recent reappraisal by Lin expands Wang’s (2022, American Political Science Review 116, 896–910) dataset and questions the robustness of its main finding. In this response, I clarify broader challenges of measurement, case selection, and inference in historical political economy. I show that my original finding—that kinship-network concentration is negatively associated with support for state-building reforms—remains robust across multiple coding strategies. I also acknowledge that two high-leverage cases, the Lü brothers, matter for inference; rather than treating them as removable anomalies, I argue that their extreme values are theoretically meaningful features of kinship-network structure. Finally, I engage Lin’s regional interpretation and show that regional status competition does not account for the observed relationship once measurement and estimation issues are addressed. More broadly, this exchange illustrates how transparent measurement and sustained dialogue between quantitative and qualitative evidence can generate cumulative knowledge from fragmentary historical archives.
12. Hiring talent in technologically advanced countries: The paradox of springboarding for firms from weak intellectual property rights ( <scp>IPR</scp> ) regimes
Victor Cui; Qian Xiang; Jiatao Li; Jianqi Zhang
Strategic Management Journal
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1002/smj.70140
Conrad School of Entrepreneurship and Business University of Waterloo Waterloo Ontario Canada; School of Finance and Economics Guangdong Polytechnic Normal University Guangzhou China; School of Business and Management Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Clear Water Bay Hong Kong; Lingnan College Sun Yat‐sen University Guangzhou China
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Anecdotes suggest that multinational enterprises (MNEs) from weak intellectual property rights (IPR) regimes often leapfrog to technological leadership by recruiting talent in advanced host countries. Yet whether—and how—such a strategy works remains unclear. Using data on 486 MNEs from 29 countries operating in the United States and interviews with skilled inventors, we uncover a paradox in the springboard‐via‐hiring logic: externally, weak home‐country IPR reputation deters skilled inventors from joining, limiting recruitment; internally, even when talent is hired, home‐country‐imprinted IPR routines constrain post‐hire learning, yielding incremental improvements. These effects are mitigated by firm‐specific exposure to host‐country patent infringement litigation. Our research suggests that springboard‐via‐hiring is institution‐based, contingent on both home‐ and host‐country IPR regimes. These findings offer implications for technology policy and MNEs' strategy amid geopolitical tension. Managerial Summary Can multinational enterprises (MNEs) from weak intellectual property rights (IPR) regimes leapfrog by hiring technical talent in advanced economies? Evidence on 486 MNEs from 29 countries operating in the US high‐technology sector suggests: weaker home‐country IPR reputations make it harder to attract skilled inventors; even after hiring, home‐imprinted IPR routines can constrain recruits' knowledge transfer, often steering outcomes toward incremental improvements. These home‐country constraints are, however, mitigated by firm‐specific exposure to US patent infringement litigation. The springboard‐via‐hiring strategy is thus institution‐based, contingent on both home‐ and host‐country IPR regimes. We accordingly offer advice on technology policy and global R&D strategies for host‐country governments and MNEs amid geopolitical tension.

14. A Signal-Extraction Theory of Inflation Pass-Through
Engin Kara
Economic Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1093/ej/ueag149
Cardiff Business School, University of Cardiff , Cardiff, CF10 3EU ,
Abstract:
Abstract I develop a theory of state-dependent signal extraction in price setting. Firms infer how much sectoral price movements reflect persistent aggregate or transitory sector-specific disturbances. When inflation is low relative to sectoral variation, sticky-price firms discount competitor movements because following transitory movements risks leaving them mispriced. As inflation rises relative to sectoral noise, firms increasingly attribute these movements to aggregate shocks and sticky firms respond more strongly. UK micro-price data support these predictions: below approximately 1.9% sectoral inflation, sticky firms respond less to competitors than flexible firms; above it, the gap closes. This convergence generates state-dependent aggregate inflation dynamics.

16. Domestic Tariffs and Consumer Welfare in Developing Countries: Evidence From India
Jerónimo Callejas; Chirantan Chatterjee; Debi Prasad Mohapatra
RAND Journal of Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1111/1756-2171.70076
University of Massachusetts Amherst Massachusetts USA; Department of Economics University of Sussex Business School Brighton UK
Abstract:
ABSTRACT This article examines the welfare consequences of industrial policies aimed at boosting domestic manufacturing in developing countries, focusing on the Indian government's tariff policy for mobile handset production. We develop a structural model of India's mobile handset industry, where firms endogenously decide supply chain alternatives, product sets, and prices. Our counterfactual simulations indicate that the status quo import tariff on ready‐to‐use handsets significantly increases local assembly operations. However, a lower tariff of around 8% would maintain similar assembly levels and significantly enhance consumer surplus. An additional input import tariff on handset components discourages firms from relocating operations to India, potentially undermining the policy's goal.
17. Territory‐as‐pedagogy: Learning with the Territorial Imaginaries of Rural and Indigenous Students in Management Education
Ana Laura Dominguez Paredes; Rajiv Maher; Clara Toxqui Teutle
British Journal of Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1111/1467-8551.70129
Facultad de Responsabilidad Social Universidad Anáhuac México Norte Huixquilucan Mexico; Egade Business School Tecnologico de Monterrey Ciudad de México 01389 Mexico; Instituto de Ciencias Sociales y Humanidades‐BUAP Heroica Puebla de Zaragoza 72000 Mexico
Abstract:
Abstract Business schools are being asked to prepare managers for a climate‐altered world, and that debate has centered largely on what curricula contain, paying less attention to territory as an epistemic and pedagogical category, or to the students whose territorial knowledge sits outside dominant models. Drawing on an interpretive study with rural and Indigenous scholarship students at a private Mexican university, in which participant‐produced drawings were read alongside interviews and social‐cartography workshops, we ask how the territorial imaginaries of students from rural and Indigenous communities might inform more epistemically just curricula in business schools. We read the drawings as expressing five recurring territorial imaginaries that cohere into four themes and compose a relational territorial imaginary. Consequently, we advance the concept of territory‐as‐pedagogy, an account of territory as a co‐teacher that supplies the terms of economic and ethical reasoning rather than serving as a backdrop. We contend that territory‐as‐pedagogy makes ecological limits and obligation visible because students reason from relations and ties they hold to land, water, crops and kin, which a module added to a curriculum would struggle to reproduce. We propose it as a context‐specific resource for educators, stating a limit of place‐based pedagogy and contributing to decolonial and Indigenous management scholarship.

20. Can Workplace Creativity Increase Positive Affect Through Absorption? The Moderating Roles of Negative Affect and Competitive Climate
March L. To; Gillian Yeo; Cynthia D. Fisher
Journal of Organizational Behavior
ABS 4 | 2026-11-27 | DOI 10.1002/job.70138
School of Management, College of Business and Law RMIT University Melbourne Australia; University of Western Australia Perth Australia; Bond University Gold Coast Australia
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Workplace creativity is often studied as an outcome influenced by employees' affective states. There is growing interest in the potential for creative activity to improve positive affect, but there is insufficient knowledge about when or why such activity may change how employees feel at work. We draw on affect regulation theory to consider the mechanisms and boundary conditions involved in the relationship between creative process engagement (CPE) and changes in positive affect. We suggest that engaging in creative processes at work has the potential to increase positive affect through increased task absorption. However, our framework highlights that these effects of CPE are contingent on two factors: the accompanying negative affect and the surrounding competitive climate for creativity. We develop progressive support for our predictions in a laboratory experiment followed by a field experience sampling study. The laboratory study found that CPE increased positive affect through greater task absorption when negative affect was high. The field study tested and supported our full model, with competitive climate for creativity impacting the moderated‐mediated relationship, such that it weakened the CPE × negative affect interactive effect on increasing positive affect via task absorption.
21. Growing, but Not Necessarily Showing: Pregnancy in Remote Versus In‐Person Contexts
Aashna M. Waiwood; Tammy D. Allen; Alyssa M. Lezcano; Joseph Regina
Journal of Organizational Behavior
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1002/job.70139
U.S. Department of Defense Washington DC USA; University of South Florida Tampa Florida USA; Rutgers University Camden New Jersey USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Pregnancy is a stigmatized state in the workplace, and research has increasingly documented the unique experience of being pregnant at work, including difficulties with pregnancy disclosure. Theory and research seemingly take as a given that women's pregnancies are visible or that they become visible. Moreover, visibility appears crucial to explaining pregnancy‐related experiences, behaviors, and treatment. However, working remotely alters the presumed inevitability of visibility. Based on qualitative and quantitative data, we explore how working remotely corresponds with women's disclosure and postdisclosure pregnancy‐related perceptions, decisions, and experiences. Findings demonstrate that time working remotely is associated with greater perceptions of disclosure control, disclosure to a narrower network, delayed disclosure timing, and reduced need to disclose for transparency purposes.
