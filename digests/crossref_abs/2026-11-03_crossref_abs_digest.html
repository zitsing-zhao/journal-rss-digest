2. Democratizing Optimization with Generative AI
David Simchi-Levi; Tinglong Dai; Ishai Menache; Michelle Xiao Wu
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2025.2412
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States; Johns Hopkins University Carey Business School, Baltimore, Maryland, United States; Microsoft Research, Redmond, Washington, United States; Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana, United States
Abstract:
Recent breakthroughs in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) have captured the public imagination, whereas mathematical optimization remains little recognized beyond specialists. In this article, we argue that GenAI can bridge the persistent gap between optimization's technical capabilities and its limited real-world uptake. We present the 4I framework (Insight, Interpretability, Interactivity, Improvisation) as a set of design principles for combining GenAI with mathematical optimization. Insight establishes a trusted, up-to-date view of current operations; Interpretability explains model logic and trade-offs; Interactivity enables conversational what-if analysis; Improvisation supports model updates and event-driven re-optimization. By making optimization tools more intuitive, explainable, interactive, and adaptable, we envision a future in which frontline decision-makers can base their choices on rigorous, iterative analysis. We discuss how GenAI complements, rather than replaces, optimization: GenAI lowers barriers to modeling and interpretation; mathematical optimization reliably enforces the business goals, rules, and hard constraints encoded in the model. We also address emerging concerns, from hallucinations to the risk of over-reliance, and outline research directions for integrating GenAI and optimization responsibly. The GenAI boom gives the optimization community an opportunity to expand its impact by making optimization more accessible and trustworthy to a wider audience.
3. Women and the double standard of ambition
Geertje J. Bol
American Journal of Political Science
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/ajps.70091
History Department University of Fribourg Fribourg Switzerland
Abstract:
Abstract Few human qualities are as morally unstable as ambition: We seem unable to decide whether to admire or condemn the desire for self‐advancement. This instability guarantees double standards—and none has been more enduring than that of women's ambition. This article traces the emergence of this double standard in early modern England, when ambition came to be widely accepted as natural and even virtuous in men, yet remained unnatural and vicious in women. It then turns to four seventeenth‐century feminists—Rachel Speght, Margaret Cavendish, Mary Astell, and Judith Drake—who challenged this emerging double standard. They reimagined ambition as a feminist tool, using it to argue for women's equality and even superiority. Unearthing this neglected strand of ambitious feminism not only contributes to the history of political thought but also points the way to a reconciliation between feminism and ambition that avoids the pitfalls identified by feminist theorists today.
4. ASYMPTOTIC PROPERTIES OF APPROXIMATE MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD ESTIMATOR IN MARKOV-SWITCHING STATE-SPACE MODELS
Chaojun Li
Econometric Theory
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1017/s0266466626100723
East China Normal University
Abstract:
For Markov-switching state-space models, it is practically infeasible to compute the likelihood function through the Kalman filter because of the path dependence on regimes. A widely applied approximation algorithm uses a collapsing step to average out the most dated regime and truncates the regime path. This algorithm’s performance, however, has not been examined analytically. This study fills the gap. We characterize the parameters that affect the approximation error, derive the upper bounds of the errors in likelihood computation and latent variable inference, and investigate the asymptotic properties of the approximate maximum likelihood estimator. We also provide a practical procedure for measuring approximation errors and selecting the number of regime periods to keep in the truncated path. All the analysis is based on our new collapsing step, which removes a nonlinear adjustment term in the existing method to facilitate theoretical derivations.
5. Life‐Cycle Wage Growth and Firm Productivity in Developed and Developing Economies
Kerstin Holzheu; Juan Munoz‐Morales
International Economic Review
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/iere.70129
Sciences Po (CNRS) &amp; IZA &amp; CESifo; Univ. Lille, CNRS, IESEG School of Management, UMR 9221 ‐ LEM ‐ Lille Économie Management Lille F‐59000 France
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Life‐cycle wage growth varies substantially across countries. We study the firm productivity distribution's role in a random‐search model with learning and labor‐market frictions, estimated for Brazil, Colombia, and the United States. Brazil has a long left tail of low‐paying firms, and Colombia has a highly compressed firm ladder. Replacing each country's firm distribution with that of the United States closes most of the cross‐country gap in wage growth, raising Brazil's relative wage growth from 73 to 103 percent and Colombia's from 52 to 115 percent. The results are robust to alternative distributional assumptions and additional sensitivity checks.

9. Does the Salience of Climate-Related Risk Affect Asset Prices?
Cloé Garnache
Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1017/s0022109026103494
Oslo Metropolitan University Housing Lab and Center for Energy Policy and Economics (CEPE), ETH Zürich
Abstract:
Abstract Using regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences designs to compare prices of California homes across a new wildfire risk zone boundary, I identify null effects in the immediate vicinity of the new risk zone boundary. In contrast, prices of homes newly assigned to a riskier zone drop by 5.7% further away from the new risk zone boundary. These findings suggest that households do not respond to the risk salience triggered by the new risk zone assignment. Instead, they update their risk beliefs in response to rezoning in a way that is consistent with the actual wildfire risk.
10. Does Climate Change Affect Firm Sales? Identifying Supply Effects
Cláudia Custódio; Miguel A. Ferreira; Emilia Garcia-Appendini; Adrian Lam
Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1017/s002210902610341x
Imperial College Business School; Nova School of Business and Economics; University of St. Gallen; University of Pittsburgh
Abstract:
Abstract We estimate the supply-side effects of climate change on firm sales by exploiting variation in local average temperature across suppliers of the same client. A 1°C increase in temperature leads to a 2.5% decline in annual sales. This effect is nonlinear, with stronger effects among suppliers in regions with the largest temperature increases and in cooler regions. Manufacturing and heat-sensitive industries are more affected, consistent with a reduction in labor productivity and labor supply when local temperatures rise. Nondiversified and financially constrained firms are also more affected, supporting the importance of operational and financial flexibility in adapting to climate change.
11. Accounting Comparability and Common Owners
Maimuna Akter; Justin Chircop; Douglas Cumming; Monika Tarsalewska
British Journal of Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/1467-8551.70124
School of Business Administration Gonzaga University 502 E Boone Ave Spokane WA 99258 USA; Lancaster University Management School Lancaster University Lancaster LA1 4YX UK; Professor of Finance and Steven Shulman ’62 Endowed Chair of Digital Innovation, Stevens Institute of Technology School of Business 1 Castle Point Terrace Hoboken NJ 07030 USA; University of Exeter Business School Streatham Court, Streatham Campus Rennes Drive Exeter EX4 4PU UK; Kozminski University 57/59 Jagiellonska Street Warsaw 03–301 Poland
Abstract:
Abstract We examine the relationship between accounting comparability and common ownership. Accounting comparability can reduce information asymmetry between firms and investors by lowering information acquisition and processing costs. At the same time, greater comparability can reduce the private information advantages that sophisticated investors, such as common owners, exploit. Which of these opposing forces dominates is ultimately an empirical question. Using a sample of US firms for 1999–2021, we find that accounting comparability is positively associated with common ownership, particularly non‐transient common ownership. Further analyses indicate that this relation is driven by a reduction in information asymmetry and is significantly stronger among firms with weaker information environments.
12. From Slack to Staff: Managers' Allocation of Slack Resources to Hiring
Veroniek Collewaert; Ine Paeleman; Tom Vanacker; Frederik Anseel; Dries Bourgois
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70185
Vlerick Business School; KU Leuven; University of Antwerp; Ghent University; University of Exeter; Hong Kong University of Science and Technology
Abstract:
Abstract Prior research views cash holdings, or high‐discretionary slack, as a buffer against shocks or a catalyst for strategic change. Drawing on stakeholder salience theory, we argue that cash can also reshape managers' interpretation and prioritization of employee hiring‐related claims, thereby increasing hiring, and that this effect is more pronounced when structural influences heighten the salience of those claims. Study 1, a large‐scale field study of Belgian firms, finds a positive but diminishing relationship between cash and hiring. This relationship strengthens under conditions of greater firm dependence on specific employee groups or following an exogenous legal reform, both of which are hypothesized to make the salience of specific employee groups more apparent. In Study 2, a vignette experiment offers causal support for the hypothesized mechanism, with employee salience cues strengthening how managers translate cash into hiring allocations. Combined, our findings contribute to research on slack resources and stakeholder salience by showing that managers use high‐discretionary cash not only for buffering or strategic change but also to respond to salient stakeholders.

15. Buyer green innovation volatility and the supplier bullwhip effect: a signalling theory perspective
Yan Hou; Shuili Yang; Lixu Li; Lujie Chen; Chenho Chao
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-05-2026-0724
Xi’an University of Technology School of Economics and Management, , ,; Xi’an Jiaotong Liverpool University International Business School Suzhou, , ,; Emlyon Business School, Lyon, France
Abstract:
Purpose Buyers' green innovation does not remain confined within the firm; its volatility may ripple upstream and reshape suppliers' operations. Drawing on signalling theory, we examine how buyers' green innovation volatility (GIV) affects the supplier bullwhip effect (SBE), and how buyers' green supplier management (GSM) and inventory utilisation efficiency (IUE) moderate this effect. Design/methodology/approach We construct a dyadic dataset covering 436 Chinese buyer firms and 871 global suppliers from 2012 to 2022, and employ fixed-effects models to test our hypotheses. Findings There is a U-shaped GIV–SBE relationship: compared with both low and high levels of GIV, moderate GIV leads suppliers to adjust production more cautiously in response to unstable demand, resulting in lower SBE. When buyers' green innovation is on an overall downward trend, GSM helps suppliers interpret demand and operational changes more accurately, curbing GIV-induced increases in SBE. Higher IUE enables suppliers to detect GIV-related requirement changes more promptly, prompting stronger production responses and widening the SBE gap between moderate and low or high GIV. Originality/value By introducing GIV, our study identifies a novel antecedent of SBE and provides insights into how buyers can help suppliers accurately interpret GIV and calibrate their operational responses.
16. EXPRESS: Partial or Full Doses First? Vaccine Allocation Under Limited Supply
Ming Hu; Chaoyu Zhang; Yun Zhou
Production and Operations Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1177/10591478261507274
Abstract:
Vaccines have proven highly effective in curbing disease spread in the face of sudden large-scale infectious diseases. However, the challenge of limited vaccine supplies during initial mass production or in certain regions such as Africa poses a significant hurdle. To address this issue, one potential solution is the implementation of a fractional dosing approach, whereby individuals receive partial doses of the vaccine to achieve partial immunity. In this paper, we investigate the optimal vaccine allocation policy in scenarios where the vaccine supply is scarce. By exploring the trade-offs between administering fractional and full dosages, we formulate the problem as an optimal control problem under disease transmission dynamics to minimize the total number of infections over a planning horizon. Specifically, we extend the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered model by introducing two new compartments for partially and fully vaccinated individuals. Under mild conditions, we demonstrate that an attainable optimal vaccine allocation policy exhibits a bang-bang structure, suggesting that either the Partial Doses First (PDF) or the Full Doses First (FDF) allocation policy achieves optimality at any time. Furthermore, we compare the committed PDF and FDF policies for the entire planning horizon and identify a critical threshold on the fractional-dose vaccine efficacy, such that when the efficacy is above (resp., below) this threshold, choosing the committed PDF (resp., FDF) policy is better. Using COVID-19 vaccination data, we calculate thresholds for different countries in January 2021 to recommend to governments whether the priority should be given to allocating the first dose or to prioritizing the allocation of both first and second doses to the population. We also illustrate the versatility of our proposed scarce capacity allocation model in addressing a broad spectrum of resource allocation problems under diffusion dynamics, for which partial resource allocation can still yield some, though not full, benefits and the optimal allocation decision involves balancing the coverage and protection level.
17. EXPRESS: Optimizing Size-Based Bundle Promotions: Balancing Customer Appeal and Product Costs
Ruijiu Mao; Shuai Jia; Chung-Piaw Teo
Production and Operations Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1177/10591478261507272
Abstract:
Size-based bundle promotions (“ p -for- s ”) are widely used in retail to increase basket size, but designing them is challenging when product costs and customer preferences are heterogeneous. Customers favor high-value products; however, including such items increases the bundle’s cost and may require a higher price to sustain profitability. Existing models typically assume identical costs or uniform preferences and therefore largely overlook the interaction between cost heterogeneity and customer preferences. To examine this interaction, we begin with the “ p -for-1” case under classical MNL and mixed MNL models and provide clear structural insights: (i) cost heterogeneity makes assortment selection nontrivial, motivating joint optimization of assortment and pricing; (ii) preference heterogeneity makes optimization under mixed MNL models computationally difficult; and (iii) extending MNL to “ p -for- s ” settings requires approximations and becomes intractable as s grows. Building on these insights, we develop an optimization framework for general “ p -for- s ” promotions with multiple concurrent offers. Our approach uses sample average approximation (SAA) to approximate the valuation distribution with a finite sample of customer valuations and reformulates the resulting problem as a discrete bilevel program that captures surplus-maximizing customer behavior. To improve computational tractability, we combine the SAA-based mixed-integer reformulation with low-rank completion, customer clustering, and consideration-set construction. Finally, a large-scale case study using data from a major e-commerce retailer shows that our framework yields substantially higher model-implied profit than the cost-threshold heuristic and outperforms other bundle-promotion benchmarks.
18. EXPRESS: Customer-to-Manufacturer: Should a Platform Cooperate with More Manufacturers?
Tonghuan Shi; Lin Tian; Zhen Xu
Production and Operations Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1177/10591478261507271
Abstract:
The traditional top-down relationship between manufacturers and consumers has led to persistently high failure rates in new product development. In response, the emerging customer-to-manufacturer (C2M) model leverages online platforms as intermediaries to directly transmit consumer feedback to manufacturers, thereby improving decision-making in product innovation and increasing the likelihood of product success. This paper develops an analytical model to study the strategic adoption of the C2M model in an online marketplace with one platform and two competing manufacturers. We focus on the role of platform information and examine how its trade-offs shape market outcomes. Our analysis shows that the impact of platform information on manufacturers' expected profits is non-monotonic. Moreover, manufacturers' cooperation decisions in C2M depend on the information price and are moderated by the amount of information. For the platform, unexpectedly, involving more manufacturers in C2M cooperation is not always optimal, especially when information is sufficient. We further show that the C2M model benefits the platform and consumers and improves social welfare, while potentially reducing manufacturers' profits. Overall, our results provide managerial and regulatory implications for platform-based C2M practices.

Abstract:
Abstract Proximity is traditionally viewed as crucial for overcoming the search and transfer costs associated with imperfectly codified knowledge. But does proximity still matter when formal standard publication jointly enhances the codification, certainty, and accessibility of technical knowledge? Evidence from the U.S. Information and Communication Technology industry shows that technological proximity drives substantial spillover gains, largely irrespective of location. 25. The Aftermath of Almost: Communicating Risk Amid Community Narratives of Near Misses
Elizabeth C. Ray; Anica Schramm; Patrick F. Merle
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70388
School of Communication Florida State University Tallahassee Florida USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Unpredictable weather means that serious threats do not always unfold as forecast. Hurricane near misses create an especially challenging communication context: The absence of harm may invite community explanations that make the event feel less threatening, even as future risk persists. This study examines how community narratives and post‐event risk messages shape risk perception, collective efficacy, preparedness intentions, message sharing, and credibility after hurricane near misses. In an online experiment, Florida residents ( N = 405) viewed social media posts about a hurricane that shifted away from a coastal community. The posts varied by explanation type, comparing a symbolic community narrative with a factual account, and by response source, comparing emergency management, peer, and control messages. Results show limits and leverage points for post‐event risk communication: In the primary analysis, neither community narratives nor risk messages significantly shifted perceived risk; however, supplementary analyses accounting for regional residence revealed a small reduction in perceived risk following narrative exposure. Risk messages increased sharing and collective efficacy, and organizational sources were more credible than peer sources. These findings suggest that post‐event risk communication after near misses may not readily recalibrate individual risk perceptions but can shape how risk information circulates and how communities perceive their capacity to respond. The study advances understanding of near misses as ambiguous risk events in which communication matters even when harm does not occur.
26. Risk‐Oriented Differentiated Governance for Ambiguous Information
Peikun Ni; Jianming Zhu; Hongzhou Zhang
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70378
People's Public Security University of China Beijing China; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences Beijing China
Abstract:
ABSTRACT The rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence has led to an exponential increase in false information, especially ambiguous information. Hampered by resource constraints, conventional governance approaches fall short of enabling efficient and precise risk management. In response, this paper innovatively proposes a risk‐oriented tiered governance framework tailored to ambiguous information. Specifically, we quantify epistemic uncertainty via multi‐level semantic features, combine it with network propagation features to determine latent influence scores, and construct a risk function capturing their synergy. Critical and acceptable boundaries are introduced to categorize ambiguous information into discrete risk levels, tier‐specific governance strategies are devised, and a propagation throttling (limiting each user's successful forwards per ambiguous information) strategy is innovatively proposed for medium‐risk information. Finally, we simulate differentiated governance strategies across risk levels of ambiguous information, systematically assess governance effectiveness, and offer a comparative discussion grounded in fairness and ethics. This study offers an operational analytical framework for refined risk governance of ambiguous information, contributes methodological innovations in risk quantification modeling and tiered governance strategies, and provides theoretical support and practical guidance for risk management authorities in designing differentiated measures.

Abstract Workplace authority is a key individual and organizational resource to which women typically have more limited access than men. Yet, less is known about how parenthood shapes this gender disparity and how the relationship varies across employment regimes. Drawing on nationally representative, longitudinal data from the China Family Panel Studies (2010–2022), I examine within-person changes in women’s and men’s access to supervisory authority following parenthood, with particular attention to variation across the state and private sectors. Abstract:
Abstract This study maps the division of music in China and examines the mechanisms underlying its formation. Methodologically, it introduces multidimensional item response theory (MIRT), a latent-trait approach rarely used in cultural sociology, to identify the social divisions of taste before examining their social stratification through regression analysis. The findings reveal six taste profiles: high-status traditional music; pop music stratified into middlebrow Hong Kong and Taiwan classics, unstratified mainstream Chinese pop, and low-status local music; and foreign music divided into high-status selective forms and unstratified trendy foreign pop. These divisions do not cleanly align with genre categories, a highbrow–lowbrow hierarchy, or omnivore–univore patterns in established accounts. Instead, they reveal dual forms of high-status taste—state-consecrated heritage and cosmopolitan selective foreign pop—reflecting a multidimensional structure organized around region of origin, historical prestige, cohort, and popularity. More broadly, this study reveals how cultural stratification in China extends beyond Bourdieusian models centered on domestic field competition to encompass global flows, state-directed legitimation, and historical local trajectories. These findings extend theories of cultural distinction beyond the assumptions derived from dominant Western liberal-market societies and offer a framework for understanding how cultural hierarchies are constituted across wider national contexts.

Abstract:
Abstract We present a comparative case study of software development teams in 14 firms, based on 50 semi-structured interviews with 39 people, plus non-participant observation. Our theory proposes that an inherent contradiction of labour management—conflicting pressures to empower workers (to enhance capabilities) and to control workers (to ensure labour discipline)—becomes refracted in distinct ways through the service triangle between customers, managers, and workers.
