1. Environmental, Social, and Governance Ratings of Environmental, Social, and Governance Index Providers
Sonakshi Agrawal; Lisa Yao Liu; Shivaram Rajgopal; Suhas A. Sridharan; Yifan Yan; Teri Lombardi Yohn
Accounting Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.2308/tar-2023-0522
Columbia University; Emory University
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We explore the role of ESG raters’ business models in the production of their ratings, noting that increasingly ESG raters not only produce ESG ratings but also construct and sell ESG indices. We examine whether index licensing incentives are associated with how ESG ratings relate to stock returns. By comparing ratings across raters for the same firm, we control for fundamental ESG performance. We find that raters with strong index licensing incentives assign higher ESG ratings to firms with better stock performance compared to raters with weaker licensing incentives, and that this association is concentrated among index-eligible firms. We further show that index construction is significantly associated with stock returns, and that the high-incentive rater adjusts its ratings following index composition changes, whereas the low-incentive rater does not. Our findings highlight the need for greater transparency in the incentives of ESG rating producers. JEL Classifications: G24; M14; M40; M41; M48; Q56.
2. Rational Information Acquisition Screens
Paul E. Fischer; Mirko S. Heinle; Jung Min Kim; Christina Zhu
Accounting Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.2308/tar-2024-0799
University of Pennsylvania; Northwestern University
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We develop and empirically test a model of information acquisition in capital markets. In the model, investors are uncertain about the returns to acquiring private information before they acquire it. This results in a novel reason for why past price movements affect future capital market outcomes: investors use public information, including past prices, as a screen to estimate the returns to acquiring private information and efficiently allocate their limited information-processing capacity across firms. The model predicts that larger unexplained price movements lead to more private information acquisition, higher future price volatility, and higher future expected trading volume. Using fine-grained data measuring information acquisition on EDGAR and Bloomberg, we provide empirical evidence in support of the model’s predictions. Data Availability: All data are available from the sources cited in the text. JEL Classifications: D83; G12; G14; M41.

4. Generative AI And Creative Goods: Market Expansion, Crowd-Out, and Copyright
Samuel G Goldberg; H Tai Lam
Quarterly Journal of Economics
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1093/qje/qjag058
Stanford Graduate School of Business, Stanford University; Anderson School of Management, University of California , Los Angeles
Abstract:
Abstract We study how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) affects creative goods markets using data from a large stock images marketplace. In December 2022, the platform announced it would allow artists to sell GenAI-produced images, subject to two conditions: all GenAI images must be labeled, and GenAI would be prohibited in certain markets. We exploit this policy variation using a difference-in-differences design. We estimate a 136% increase in image production and 47% increase in active artists, accompanied by a 15% decline in non-GenAI production and 29% decline in non-GenAI active artists. On the demand side, total sales increase by 82%, but non-GenAI sales fall by 28% and non-GenAI sales rates decline by 3 percentage points. We use image embeddings to develop novel measures of image quality, characteristics, and market composition.We find that quality (measured as predicted sales probability) increases by 9 percentage points overall and 2 percentage points for non-GenAI content, suggesting low-quality non-GenAI artists exit. Our product characteristic measures indicate that GenAI production is heavily concentrated in characteristic space, potentially limiting the value of these images to the market. Non-GenAI production responds to GenAI by differentiating into niches. We discuss implications for copyright policy and the longer-run sustainability of original creative work.

8. Widely Used Measures of Overconfidence Are Confounded with Ability
Stephen A. Spiller
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.05184
Anderson School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles, California 90095
Abstract:
Measures of overconfidence are used both to assess biases in people’s beliefs regarding their own abilities and to identify correlates of those biases. Proposed correlates of overconfidence include narcissism, social status, (low) task anxiety, savings, planning, and many more. Research on these correlates typically explicitly or implicitly claims that the associations with overconfidence are not attributable to ability (i.e., latent inputs enabling successful task performance, including inputs that vary across people, states, or contexts). I argue that several typical approaches used to measure overconfidence are not sufficient to support such claims. This argument follows from two underappreciated properties of these measures of overconfidence. First, performance is an imperfect measure of ability: accounting for performance does not sufficiently account for ability. Second, self-evaluations of performance should reflect ability in addition to performance: because performance is ambiguous, people should incorporate prior beliefs about their ability. These properties imply that multiple commonly used measures of overconfidence are confounded with ability. I support these analytical results by reexamining previously published findings. In the first analysis, I find that overconfidence predicts subsequent performance, consistent with measures of overconfidence serving as a signal of ability. In the second set of analyses, I find that some purported associations between overconfidence and other constructs could be attributed to ability instead. I close with recommendations to recognize and reduce the potential for mistaken inferences. When research indicates that differences in overconfidence are associated with other behaviors, beliefs, or evaluations, researchers should account for the possibility that differences in ability could provide a sufficient explanation. This paper was accepted by Jack Soll, behavioral economics and decision analysis. Funding: This work was supported by the UCLA Anderson Morrison Center for Marketing and Data Analytics. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05184 and https://researchbox.org/1597 .
9. Screening, Investment Incentives, and the Welfare Trade-Offs of Privacy
Anastasios Dosis; Casey Rothschild
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01507
ESSEC Business School, 95021 Cergy, France; and THEMA, 95021 Cergy, France; Wellesley College, Wellesley, Massachusetts 02481
Abstract:
This paper studies how information disclosure affects investment incentives and contracting efficiency. We analyze a class of principal-agent models in which information helps the principal screen the agent but also facilitates surplus appropriation, thereby weakening the agent’s incentive to invest before contracting. Efficient privacy regimes balance these two forces. Along the frontier of efficient information policies, more privacy is associated with stronger investment incentives and higher agent payoff. We characterize conditions under which full privacy, full disclosure, or intermediate privacy is efficient. The results provide guidance for intellectual property and innovation policy by identifying when additional privacy fosters or hinders economic efficiency and innovation. 10. Gatekeepers and Kingmakers: Do Superplatforms Distort Digital Competition?
Umit G. Gurun; Zheng Liu; Steven Chong Xiao
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.03354
Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, Texas 75080; Metropolitan College, Boston University, Boston, Massachusetts 02215
Abstract:
The rising dominance of superplatforms raises concerns about conflicts of interest and externalities on innovation, because they act as both marketplace gatekeepers and strategic investors. Studying Google’s dual role via Google Ventures (GV) and the Android marketplace, we find Google-backed startups receive 115% more reviews, 12% higher ratings, and 77% fewer bug complaints than their iOS counterparts—despite no major app updates. These gains are consistent with selective review removal and platform promotion. Using a difference-in-differences approach with iOS versions as controls, we isolate treatment from selection effects. Our findings challenge platform neutrality and highlight subtle mechanisms of competitive distortion. This paper was accepted by D. J. Wu, information systems. 11. Determinants of Norm Compliance: Moral Similarity and Group Identification
Alexander Schneeberger; Erin Krupka
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08000
Department of Corporate Development and Business Ethics, Faculty of Management, Economics and Social Sciences, University of Cologne, Cologne NRW 50923, Germany; and Max Planck Institute for Behavioral Economics, Bonn NRW 53113, Germany; School of Information, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109; and Institute for the Study of Labor, Bonn NRW 53113, Germany
Abstract:
What determines whether someone complies with a social norm? The social identity approach offers a mechanism for norm compliance: A person who feels similar to a group identifies more with that group and, in turn, complies with the group’s norms. Using an experiment, we test whether similarity in values increases identification and adherence to a group rule. To do so, we varied the similarity/dissimilarity between the values of an individual and members of a social group and measured group identification and rule compliance. We find that moral similarity in progressivism increases group identification, and group identification can increase compliance with rules from the social group. However, our results also suggest that this relationship can be disrupted. Because of our design and empirically verified in our study, social norms do not differ between treatments. As a consequence, observed behavior changes are attributable to increased group norm sensitivity and not changes in group norms. We advance the study of social identity by testing a causal pathway between group identification, group norm sensitivity, and behavior change. Our contribution to the management literature is to extend the empirical rationale, and cautionary notes, for investing in value-alignment between individuals and organizations. 12. Excessive Heat and Correctional Officer Turnover: Evidence From Texas
Benjamin Goehring; Jacob Harris
Public Administration Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1111/puar.70233
Frank Batten School of Leadership and Public Policy University of Virginia Charlottesville Virginia USA; Department of Criminal Justice and Criminology, Andrew Young School of Policy Studies Georgia State University Atlanta Georgia USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Correctional work is difficult, dangerous, and hot. Many US prisons lack air‐conditioning, resulting in sweltering conditions for both incarcerated people and the street‐level bureaucrats tasked with their supervision. We examine the effect of working in the summer heat without air‐conditioning on correctional officer turnover using personnel records of nearly 60,000 Texas Department of Criminal Justice (TDCJ) employees across 68 prisons between 2010 and 2023. We demonstrate that, among the least‐experienced frontline employees before the onset of COVID‐19, the difference in the probability of departing between those working in prisons without and with air‐conditioning is, on average, 0.33 percentage points larger in the hottest months than in the coolest—an approximately 12% change relative to the baseline departure rate. Back‐of‐the‐envelope estimates suggest that installing air‐conditioning to combat understaffing is comparable in cost to raising salaries, the TDCJ's standard response to high turnover.

16. Selective Recommendation and Credible Exaggeration in Multidimensional Communication
Ye Jin; Zhen Zhou
Economic Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1093/ej/ueag148
The University of Osaka, New York University Abu Dhabi; Tsinghua University
Abstract:
Abstract A sender who wants a receiver to acquire items regardless of quality cannot communicate credibly about one item, but with several she can, by recommending weakly better items. We characterise a continuum of selective recommendation equilibria combining partial statements with full statements recommending everything. Recommendations in partial statements are always followed; those in full statements are followed probabilistically, leaving senders indifferent. Full statements remain credible despite recommending bad items—what we call credible exaggeration. In experiments, senders use partial statements selectively alongside full statements; receivers follow nearly all recommendations in partial statements and almost half in full statements, transmitting information.

22. Theory‐based foundations of Bayesian entrepreneurship
Joshua S. Gans; Erin L. Scott; Scott Stern
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70070
Rotman School of Management University of Toronto Toronto Ontario Canada; Sloan School of Management Massachusetts Institute of Technology Cambridge Massachusetts USA; NBER Cambridge Massachusetts USA
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Entrepreneurs can observe the same evidence yet reach different conclusions about an opportunity. We connect the theory‐based view with Bayesian Entrepreneurship by separating a qualitative theory of value creation—the mechanisms it admits, what it rules out, and where it applies—from the probabilistic beliefs needed to use it. The separation explains why actors disagree despite shared evidence, why different disagreements call for different experiments, and why an experiment can improve prediction without separating theories. It also distinguishes disagreement about an opportunity from differences in costs, constraints, or capabilities. When evidence is thin, a more focused theory can make acting worthwhile sooner without being more accurate. A worked theory card for Vanguard shows how to diagnose disagreement, and we outline tests of the resulting hypotheses. Managerial Summary Founders, investors, and mentors often read the same evidence differently because they hold different theories of how a venture will create value, not because one side lacks information. Before asking for “more traction,” they should state the founder's theory and its strongest rival, then locate the disagreement: whether a mechanism matters, how strong it is, which market conditions will arise, whether lessons transfer between settings, or how predictable results are. Each calls for a different test. When the parties share a forecast but differ in costs, contracts, or capabilities, the remedy is a change in terms or staging rather than another test. A worked example for Vanguard shows the method. Focused theories can justify acting sooner, but focus does not make them right.

Abstract:
Purpose As geopolitical shifts reshape global sourcing, firms increasingly reconsider suppliers in regions previously excluded by sanctions, boycotts and other geopolitical instruments. Such exclusions often leave a lasting stigma, and suppliers in these regions struggle to establish legitimacy as buyers discount their signals. This research examines how supply chain intermediaries undertake legitimation work to overcome suppliers' legitimacy deficits that otherwise deter reintegration. Design/methodology/approach We conduct a longitudinal multiple-case study in the Uzbekistan cotton–textile industry (2019–2026), a revelatory instance of reintegration after the lifting of a global boycott. The three cases are buyer–supplier relationships in this setting, each brokered by the same three intermediaries. We draw abductively on 46 interviews with buyers, suppliers and intermediaries, over 80 h of observations, and 2,231 pages of archival documents. Findings Uzbek suppliers face three legitimacy deficits: cognitive illegibility, pragmatic incompatibility and moral fragility. In response, intermediaries employ three strategies: (1) cognitive bridging to render suppliers comprehensible; (2) network grafting to make exchange workable; and (3) norm cascading to embed global standards into supplier practices so that compliance becomes credible and durable. Together, these strategies move suppliers from exclusion to accepted sourcing relationships with global buyers. Originality/value We extend supply chain geopolitics research to reintegration, showing how a previously excluded region re-enters global sourcing following a geopolitical shift. We theorize intermediated legitimation as the mechanism enabling this, with legitimation work shifting to the intermediary. Contrary to the sequence assumed in prior literature, cognitive legitimacy emerges as the gateway to pragmatic and moral legitimacy.
36. Refine more, explore less? How buyer–supplier inventor ties shape innovation search outcomes
Bowen Zheng; Baozhuang Niu
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-07-2026-1021
School of Business Administration, South China University of Technology , ,
Abstract:
Purpose Innovation in supply chains depends on technical coordination between buyers and suppliers, yet close coordination may constrain a firm's ability to search beyond established supplier interfaces. This study examines how inventor ties shape buyers' exploitative and exploratory innovation as distinct outcomes of innovation search. Inventor ties refer to overlaps in inventor identities across buyer and supplier patent records. We further examine whether these relationships vary with technological distance and supplier concentration. Design/methodology/approach We use a panel of 1,872 buyer–supplier dyad-year observations from Chinese A-share listed firms over the period 2007–2024 and estimate firm fixed-effects models. The analysis examines the relationships between inventor ties and two innovation outcomes within firms. It also tests the moderating effects of technological distance and supplier concentration. Findings Inventor ties are positively associated with exploitative innovation but negatively associated with exploratory innovation. Technological distance weakens the positive relationship with exploitative innovation and weakens the negative relationship with exploratory innovation. Supplier concentration strengthens both relationships because a concentrated supply base reduces the buyer's flexibility to redirect innovation across alternative supplier interfaces. Originality/value The study shows that inventor ties can facilitate interface-based refinement while also reinforcing innovation lock-in. It advances operations and supply chain management research by explaining how technical actors embedded in buyer–supplier interfaces shape firms' innovation portfolios, thereby linking supply chain coordination mechanisms to the exploration-exploitation trade-off.

British Journal of Political Science
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1017/s0007123426101811
Universität Potsdam
Abstract:
Abstract Do race or merit make an international expert? While existing research has uncovered epistemic biases related to gender and professions, racial bias in international organisations’ expert selection has not been captured. We argue that signalling whiteness is central to the certification of expertise because expert status is built on racialised structures of epistemic power. Examining individual-level data from 2,053 applications and selection outcomes across several stages in 124 decision rounds between 2011 and 2024 for the United Nations’ Special Procedures, we show that expert selection is subject to racial bias when candidates are chosen for in-person interviews and how markers of whiteness and merit overlap. We also find that non-white experts can increase their chances of being selected when they possess higher merit-related credentials than their white counterparts. Our research informs scholarship on racism in international relations, the production of expertise in international organisations, and human rights politics.

42. Derivation of a Risk‐Based Recommended Exposure Limit for Arsine
Nathalia Canellys; Su‐Yin Chiang; Wei‐En Huang; Hsiao‐Yu Yang; Kuen‐Yuh Wu
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-24 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70392
Institute of Environmental and Occupational Health Sciences College of Public Health, National Taiwan University Taipei Taiwan; School of Chinese Medicine, College of Chinese Medicine China Medical University Taichung Taiwan; Institute of Food Safety and Health College of Public Health, National Taiwan University Taipei Taiwan
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Arsine (AsH 3 ) is a toxic gas widely used in semiconductor manufacturing and metal refining, yet existing occupational exposure limits (OELs), including the US OSHA permissible exposure limit of 200 µg/m 3 (50 ppb) and the nonregulatory American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) threshold limit value of 16.0 µg/m 3 (5 ppb), lack modern, science‐based derivations. Although AsH 3 is not classified independently by International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), its primary in vivo metabolites, inorganic arsenic (iAs) species, are Group 1 human carcinogens. This study conducted an occupational risk assessment deriving recommended exposure limits (RELs) for both sub‐chronic hemotoxicity (REL non‐cancer ) and carcinogenic risk (REL cancer ). Benchmark dose (BMD) modeling was applied to continuous endpoints from a 90‐day inhalation bioassay. Circulating red blood cell count reduction in male rats was the most sensitive endpoint. The Hill model provided the optimal fit, yielding a BMD lower confidence limit (BMDL) of 0.050 ppm. Adjusting for occupational exposure duration (BMDL adj = 0.038 ppm) and applying an uncertainty factor of 100 established an REL non‐cancer of 1.17 µg/m 3 (0.38 ppb). Assuming systemic biotransformation of inhaled AsH 3 to iAs, lifetime exposure at the REL non‐cancer using the updated US Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA) cancer slope factor for iAs yielded an incremental lifetime cancer risk of 6.2 × 10 −3 , exceeding the acceptable risk of 1.0 × 10 −3 . Restricting risk to this threshold yielded an REL cancer of 0.2 µg/m 3 (0.06 ppb). Legacy international standards exceed these health‐protective REL cancer by one to three orders of magnitude, underscoring an urgent need to modernize workplace safety regulations across global semiconductor supply chains.
