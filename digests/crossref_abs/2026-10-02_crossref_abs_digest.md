    Abstract People may fail to recall and use information they already know when making decisions. We empirically investigate whether such “retrieval failures” distort consumption-smoothing behavior among Zambian farmers, who derive their income from one annual harvest and spend it over the course of the year. We document that individuals underestimate upcoming spending by 50%, creating scope for undersaving. - [Unrest, Shifting Power, and Death-Watch War](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0020818326101490)
  - DOI: 10.1017/s0020818326101490
  - Authors: Jesse C. Johnson; Scott Wolford
  - Affiliations: The University of Texas at Austin
  - Published: 2026-10-02
  - Crossref journal: International Organization
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Domestic unrest often creates problems of both shifting power and shifting preferences. We analyze a model in which a foreign state considers a “death-watch” attack against a domestic state temporarily weakened by unrest in which (1) two polarized factions compete for the power to set policy in the future and (2) the foreign state’s favored faction may support an invasion. Military success allows the foreign state to seize policy control, but defeat may hasten the rise of its disfavored faction when the favored faction links the outcomes of the two conflicts by bandwagoning with the attacker. In equilibrium, decisions to attack and bandwagon depend on the interaction of shifting power and shifting preferences. We show first that temporary weakness causes war, but only when the favored faction is unlikely to survive unrest. Second, increasing factional polarization can encourage or discourage foreign attack and local bandwagoning, conditional on how domestic preferences are shifting. Third, extensions show that (1) death-watch attacks are possible at any distribution of power; (2) the threat of attack may not be sufficient to deter domestic unrest; and (3) attack may be profitable even when alternative options of aiding a favored faction are available. We also use the model to explain why similar episodes of unrest in Ukraine prompted Russian invasion in 2014 but not 2004.

- [Contestation of International Organizations: Patterns, Drivers, and Responses](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0020818326101477)
  - DOI: 10.1017/s0020818326101477
  - Authors: Jonas Tallberg; Stefanie Walter; Michael Zürn
  - Affiliations: Stockholm University; University of Zürich; WZB Berlin Social Science Center
  - Published: 2026-10-02
  - Crossref journal: International Organization
  - Abstract:
    Abstract The contestation of international organizations (IOs) over past decades has inspired a rich research program. This essay takes stock of progress by reviewing key findings in existing scholarship and identifying critical areas for future study. We propose a typology that distinguishes between internal contestation, which criticizes the institutions or policies of an IO with the aim of reform, and external contestation, which challenges an IO’s foundational principles and very existence. Building on this conceptual distinction, we review the most important findings of existing research on three key themes: patterns of contestation, drivers of contestation, and responses to contestation. We also argue that this conceptualization helps us better understand the changing nature of contestation: while criticism of how IOs function has long been a staple of world politics, it recently appears to have shifted toward a deeper form involving more fundamental questioning of IOs themselves. We conclude by highlighting the need for dynamic theories and improved measurements to explain when and how IO contestation shifts from internal criticism to external rejection, and by outlining a forward-looking research agenda on organizational robustness, hegemonic contestation, and the relationship between IO contestation and the broader contestation of global governance.
