- [Navigating the Sea of Reviews: Unveiling the Effects of Introducing AI-Generated Summaries in E-Commerce](https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2024.1282)
  - DOI: 10.1287/isre.2024.1282
  - Authors: Qili Wang; Yi SU; Liangfei Qiu; Runyu Chen
  - Affiliations: University of Florida Warrington College of Business, Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Gainesville, Florida, United States; University of Florida, ISOM, Gainesville, Florida, United States; University of Florida, Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Gainesville, Florida, United States; University of International Business and Economics, e-commerce, Beijing, China
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Information Systems Research
  - Abstract:
    Amidst the burgeoning incorporation of generative AI into commercial realms, numerous companies are exploring ways to leverage generative AI tools in practice. However, there is still a lack of empirical evidence to examine their effectiveness. In this research, we explore how AI-generated summaries (AIGS), a relatively new generative AI tool, influence consumer review behavior. While one may assume that AI-generated content diminishes users’ incentive to contribute by replacing user-generated content, we find the opposite: the introduction of AIGS actually leads to an increase in the volume of consumer reviews. We propose that this effect is driven by enhanced perceptions of collective efficacy. By highlighting the value of consumer reviews as a collective force, AIGS reinforces contributors’ belief that their individual input meaningfully contributes to a shared body of product knowledge, thus motivating participation. This mechanism is supported by an additional controlled laboratory experiment where we directly measure perceived collective efficacy and find that it mediates the effect of AIGS on the likelihood of leaving a review. Our moderation analysis results reveal that the positive effect of AIGS on review contributions is more pronounced among products that are well-reviewed (vs. under-reviewed) and polarizing (vs. non-polarizing). Moreover, a fine-grained review content analysis shows that reviews after the AIGS introduction exhibit enhanced content richness and provide complementary information beyond the AIGS content. Specifically, contributors discuss fewer product attributes already covered by AIGS, discuss more product attributes not covered by AIGS, and elaborate on AIGS product attributes rather than restating the summary. Taken together, these findings advance our understanding of how AI integration influences consumer review behaviors in e-commerce and offer valuable practical guidance for business strategies on employing generative AI.

- [Facade of Trust: The Impact of Adopting AI-Enhanced Profile Pictures on Trust](https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2023.0431)
  - DOI: 10.1287/isre.2023.0431
  - Authors: Sijia Ma; Chong (Alex) Wang; Xiaoquan (Michael) Zhang; Tao Lu
  - Affiliations: Tilburg University Tilburg School of Economics and Management, Department of Management, Tilburg, 5037 AB, Netherlands; Peking University, Information Systems, Beijing, Beijing, China; Chinese University of Hong Kong, Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, Shatin, N.T., CUHK Business School, Hong Kong; Southern University of Science and Technology, Information Systems, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Information Systems Research
  - Abstract:
    Virtual profiles are critical elements of online social interactions, often featuring portraits enhanced by AI-driven tools such as beauty filters and virtual makeup. While the increasing use of these technologies reshapes how individuals present and perceive themselves, little is known about their implications for trust-related behavior. Drawing on self-presentation theory, this study examines how adopting an enhanced profile photo influences behavioral trust and trustworthiness toward others. We conducted a randomized experiment based on the trust game and adapted it by embedding a novel profile photo enhancement module. Participants were assigned to either a treatment group, in which they adopted an AI-enhanced profile photo, or a control group, in which they used their original photo. Our findings reveal an intriguing asymmetry. Senders with an enhanced photo demonstrated less trust by sending smaller amounts. Conversely, receivers with an enhanced photo displayed greater trustworthiness, returning higher percentages. These effects were offset when participants were interacting with an opponent using an enhanced photo. Further analysis indicates that enhanced self-presentation increases perceived risk, which explains the observed reduction in trust. We also document gender differences: female senders show a larger decline in trust, while male receivers exhibit a greater increase in trustworthiness. Overall, this research highlights the asymmetric behavioral consequences of AI-powered self-presentation and underscores the role of risk perception in linking profile photo enhancement to trust behavior in online interactions.

  - Published: 2026-10-04
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management Studies
  - Abstract:
    Abstract This study illuminates the role of brokers, that is, actors spanning inter‐organizational boundaries, in the process of collective identity formation in fragmented industry groups. Through an in‐depth longitudinal analysis of the Franciacorta wineries from the 1960s to the 1990s, we uncover how technical consultants acting as brokers facilitated collective identity formation by interacting with wineries through three mechanisms: translation, cross‐pollination, and integration. Our grounded model of collective identity formation reveals a distributed and co‐creative pathway to cohesive identity understandings built from the interstices of fragmented industry groups, through the co‐creation of practices, the weaving of social connections, and the facilitation of an optimally distinctive collective identity via inward adaptation and outward alignment between broader industry domains and local contexts. Our study advances theory by foregrounding the more inconspicuous practice‐level and social work that precedes, and ultimately determines the success of, the more visible identity‐defining moments that happen via symbolic representation or institutional codification.

- [Theorizing Cross‐Boundary Strategizing as Situated in Multilayered Supply Chains: A Strategy‐as‐Practice Approach to Supply Chain Management](https://doi.org/10.1111/jscm.70035)
  - DOI: 10.1111/jscm.70035
  - Authors: Qian Li; Julia Grimm; Paula Jarzabkowski
  - Affiliations: Warwick Business School University of Warwick Coventry UK; Stockholm Business School Stockholm University Stockholm Sweden; House of Sustainable Society Stockholm School of Economics Stockholm Sweden; UQ Business School University of Queensland St Lucia Australia; Bayes Business School City St George's, University of London London UK
  - Published: 2026-10-04
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Supply Chain Management
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Supply chain strategy is increasingly recognized as a multilayered phenomenon, with firms strategizing not only within their own boundaries but across supply chain tiers, jurisdictions, and wider regulatory and ecological systems. Yet most existing supply chain management research tends to treat these tiers, jurisdictions, and systems as contexts for strategizing rather than as something strategizing helps to constitute, leaving the cross‐boundary character undertheorized. This article introduces the strategy‐as‐practice (SAP) lens as an epistemic tool for theorizing cross‐boundary strategizing in multilayered supply chains. Drawing on SAP's tripartite framework of practices, practitioners, and praxis (three Ps), we show that strategizing is inherently cross‐boundary, both implicitly through the knowledge practitioners draw on beyond their organization and explicitly through direct interaction with counterparts in other firms and institutions. We introduce three approaches SAP scholars use to conceptualize the complex systems within which strategizing is situated: a “tall” ontology separating intraorganizational from extraorganizational practice, a “flat” ontology treating strategizing as a web of practices stretching sideways, and a zoom‐in/zoom‐out approach tracing connectivity and identifying consequentiality across layers. Using a heuristic case of a European solar company and its Chinese supplier network, we show how each approach can shape the research orientation and theoretical payoff. We close with a research agenda for cross‐boundary strategizing in supply chains and argue that supply chains have the potential to extend SAP's own theoretical apparatus.

- [Mapping Interactions Within EU Food and Feed Risk Analysis: A Comparative Study of Risk Assessors and Risk Managers](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.70376)
  - DOI: 10.1111/risa.70376
  - Authors: Axel Menning; Leonie Dendler; Joao Borges; Anneluise Mader; Mats Gunnar Andersson
  - Affiliations: German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) Berlin Germany; Wageningen University Wageningen the Netherlands; National Veterinary Institute (SVA) Uppsala Sweden
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Risk Analysis
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Considering prominent calls for various forms of cooperation, coordination, and collaboration within risk analysis processes, this empirical research study aims to delineate current forms of interaction between risk assessors and risk managers. Utilizing an online survey, it analyses responses from 95 professionals working in the field of food and feed risk analysis across at least 23 EU countries and determines interaction scores based on the degree of cooperation, coordination, and collaboration. The scores show that current interaction dominantly aligns with cooperation across multiple dimensions. However, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) reveals significant perceptual differences between risk assessors and managers regarding their mutual interaction. In particular, risk assessors perceive their interaction with risk management more cooperative and risk managers perceive their interaction with risk assessment more collaborative. A notable difference was also observed in the interaction scores for routine risk analysis versus rapid risk analysis. In addition, clustering analysis and regression analysis was conducted to examine the relation and impact of different interaction dimensions on perceived quality of risk analysis. While the results do not support uniform calls for more collaboration, they show positive effects of open and frequent communication on the perceived quality of risk analysis. These results suggest working towards more individualized and fit‐for‐purpose interactions as well as to extend practitioner‐oriented dialogue on desired modes of interactions, to enhance mutual understanding in their collective risk analysis.
