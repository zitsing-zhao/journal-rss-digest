- [The Networks of Others: A Model of Secondhand Brokerage and its Effects on Employee Innovation](https://doi.org/10.1177/01492063261480612)
  - DOI: 10.1177/01492063261480612
  - Authors: Rohit S. Piplani; Travis J. Grosser
  - Affiliations: Melbourne Business School, University of Melbourne; University of Connecticut
  - Published: 2026-09-07
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management
  - Abstract:
    Social network research on creativity and innovation has primarily focused on the focal employee (i.e., ego), implicitly assuming that ego’s own network position determines the resources available for creative work. We advance an alter-centric perspective, arguing that what matters is not merely the availability of diverse information but the capacity of network contacts (i.e., alters) to make it usable. The brokerage characteristics of ego’s alters (i.e., secondhand brokerage) are decisive in that alters who are brokers are likely to have both valuable non-redundant information and the capability to convey it effectively. Prior work treating secondhand brokerage as a monolithic construct has yielded equivocal results; we reconcile these findings by decomposing it into distinct forms and matching each to the demands of a specific phase of the innovation process (i.e., idea generation, idea elaboration, idea championing, and idea implementation). We theorize that external secondhand brokerage facilitates idea generation, internal secondhand brokerage facilitates idea elaboration, internal alters’ network oscillation facilitates idea championing, and external alters’ network oscillation facilitates idea implementation, with ego’s knowledge integration capability, psychological ownership, organizational status, professional status, and tie strength serving as phase-specific and cross-phase boundary conditions. In doing so, we also challenge the prevailing view of brokers as default opportunists, specifying when cooperation, rather than opportunism, is the more rational strategy. By integrating the social network and innovation literatures, our framework clarifies how, when, and why the networks of others shape ego’s success across the innovation process.

  - DOI: 10.1111/puar.70197
  - Authors: Manon Koopman; Koen Migchelbrink; Leonie Heres
  - Affiliations: Department of Public Administration and Sociology Erasmus University Rotterdam Rotterdam the Netherlands
  - Published: 2026-09-06
  - Crossref journal: Public Administration Review
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT The moral decision‐making and behavior of politicians is influenced by the distinctive and characteristic attributes of political office. This literature review analyzes 80 articles from public administration, political science and related disciplines to systematically map these attributes and their underlying mechanisms. This study identifies six categories of attributes of political office that relate to moral decision‐making and behavior: political system design, political role, relations to societal actors, legal and regulatory measures, political cycle, and some general attributes such as the size of the political institution. Interestingly, existing research focuses mostly on unethical behavior, creating white spots in our understanding of how the attributes of political office shape individual and collective processes of moral decision‐making and behavior more generally. We conclude with an agenda for future research that advocates for more targeted research of the sociopsychological aspects that shape the moral decision‐making and behavior of politicians across their different roles.

- [FabLabs as Promissory Organizations: A future-making perspective](https://doi.org/10.1177/01708406261488577)
  - DOI: 10.1177/01708406261488577
  - Authors: Maria Rita Micheli; Marta Gasparin; Daniel Hjorth; Mario Campana Campana; Amanda Shantz
  - Published: 2026-09-06
  - Crossref journal: Organization Studies
  - Abstract:
    This paper examines FabLabs as machine-centred promissory organizations, where futures are not only imagined, but also materially enacted. Drawing on new materialist and process perspectives, we conceptualize FabLabs as promissory assemblages of entangled human and non-human actors, through which future-making is enacted. We show that machines are central agents for the materialization of imagined futures, especially for the boundaries they create between what is possible and what is not possible to actualize. Based on an ethnographic study of 20 FabLabs across nine countries conducted over twelve years, we identify two core processes – fabulation and fabrication – through which the future is first created and then materialized. Fabulation refers to narratively performed imagination leading to the creation of imagined futures, while fabrication concerns the material experimentation and prototyping to test and eventually materialize imagination. This study advances research on promissory organizations by revealing their material underpinnings; it also extends future-making research by demonstrating how futures emerge through iterative material-discursive practices, rather than through linear planning. Overall, the paper contributes a machine-centred account of future-making, highlighting how imagination, materiality, and technology co-produce what futures become possible for entrepreneurs.

- [Corporate Design as a Symbolic Governance Device: A comparative analysis of governance tensions at German and Danish universities](https://doi.org/10.1177/01708406261488565)
  - DOI: 10.1177/01708406261488565
  - Authors: Angela Graf; Klarissa Lueg
  - Published: 2026-09-06
  - Crossref journal: Organization Studies
  - Abstract:
    Corporate design has become a prominent yet underexplored element of contemporary university governance. While often treated as a technical or aesthetic branding tool, in this study we conceptualize corporate design manuals as symbolic governance devices through which universities organize legitimacy, authority, and organizational coherence under conditions of limited managerial control. Drawing on a qualitative comparative analysis of corporate design manuals from German and Danish universities and informed by institutional theory, we examine how university leadership discursively frames and mobilizes corporate design. The findings reveal four governance tensions through which corporate design manuals operate as symbolic governance devices: (1) authority and authorship, (2) participation and compliance, (3) standardization and discretion, and (4) external visibility and internal organization. The comparative analysis shows that German universities use corporate design in organizational transformation and legitimation, while Danish universities enact corporate design as part of existing, routinized and professionalized governance infrastructures. We contribute to institutional theory by demonstrating how formalized communicative artifacts symbolically stabilize hybrid governance arrangements and mediate between managerial authority and collegial autonomy in higher education.

Drawing on textual analysis and 30 in‐depth interviews with members of the “Roim Rachok” (“Looking Ahead”) program, which facilitates the inclusion of individuals on the autism spectrum in the Israel Defense Forces as a case study, we addressed this lacuna by exploring how policy entrepreneurs independently advance policy change.     ABSTRACT The Social Amplification of Risk Framework (SARF) is intended to facilitate the integration of studies about risk across natural, biological, physical, and social sciences. Researchers employing SARF have advanced understandings of the complex dynamic responses of social systems to hazards, the ways that risks are experienced and perceived, and the ways that consequences can ripple across social and governance systems, geographies, and economic sectors. In seeking to build on a Special Issue of Risk Analysis on SARF organized and edited by Kasperson, et al. - [Risk Calibration: Divergent Interpretations of Risk Assessment in Norway's Early COVID‐19 Response](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.70355)
  - DOI: 10.1111/risa.70355
  - Authors: André R. Karlsen
  - Affiliations: NTNU Samfunnsforskning AS Trondheim Norway
  - Published: 2026-09-06
  - Crossref journal: Risk Analysis
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT During the early phases of novel disease outbreaks, risk management must rely largely on non‐pharmaceutical interventions, since consensus‐based biomedical solutions are yet to be developed. This paper examines how and why expert organizations arrive at divergent interpretations of the same risk assessments, and what such divergence means for risk management strategies. Using Norway's early COVID‐19 response as a qualitative case, the study analyzes the disagreement between national health authorities over suppression versus mitigation strategies. Data consist of interviews with key decision‐makers and senior analysts, supplemented by extensive interview material and reports from the Norwegian Corona Commission, and are analyzed using thematic analysis. The findings show that the disagreement did not primarily stem from different estimates of epidemiological risk, but from divergent interpretations of what those estimates implied: where the main consequences of the pandemic would concentrate, and whether existing health‐system capacities could control the situation. To explain this, the paper introduces the concepts of (1) risk epicenter, where the risk is interpreted to concentrate and (2) risk controllability, the assessed institutional capacity to control it. Together, these constitute a process termed risk calibration, through which technical risk assessments are socially and institutionally interpreted. The contribution is a two‐dimensional framework for analyzing the structure of divergent interpretations during the early phase of novel risk events. The study argues that incorporating both dimensions into early‐phase risk analysis is essential for understanding policy disagreement and improving preparedness for future large‐scale, novel risks.

- [Temporal regularities in scientific authorship: the distribution of time to previous publication](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-026-05800-0)
  - DOI: 10.1007/s11192-026-05800-0
  - Authors: Jeppe Nicolaisen
  - Published: 2026-09-07
  - Crossref journal: Scientometrics
  - Abstract:
    Abstract This study examines whether the time interval between successive publications by individual authors follows systematic statistical regularities analogous to those observed in classical productivity research. While Lotka’s law describes how scientific output is distributed across authors, far less is known about the temporal spacing of their publications. Using bibliographic data from Scopus, the analysis covers five subject fields representing contrasting research domains. For each field, authors active in a reference year are identified, and the number of years since their most recent prior publication is recorded, yielding a measure of time to previous publication. Across all fields, the resulting distributions are highly skewed, with a strong concentration of short intervals and a pronounced long tail. Power-law modeling provides limited support, as any such behavior is confined to the upper tail and accounts for only a small fraction of observations. Over the fitted range, lognormal models perform at least as well as the power-law model in all five fields and significantly better in three. These findings suggest that temporal regularities in scientific authorship differ from those governing how publication output is distributed across authors, pointing to complementary but distinct dimensions of scientific activity.
