  - Published: 2026-09-22
  - Crossref journal: Information Systems Research
  - Abstract:
    Machine-learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) systems often perform well in controlled settings but fail when deployed in the real world. These failures stem from a fundamental problem: ML/AI models optimize data-model fit by increasing statistical performance on training data, whereas real-world deployment demands a new paradigm in ML/AI: model-world fit that preserves domain semantics and contextual understanding. We propose a Machine Learning Model-World Fit framework that provides both a conceptual structure for understanding how domain knowledge shapes ML/AI systems and a research roadmap for investigating these relationships. The framework identifies how domain knowledge can be integrated into ML/AI development process via external knowledge representations, such as data, value, process models or domain ontologies. Incorporating external knowledge representations can help overcome three types of knowledge deficiencies in AI: know-what, know-how, and know-why. To illustrate the framework, we apply it to a child welfare placement case, a domain where contextual understanding is critical and statistical accuracy is an insufficient evaluation criterion. Our insights lead to several directions for scholars working at the intersection of machine learning, design science, and knowledge representation.

  - DOI: 10.1177/01492063261481739
  - Authors: Haoying (Howie) Xu; Xing Wang; Daan van Knippenberg
  - Affiliations: Stevens Institute of Technology; Colorado State University; Rice University
  - Published: 2026-09-22
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management
  - Abstract:
    Organizational scholarship has long focused on leadership behavior, largely assuming that “good” and “bad” leaders can be distinguished by distinct sets of behaviors that can be organized into constructs with an inherent positive or negative valence (e.g., transformational, ethical, abusive). This assumption has guided extensive construct development and generated a substantial body of evidence consistent with each leadership construct’s expected valence. Yet, emerging research has documented the “dark side” of positive leadership and the “bright side” of negative leadership, revealing effects that contradict these expectations—effects that we call leadership contradictory effects. Despite growing interest in this line of research, the literature remains fragmented, with limited clarity regarding its scope and little theoretical integration explaining why, how, and when such effects occur. This motivates our review, which synthesizes these fragmented findings into a cohesive theoretical account organized around shared themes that cut across leadership constructs. First, we define leadership contradictory effects and review major approaches to operationalize them. Then, we advance an integrative framework that delineates three major theoretical themes, their associated theories, and three categories of boundary conditions to explain why, how, and when leadership contradictory effects arise. Finally, we propose paths forward, including suggestions to guide future research in this emerging domain. Overall, our work reorients scholars and points them beyond valence-based assumptions about leadership effects as a means to explore a broader range of consequences.

  - Authors: Linda Allen; Lin Peng; Yu Shan

  - Published: 2026-09-22
  - Crossref journal: Management Science
  - Abstract:
    We study how intercommunity social networks influence loan demand and supply on fintech lending platforms. Demand for online loans rises following increases in online borrowing activity in geographically distant but socially connected areas. On the supply side, borrower area social proximity to deposit-rich regions increases funding likelihood and is associated with better ex post loan performance. We establish causality with shift-share instrumental variables (SSIVs) obtained from natural disasters (demand side) and financial adviser misconduct (supply side). Social connectedness expands both loan demand and supply in disadvantaged communities without increasing delinquency rates. Intercommunity social networks raise awareness of alternative lending platforms and transmit hard-to-obtain information that mitigates community-level information asymmetry. - [How Third‐Party Sellers Respond to Platform Owner Entry: Reselling Versus Private‐Label Modes in E‐Commerce](https://doi.org/10.1111/isj.70064)
  - DOI: 10.1111/isj.70064
  - Authors: Shiying Zheng; Xiaodie Pu; Alain Yee Loong Chong
  - Affiliations: Nottingham University Business School China The University of Nottingham Ningbo China Ningbo China
  - Published: 2026-09-22
  - Crossref journal: Information Systems Journal
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT E‐commerce platforms depend on a large base of third‐party sellers, yet platform owners increasingly enter the product markets of those sellers, competing with them while retaining control over the search rankings and recommendation systems that shape which products buyers encounter. Prior research on this phenomenon has produced mixed findings and has largely focused on innovation platforms (e.g., app stores and software ecosystems), leaving e‐commerce marketplaces comparatively underexplored. This study examines how third‐party sellers respond to two distinct modes of platform owner entry—reselling, in which the owner begins selling an existing product on the same product listing, and private‐label entry, in which the owner introduces a separately listed substitute whose exposure is also shaped by platform‐controlled search and recommendations. Using longitudinal data from Amazon.com and discontinuous growth modelling, we find that sellers immediately reduce prices following reselling entry but increase prices following private‐label entry. Over the longer term, both entry modes attenuate the trajectories of seller participation and investment in FBA relative to their pre‐entry trajectories, with stronger attenuation following private‐label entry. These findings extend research on platform owner entry by showing that third‐party seller responses vary systematically across entry modes, moving beyond treatments of owner entry as a single competitive event. They also develop a visibility‐based explanation for why seller responses differ across entry modes and show that pricing, participation and platform‐specific investment may unfold over different time horizons following platform owner entry.

  - DOI: 10.1177/10591478261489979
  - Authors: Ciwei Dong; Xi Li; Xiutian Shi
  - Affiliations: Zhongnan University of Economics and Law; University of Hong Kong; Nanjing University of Science and Technology
  - Published: 2026-09-22
  - Crossref journal: Production and Operations Management
  - Abstract:
    Retailers incur price-adjustment costs called menu costs when changing the prices of their products over time. These products are often sourced from upstream suppliers, who endogenously determine wholesale prices, which have cascading effects on retail prices. In this paper, we consider the pricing strategies of supply chain members in the presence of menu costs. Our analysis suggests that even a minuscule menu cost can substantially affect pricing strategies and the equilibrium outcome. Upstream manufacturers always benefit from a menu cost whereas downstream retailers benefit from only a moderate menu cost and are hurt by both high and low menu costs. Meanwhile, menu costs always alleviate the issue of double marginalization, thereby improving supply chain profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare. This study provides channel members with guidance on how to set prices in the presence of menu costs. It also recommends retailers to optimally maintain a moderate menu cost.

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT This study contributes to global risk analysis by providing a structural and exploratory enhancement to the analytical framework used to interpret the World Economic Forum's Global Risks Report (GRR), particularly the 2024 edition. It explores the complex interactions among critical global and regional risks using interpretive structural modeling (ISM) and systems thinking (ST)‐based causal loop diagrams (CLDs). Rather than offering validated intervention guidance or predictive modeling, the study focuses on qualitative and structural mapping of risk interdependencies. Instead of combining the two methodologies, their insights are systematically integrated to provide a deeper understanding of global and regional risk dynamics. Through ISM, the research identifies hierarchical relationships among risks, highlighting key drivers and consequences, while CLDs reveal reinforcing feedback loops and interdependencies that illustrate the cyclical nature of systemic risks. The complementary use of ISM and CLDs offers exploratory insights beyond existing GRR network metrics by making explicit the hierarchical positioning of risks and their qualitative feedback structures. This study positions its contribution as groundwork for future quantitative modeling and scenario analysis, providing interpretive and structural insights rather than operational decision support.
