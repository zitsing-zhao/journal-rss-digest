2. Navigating the Family Support Dilemma in Female Entrepreneurship: An Embeddedness Perspective
Mulu Hundera; Stefanie Habersang; Mona Mensmann
Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice
ABS 4* | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1177/10422587261496320
Department of Corporate Development, Faculty of Management, Economics, and Social Sciences, University of Cologne, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany; Institute of Management and Organization, Leuphana University of Lüneburg, Lower Saxony, Germany
Abstract:
Drawing on family embeddedness and gender performativity, we examine how family support is enacted and governed through gendered norms, and how female entrepreneurs navigate these dynamics. Based on indepth interviews with female entrepreneurs and family members in Ethiopia, we introduce the family support dilemma: a process in which support enabling female entrepreneurship simultaneously generates explicit or internalized obligations that constrain sustained engagement and growth over time. Our findings show that family support is embedded in gendered social structures, functioning as a site of norm enforcement where support is granted or withdrawn based on women’s enactment of gender role expectations.
3. Unintended Consequences of Holding Dollar Assets
ROBERT CZECH; SHIYANG HUANG; DONG LOU; TIANYU WANG
Journal of Finance
ABS 4* | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1111/jofi.70097
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We propose and examine a novel mechanism whereby holdings of dollar assets by foreign institutions can have a large, unexpected impact on foreign Treasury yields in crisis periods. Non‐U.S. institutions hold substantial dollar assets and hedge dollar exposures by selling dollars forward. In crisis periods, the dollar appreciates against other currencies. To meet margin calls on foreign exchange (FX) hedging positions, traditionally passive institutions liquidate domestic safe assets to raise cash. We show that during the recent COVID crisis, U.K. institutions with substantial dollar holdings and FX hedging positions sold large amounts of gilts, contributing significantly to the observed gilt yield spike.
4. Crossing the Design–Use Divide: How Organizations Select Innovations to Adopt
Rebecca Karp
Administrative Science Quarterly
ABS 4* | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1177/00018392261493079
Harvard Business School
Abstract:
Prior scholarship on technology and organizations has focused either on how innovations are designed or how they are used, often assuming that successful selection stems from successful design. But this assumption has limited our understanding of patterns of resistance and advocacy that can influence how and whether innovations reach organizations in the first place. To explore these patterns, this study asks, how does selection unfold within organizations, and what are the consequences of this process for the design and use of innovations? Drawing on a two-year ethnographic study, I examine how entrepreneurs piloted their AI-based innovations with potential adopting organizations. Rather than testing for learning or generalizability, organizations set up committees that engaged in process manipulation by narrowing the scope of pilots, controlling conversations, assigning pilot responsibility, and using goal claiming to align pilot outcomes with committees’ majority preferences. Committees with more adversarial members designed pilots that tested advanced aspects of entrepreneurs’ innovations, hoping for failure. Committees with a majority that championed the innovations tested basic aspects, ensuring success. While pilots run by champions mostly succeeded, these innovations faced more resistance later, during implementation, than did innovations piloted successfully by adversaries. This study shows that innovation is endogenous to selection, and reveals that champions can temper innovation, while adversaries can enable it.
5. Restaurant Density and Delivery Speed in Food Delivery Platforms
Wenchang Zhang; Zhanzhi Zheng; Ruomeng Cui
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2025.2299
Indiana University Bloomington Kelley School of Business, Operations & Decision Technologies, Bloomington, Indiana, United States; The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Kenan-Flagler Business School, Chapel Hill, North Carolina, United States; Emory University Goizueta Business School, Atlanta, Georgia, United States
Abstract:
Food delivery platforms (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Meituan) are racing to expand by signing as many restaurants as possible. Such expansion directly translates into changes in restaurant density on the platform, making it crucial to understand how density affects restaurant outcomes. This paper examines the impact of restaurant density on incumbent restaurants and compares three key externalities induced by increased density: customer expansion and cannibalization, which are well documented in the literature, and delivery pooling (i.e., drivers being closer to restaurants and bundling multiple orders before delivery), which has received much less attention. Using a queueing-theoretical model, we develop testable hypotheses on how these externalities influence delivery speed, sales, revenues, and customer spending. Employing two-way fixed effects and instrumental variable estimation with data from a major Chinese food delivery platform, we find that higher restaurant density improves delivery speed and sales for partnered restaurants but reduces order size; heterogeneous-effects estimation further reveals that fast-food and high-volume restaurants benefit more from increased density, while made-to-order and thermally sensitive cuisines see weaker gains. Combining the empirical evidence with our theoretical predictions, our evidence is most consistent with delivery pooling playing a more pivotal role than customer expansion or cannibalization in shaping density effects. Furthermore, nonlinear least squares estimation of our theoretical model reveals that driver arrival speed at restaurants rises with density, shortening customers' wait times and inducing them to place more—but smaller—orders. However, this effect follows an inverted-U-shaped pattern, indicating diminishing delivery pooling benefits—and even adverse effects—at very high density. Finally, we extend our theoretical framework to develop a simulation model, estimated using data, which allows us to isolate the effect of each externality and identify restaurant-specific optimal densities through counterfactual simulations. Results show substantial heterogeneity in sales-maximizing densities across restaurants, with most operating below their optima. Our findings also suggest that platforms should prioritize expansion in low- to moderate-density regions to maximize delivery efficiency and restaurant sales, while carefully managing congestion and tailoring support based on restaurant type in denser markets.
6. Online Reusable Resource Allocations with Multi-class Arrivals
Tianming Huo; Wang Chi Cheung
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2022.0612
Singapore Management University, College of Integrative Studies, Singapore, Singapore; National University of Singapore, Department of Industrial Systems and Management, Singapore, Singapore
Abstract:
In this paper, we study an adversarial online reusable resource allocation problem with multi-class arrivals, where each class is associated with its price per unit time and usage duration. Each unit of resource will be returned after the usage duration of the class of customer that the unit has been allocated to. We propose a simple online algorithm which achieve the best possible competitive ratio among all online policies when the usage durations of any two classes are divisible. Its competitive ratio is proved using a novel Auxiliary Algorithm that constructs a feasible solution to a linear program that upper bounds the optimum. We show that our policy can be extended to the case of general durations and the advance booking problem where each usage duration only starts after an arbitrary period. We show that our policy achieves at least half of the optimal when the durations are general, and for advance booking with equal usage durations.
7. Online Decision Making with Local Differential Privacy
Yuxuan Han; Zhipeng Liang; Yang Wang; Jiheng Zhang
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2024.0984
Hong Kong University of Science of Technology, Mathematics, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong; Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Department of Industrial Engineering and Logistics Management, Hong Kong, Hong Kong; Hong Kong University of Science of Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong
Abstract:
Contextual bandit algorithms are useful in personalized online decision-making. However, many applications, such as personalized medicine and online advertising, require the utilization of user-specific information for effective learning while maintaining user privacy due to privacy concerns. This motivates the introduction of local differential privacy (LDP), a stringent notion of privacy, to contextual bandits. In this paper, we design LDP algorithms for stochastic generalized linear bandits to achieve the same regret bound as in non-privacy settings. Our main idea is to develop a stochastic gradient-based estimator and an update mechanism to ensure LDP. We then exploit the flexibility of stochastic gradient descent (SGD), whose theoretical guarantee for bandit problems is rarely explored, to address generalized linear bandits. We further conduct experiments using both simulated and real-world datasets to demonstrate the consistently superior performance of our algorithms under LDP constraints, with reasonably small LDP parameters (ε, δ) to ensure strong privacy protection.
8. Joint Assortment Planning and Inventory Control under General Choice Models
Qiaochu Fu; Woonghee Tim Huh; Yanzhi Li; Tongwen Wu
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2025.1654
City University of Hong Kong, Department of Decision Analytics and Operations, Hong Kong, Hong Kong; The University of British Columbia, Sauder School of Business, Vancouver, Canada; City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong; University of Toronto, Rotman School of Management, Toronto, Canada
Abstract:
We propose a constant-factor approximation framework for the joint assortment planning and inventory management problem, where customers substitute to less preferred in-stock products if more preferred products are out of stock. The customer arrivals follow an increasing-failure-rate (IFR) distribution, and customer purchasing behavior is governed by a discrete choice model with dynamic inventory availability. Our decision involves determining the initial inventory vector, subject to a capacity constraint. Our algorithm begins with a novel fluid relaxation that identifies at most two candidate assortments with the highest potential profitability. For each assortment, we construct a static-allocation-based lower bound on the expected total revenue for an inventory vector that stocks only the products in the assortment. The final inventory decision is the vector that maximizes the stronger of these two lower bounds. We establish a (κ/4−ε)-approximation guarantee for a broad class of substitutable choice models, in which adding products weakly decreases the choice probabilities of existing items, and for which the cardinality-constrained assortment optimization problem admits a κ-approximation algorithm. Our result provides the first constant-factor approximation ratios for this problem under several important choice models, including the Markov chain choice model and the mixture of MNL models. Moreover, our framework also achieves the state-of-the-art approximation ratio of 0.25 for the MNL choice model. Our guarantees can be further strengthened when the capacity constraint is relatively loose or when the demand distribution is deterministic or Poisson.

11. The coevolution of trust and behavioral uncertainty: Toward a theory of vulnerability acceptance in entrepreneurial ventures
Lars Huemer; Jan Ketil Arnulf
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70068
BI Norwegian Business School Oslo Norway
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary This conceptual study develops a process theory of vulnerability acceptance explaining how trust and behavioral uncertainty coevolve as entrepreneurial actors form, interpret, and realize opportunities. Trust is frequently invoked to explain creativity, exploration, and opportunity formation, yet remains theorized through predictability and evaluability, which entrepreneurial opportunities often lack ex ante. We distinguish three forms of vulnerability acceptance: uncertainty creating, releasing, and decreasing trust. Each transforms the uncertainty it engages, which in turn reinforces or unsettles that form, so no form is terminal. Their salience shifts across opportunity formation, interpretive recombination, and realization, and misalignment between a form and the conditions it confronts impairs entrepreneurial progress. Trust thereby becomes a means through which epistemic conditions are produced, not only a response to them. Managerial Summary Entrepreneurship exposes forms of vulnerability that cannot be explained by risk‐based models of trust alone. Entrepreneurs often face the practical question: “Trust them to do what?” The answer depends on the kind of agency they are willing to be vulnerable to, and it changes as an opportunity takes shape. That may mean backing people to take the work somewhere no one can yet specify, relying on others' expertise to make sense of what is emerging, or counting on them to deliver what was agreed. Trust is therefore not only a way of reducing uncertainty but a means of transforming it, and progress depends on recognizing when the prevailing form fits the conditions and when it does not.
12. From Contested Endogenization to Regulatory Unravelling of Mandatory Human Rights Due Diligence: Extending the Endogeneity of Law Framework
Nora Lohmeyer; Elke Schüßler; Sarah Ashwin
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70180
Radboud University; Leuphana University Lüneburg; Johannes Kepler University; London School of Economics and Political Science
Abstract:
Abstract Drawing on the endogeneity of law framework, this study examines how business, legal, public, and civil society actors constructed the meaning and practice of mandatory human rights due diligence in the context of the German garment industry and the German Supply Chain Act. Using interview, document, and observation data spanning the period 2019 to 2026, our findings reveal a two‐stage process: an initial phase of contested endogenization in which actors experienced with private regulation prevented convergence on a managerialized interpretation of the law, followed by regulatory unravelling in which changes in the political‐economic climate provided avenues for substantively weakening the law and preventing its implementation. We discuss how the dual processes of managerializing a law and legalizing of organizations is complicated by three boundary conditions: the complexity of the regulatory issue, the field's regulatory maturity, and the degree of regulatory uncertainty. Connecting the endogeneity of law framework with research on the public and private governance of global supply chains allows us to theorize refinements to this framework when applied to contested, complex governance fields, and to derive a set of theoretical and practical implications for public supply chain regulation.
13. Flexibility revisited: Post-socialist flexibility stigma and its paradoxes for Romanian women corporate leaders
Irina Elena Ion
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261487000
International Business and Economics Faculty, Bucharest Academy of Economic Studies, Romania
Abstract:
This study improves the understanding of the relationship between flexible work and gender regimes by exploring the flexibility stigma in a non-Western country – Romania, a former socialist Central and Eastern European nation, characterised by a particular gender order. The research delves into the experiences of 22 women managers across nine financial organisations through narrative interviews. The article contributes the concept of a hybridised, post-socialist flexibility stigma, defined by a high familistic penalty that emerges from the distinctiveness of the Romanian post-socialist gender regime – demanding workplace expectations of work devotion paradoxically adopted by the respondents despite high personal needs of flexibility – and cultural socialist legacies. Additionally, the research sheds light on organisational mechanisms of persuasion, control and consent manufacturing that organisations use for discouraging flexible work and that contribute to managers’ contradictory roles in stigma formation.
14. Enhancing or Transcending the Pipeline? How Incumbents Transition Toward Marketplace Platforms
Alexander Engelmann; Georg Reischauer; Annabelle Gawer
Journal of Product Innovation Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1111/jpim.70063
WU Vienna University of Economics and Business Vienna Austria; Leuphana University Lüneburg Lüneburg Germany; Johannes Kepler University Linz Linz Austria; Linz Center of Mechatronics Linz Austria; University of Surrey Guildford, Surrey UK
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Business‐to‐business incumbents whose value creation rests on pipeline business models increasingly launch their own marketplace platforms. Yet how these business model transitions toward marketplace platforms unfold, and what they imply for the pipeline model, remains poorly understood. Adopting a cognitive perspective on managerial interpretations and decisions, we conduct a comparative case study of five marketplace platforms launched by incumbents in the European industrial products industry. We find that platform competition and the opportunities managers perceive in business‐to‐consumer platforms trigger platform creation, after which incumbents follow one of two pathways. In pipeline‐enhancing transitions, managers locate future value in the existing pipeline model and adopt platform features that strengthen it, resulting in cognitive alignment and interconnected platform–pipeline business models. In pipeline‐transcending transitions, managers locate future value in the marketplace platform and use business‐to‐consumer marketplaces as a reference point. This generates cognitive tensions with the pipeline model that ultimately resolve into independent pipeline and marketplace platform business models. Which pathway unfolds depends on the customer segments served, which shape managers' reference points for interpreting the evolving platform business model. Our process model explains these divergent outcomes and contributes to innovation management research on business model transitions toward platforms and their cognitive dynamics.
15. The representation of SDG-related research in bibliometric databases: persisting imbalances and varying perspectives
Matteo Ottaviani; Stephan Stahlschmidt
Scientometrics
ABS 4 | 2026-11-02 | DOI 10.1007/s11192-026-05857-x
Abstract:
Abstract The societal impact of research has become an explicit goal of research activities and argument for the funding of the science system. Providers of bibliometric databases algorithmically assign scientific publications to SDG goals to show how single research outputs contribute to society, but substantial disagreements among these classifications have been quantified. Moreover, large language models (LLMs) are being introduced as a technology that processes scholarly records. We apply LLMs to observe what type of research is assigned to SDGs by these automatic procedures and what structural differences emerge. To this end, we fine-tune in parallel three generic LLMs by SDG-classified abstracts in the databases Web of Science, Scopus and OpenAlex and apply the same prompts to the differently fine-tuned LLMs. Five SDGs are being analyzed - three related to social equity and two to economic and technological development. A human-feedback-driven analysis of the generated noun phrases reveals that the LLM-mediated SDG-classified research information fails to represent the most disadvantaged populations, the poorest countries, and underrepresented topics that SDG targets explicitly address, while economic superpowers in the Global North stay very visible. These paradoxical findings indicate the systemic discrimination against marginalized groups operated by the LLM technology within conventional bibliometric classification frameworks, challenging their assumed neutrality, and question the current visibility of the societal impact of research.
16. Orchestrating inter-organizational technology networks to build data analytics capability: the case of a professional service firm
Jas Kalra; Kell Jones; Grant Mills
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-10-2025-1081
Alliance Manchester Business School, The University of Manchester , ,; The Bartlett School of Sustainable Construction, University College London , ,
Abstract:
Purpose This study examines how professional service firms (PSFs) orchestrate inter-organizational technology networks to develop data analytics capability. Adopting an organizational information processing perspective, we examine how PSFs vary orchestration modes across networks to mobilize distributed resources and increase their information-processing capacity. Design/methodology/approach We conducted a longitudinal single-case study of a global PSF in the UK construction sector, triangulating 37 interviews with observations and project documentation. Using an abductive theory-elaboration approach, we explain how the PSF accessed, bundled and leveraged network-embedded resources to build analytics capability. Findings We identify three interrelated components of analytics capability: data capture, data transformation and processing, and data visualization and interpretation. Closed-mode orchestration enabled standardized data capture across the supply network; hybrid orchestration combined broad open engagement with selective closure to integrate technological expertise from SMEs; and open-mode orchestration within the client network fostered use-case elicitation, sensemaking and interpretive alignment. We theorize how inter-organizational network orchestration enables PSFs to assemble information-processing capacity from distributed data, technical expertise and interpretive requirements, thereby improving fit between information-processing needs and capacity. Originality/value We conceptualize analytics capability as a composite inter-organizational capability assembled across organizational boundaries. We extend Organizational information processing theory by showing how information-processing capacity can be assembled across networks of external actors through inter-organizational network orchestration, and advance network orchestration research by identifying a contingency logic linking orchestration mode to the coordination conditions surrounding distributed resources.

19. The Immortality Paradox: One‐Party Dominance, Political Tenure, and Organizational Continuity in Japanese Agencies
Ayako Nakamura
Governance
ABS 4 | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1111/gove.70160
Graduate School of International Relations International University of Japan Minamiuonuma Japan
Abstract:
ABSTRACT In his classic 1976 study on organizational survival, Kaufman argued that public organizations are “immortal”. More recent research, however, has come to the opposite conclusion, finding that public organizations are frequently terminated. These findings largely relate to contexts where changes to the ruling party occur regularly. In contrast, this study examines the survival of government agencies in Japan, where the postwar political system has been characterized by one‐party dominance and short‐lived political leadership. The findings suggest that Japanese agencies remain largely “immortal,” and that when they do undergo transitions this tends to be linked to prime ministers with longer tenures who are able to implement minor, cosmetic changes; hence agencies typically persist even after such events. This study thus sheds new light on how political–administrative relations shape patterns of agency transition and highlights the importance of leadership tenure in sustaining commitments to organizational change.
20. Emulate the Leader? Explaining Crisis Policy Change in the Canadian Federal System
Liddell Hastings
Governance
ABS 4 | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1111/gove.70159
University of Western Ontario London Ontario Canada
Abstract:
ABSTRACT In times of crises, do governments coordinate or do they make their own choices independent of what others do? Convergence of COVID‐19 policy at the beginning of the pandemic has been widely studied in federal countries. Yet, there is limited literature looking at the mechanisms that made policy between jurisdictions so similar. Utilizing Canada's decentralized federal system, this paper posits that convergence can be explained by a process of policy diffusion through emulation and a series of leader‐follower relationships. Drawing on the policy diffusion literature, this paper proposes three hypotheses for why some jurisdictions take on leadership roles in crisis policy while others follow. Synthesized as proximity, capacity, and urgency, these hypotheses are tested using a series of pairwise vector autoregressions identifying significant leader‐follower relationships between Canadian provinces. The findings suggest that policy primarily flowed from only a small number of large provinces (Quebec and Ontario), lending partial evidence, but also exceptions to the hypotheses proposed.

23. Explainable Machine Learning for Risk‐Driven Forecasting of Global Food Commodity Prices: Geopolitical, Logistic, and Energy Drivers
Foued Saâdaoui; Hana Rabbouch
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-01 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70405
Rabat Business School International University of Rabat Sala Al Jadida Morocco; Lab: LR18ES15 Algèbre, Théorie de Nombres et Analyse Non‐linéaire Faculté des Sciences de Monastir Monastir Tunisia; Department of Business Computing Higher Institute of Management of Sousse Sousse Tunisia
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Escalating geopolitical tensions, supply chain disruptions, and energy market volatility have intensified global food price instability, posing serious risks to food security. To address this challenge, this study proposes an explainable forecasting framework that integrates historical price dynamics with external drivers, applied to five essential food commodities, olive oil, eggs, chicken, beef, and bread, over the period 1990–2023. The analysis examines how logistics efficiency, energy costs, and geopolitical uncertainty relate to food prices through complex, nonlinear relationships. Going beyond conventional models, the proposed nonlinear autoregressive neural network with exogenous inputs (NARX) captures these dynamics while preserving explainability: through an ablation‐style comparison against a baseline autoregressive model, it systematically evaluates alternative panels of exogenous inputs to identify those with the greatest forecasting contribution. The results show that the predictive relevance of exogenous panels varies across commodities and evaluation metrics, and underscore the value of transparent, multidimensional analysis for improving food price forecasting and understanding price dynamics in an increasingly uncertain global context.
