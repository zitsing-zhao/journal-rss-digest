- [“The Minimum Wage Is the Maximum Wage”: How Goal Replacement Perpetuates Means–Ends Decoupling](https://doi.org/10.1177/01492063261487366)
  - DOI: 10.1177/01492063261487366
  - Authors: Satwant Dahiya; Christian Busse
  - Affiliations: Carl von Ossietzky University of Oldenburg
  - Published: 2026-10-26
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management
  - Abstract:
    This research examines how organizations contribute to the perpetuation of means–ends decoupling (MED) within issue-based fields organized around societal problems, under conditions of heightened field scrutiny. In such settings, organizations often profess commitment to field goals and visibly comply with institutionally prescribed practices to satisfy monitoring, reporting, and public evaluation requirements. However, many adopted goals remain underachieved, raising questions about how compliance without progress endures and how claims of commitment remain credible under transparency regimes intended to spur substantive goal pursuit. Our data came from 75 interviews with 68 brands, suppliers, and third-party organizations pursuing worker well-being in global fashion supply chains. We unveil auditability-based goal replacement as an organizational-level mechanism through which actors rhetorically foreground compliance as public evidence of commitment while narrowing internal goal pursuit to auditable thresholds that serve their core goals, thereby allowing shortfalls to persist. Self-silencing about this substitution and the instrumentalizing of shortfalls close the configuration into a self-reinforcing loop. The study advances MED scholarship by (i) showing how organizations leverage field conditions and appropriate the existing scrutiny infrastructures to advance their core goals while reproducing the means–ends gap, (ii) problematizing the authenticity of commitment claims, and (iii) explaining how goal-ineffective compliance prevails as a credible substitute for adopted goals. It also extends research on organizational responses to institutional complexity and calls for greater attention to institutional impact—that is, the realized advancement of the societal aim an institution emerged to address.

- [The threat‐enhancing effect of authoritarian power sharing](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.70089)
  - DOI: 10.1111/ajps.70089
  - Authors: Jack Paine
  - Affiliations: Associate Professor, Department of Political Science Emory University Atlanta Georgia USA
  - Published: 2026-10-25
  - Crossref journal: American Journal of Political Science
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Power‐sharing deals can potentially solve the autocrat's commitment problem, yet often fail to prevent conflict. This article develops a dynamic model to examine a largely overlooked friction: Sharing power within an authoritarian regime bolsters the opposition's coercive capabilities. The resultant threat‐enhancing effect creates two distinct commitment problems for the opposition, which determine if and when power sharing occurs. First, the opposition cannot commit to refrain from leveraging its enhanced threat. The ruler prefers to incur a revolt than peacefully share power if the threat‐enhancing effect is large. Second, the threat‐enhancing effect tempts the opposition to wait for a future power‐sharing deal, which undercuts the opposition's commitment to revolt today. New dynamic implications arise: delayed power sharing, conflict, or an increased willingness for the ruler to share power are all possible. Scrutinizing the opposition's limited commitment ability contrasts with the predominant existing focus on the autocrat's commitment problem.

    Abstract Research Summary Entrepreneurs cannot pursue ventures they never imagine. This article develops a generative view of entrepreneurial imagination as the construction of an evolving candidate set through generation, provisional evaluation, and revision. Conceptual search represents venture concepts as text, transforms them through interpretable operations, and compares their promise. A large language model prototype demonstrates complementary modes: local refinement lifts a concept from sixteenth to third among 30 ventures under a validated evaluator, while global recombination reaches distant possibilities that refinement makes competitive. The framework shows how search architecture determines what enters, survives, and develops, and how AI may broaden imagination within firms while producing correlated imagination across firms. Managerial Summary Entrepreneurs can pursue only the ventures they imagine, and valuable ideas routinely go unbuilt because nobody thinks of them. I demonstrate a system that evolves real venture concepts: step‐by‐step refinement lifted a middling concept to third place among 30 real ventures, as ranked by an AI judge shown to predict fundraising, while recombining distant ideas created novel concepts that refinement made competitive. The entrepreneur's job shifts from generating a few ideas to governing many: choosing what to search, keep, and test in the market. One caution: rivals using the same AI will imagine similar ventures; advantage comes from proprietary problems, data, and evidence.

    Abstract Craft as an approach to work has been proposed as an alternative to industrial mass production. While industrialization has led to the replacement of craft logic with industrial logic to create uniformity and scale operations, the role of craft in industrialization remains ambiguous. Building on the analysis of engineering work performed at an R&D laboratory in the pulp and paper industry, we explore how and why craft enables industrialization. Our findings reveal how elements of craft manifest in engineering work by helping to transform ideas into useful objects, balancing scientific accuracy with practical usefulness, and fostering a sense of community and identity within the engineering field.     Process complexity is a widely used concept for analyzing and improving socio-technical processes, but the term is applied in inconsistent ways, and its conceptualizations and operationalizations are often ambiguous.     Abstract In protracted conflicts, civilians frequently encounter low-level, hostile actions from adversary civilians in nearby communities, yet the political consequences of these events remain understudied.     ABSTRACT Whether public concern about risk rises or falls with national development has been debated for three decades, but the debate has compared countries hazard by hazard, asking how much people worry rather than how worry is organized.   - DOI: 10.1111/risa.70399

  - Affiliations: Department of Ecology and Vertebrate Zoology Faculty of Biology and Environmental Protection University of Lodz Lodz Poland
  - Published: 2026-10-26

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Biological invasion decision‐support methods are often compared under broad labels such as “risk assessment,” even when they perform different functions. This Perspective argues that functional commensurability must be established before methods are compared. In biological invasion risk analysis, screening serves an initial risk identification function, flagging extant or horizon non‐native species within a defined risk assessment area for prevention, surveillance, or more detailed evaluation. Comprehensive risk assessment examines entry or introduction, establishment, spread, and their ecological, economic, and socio‐cultural consequences in greater detail. Risk management integrates risk with feasibility, cost, effectiveness, legal constraints, and stakeholder values. Impact classification and post‐establishment population prioritization may contribute to these functions without being equivalent to them. Published comparisons show how conflating such functions can create false equivalence. The emerging Dispersal‐Origin‐Status‐Impact (DOSI) literature provides an illustrative example through its comparisons with applications of the globally implemented Invasiveness Screening Kit (ISK) decision‐support tools: population status and management priority are not the same quantities as prospective invasion risk screening, and species‐based screening does not imply coarse spatial resolution. Forty‐five peer‐reviewed ISK‐family studies report local or subnational screenings, including spatial resolutions comparable with or finer than DOSI applications. Valid comparisons require a shared decision objective, explicit spatial and temporal boundaries, independent evaluation evidence, and assessment of uncertainty and the consequences of error. These requirements permit methods to be evaluated for a common use, or combined within adaptive management, without treating disagreement between unlike outputs as evidence of superior performance.
