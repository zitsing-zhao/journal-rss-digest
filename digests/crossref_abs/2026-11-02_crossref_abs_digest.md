- [Crossing the Design–Use Divide: How Organizations Select Innovations to Adopt](https://doi.org/10.1177/00018392261493079)
  - DOI: 10.1177/00018392261493079
  - Authors: Rebecca Karp
  - Affiliations: Harvard Business School
  - Published: 2026-11-01
  - Crossref journal: Administrative Science Quarterly
  - Abstract:
    Prior scholarship on technology and organizations has focused either on how innovations are designed or how they are used, often assuming that successful selection stems from successful design. But this assumption has limited our understanding of patterns of resistance and advocacy that can influence how and whether innovations reach organizations in the first place. To explore these patterns, this study asks, how does selection unfold within organizations, and what are the consequences of this process for the design and use of innovations? Drawing on a two-year ethnographic study, I examine how entrepreneurs piloted their AI-based innovations with potential adopting organizations. Rather than testing for learning or generalizability, organizations set up committees that engaged in process manipulation by narrowing the scope of pilots, controlling conversations, assigning pilot responsibility, and using goal claiming to align pilot outcomes with committees’ majority preferences. Committees with more adversarial members designed pilots that tested advanced aspects of entrepreneurs’ innovations, hoping for failure. Committees with a majority that championed the innovations tested basic aspects, ensuring success. While pilots run by champions mostly succeeded, these innovations faced more resistance later, during implementation, than did innovations piloted successfully by adversaries. This study shows that innovation is endogenous to selection, and reveals that champions can temper innovation, while adversaries can enable it.

  - Authors: Wenchang Zhang; Zhanzhi Zheng; Ruomeng Cui
  - Affiliations: Indiana University Bloomington Kelley School of Business, Operations & Decision Technologies, Bloomington, Indiana, United States; The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Kenan-Flagler Business School, Chapel Hill, North Carolina, United States; Emory University Goizueta Business School, Atlanta, Georgia, United States
  - Published: 2026-11-02
  - Crossref journal: Operations Research
  - Abstract:
    Food delivery platforms (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Meituan) are racing to expand by signing as many restaurants as possible. Such expansion directly translates into changes in restaurant density on the platform, making it crucial to understand how density affects restaurant outcomes. This paper examines the impact of restaurant density on incumbent restaurants and compares three key externalities induced by increased density: customer expansion and cannibalization, which are well documented in the literature, and delivery pooling (i.e., drivers being closer to restaurants and bundling multiple orders before delivery), which has received much less attention. Using a queueing-theoretical model, we develop testable hypotheses on how these externalities influence delivery speed, sales, revenues, and customer spending. Employing two-way fixed effects and instrumental variable estimation with data from a major Chinese food delivery platform, we find that higher restaurant density improves delivery speed and sales for partnered restaurants but reduces order size; heterogeneous-effects estimation further reveals that fast-food and high-volume restaurants benefit more from increased density, while made-to-order and thermally sensitive cuisines see weaker gains. Combining the empirical evidence with our theoretical predictions, our evidence is most consistent with delivery pooling playing a more pivotal role than customer expansion or cannibalization in shaping density effects. Furthermore, nonlinear least squares estimation of our theoretical model reveals that driver arrival speed at restaurants rises with density, shortening customers' wait times and inducing them to place more—but smaller—orders. However, this effect follows an inverted-U-shaped pattern, indicating diminishing delivery pooling benefits—and even adverse effects—at very high density. Finally, we extend our theoretical framework to develop a simulation model, estimated using data, which allows us to isolate the effect of each externality and identify restaurant-specific optimal densities through counterfactual simulations. Results show substantial heterogeneity in sales-maximizing densities across restaurants, with most operating below their optima. Our findings also suggest that platforms should prioritize expansion in low- to moderate-density regions to maximize delivery efficiency and restaurant sales, while carefully managing congestion and tailoring support based on restaurant type in denser markets.

    We propose a constant-factor approximation framework for the joint assortment planning and inventory management problem, where customers substitute to less preferred in-stock products if more preferred products are out of stock.   - Abstract:
    Abstract Research Summary This conceptual study develops a process theory of vulnerability acceptance explaining how trust and behavioral uncertainty coevolve as entrepreneurial actors form, interpret, and realize opportunities. Trust is frequently invoked to explain creativity, exploration, and opportunity formation, yet remains theorized through predictability and evaluability, which entrepreneurial opportunities often lack ex ante. We distinguish three forms of vulnerability acceptance: uncertainty creating, releasing, and decreasing trust. Each transforms the uncertainty it engages, which in turn reinforces or unsettles that form, so no form is terminal. Their salience shifts across opportunity formation, interpretive recombination, and realization, and misalignment between a form and the conditions it confronts impairs entrepreneurial progress. Trust thereby becomes a means through which epistemic conditions are produced, not only a response to them. Managerial Summary Entrepreneurship exposes forms of vulnerability that cannot be explained by risk‐based models of trust alone. Entrepreneurs often face the practical question: “Trust them to do what?” The answer depends on the kind of agency they are willing to be vulnerable to, and it changes as an opportunity takes shape. That may mean backing people to take the work somewhere no one can yet specify, relying on others' expertise to make sense of what is emerging, or counting on them to deliver what was agreed. Trust is therefore not only a way of reducing uncertainty but a means of transforming it, and progress depends on recognizing when the prevailing form fits the conditions and when it does not.
