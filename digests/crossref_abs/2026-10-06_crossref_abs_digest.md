  - Abstract:
    Rethinking How Cities Prepare for Extreme Rainfall Cities often prepare for extreme rainfall using a single “design storm,” such as a 100-year storm of a predetermined duration. But storms with the same return period can produce very different flooding losses. In “Planning Stormwater Retention for Resilience Against Extreme Rainfalls,” Liu, Qi, and Zhang develop a robust optimization framework that identifies the most damaging rainfall scenario for a city based on its existing drainage and retention infrastructure. Case studies of Toronto, Manhattan, and New Orleans show that the storm a city should prepare for changes with local infrastructure and climate conditions. In Toronto, robust planning could save an estimated $6 million annually today and as much as $1.5 billion annually by 2050.

- [Early Environmental Shocks and Organizational Reconfiguration in High-Growth Ventures](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2023.18371)
  - DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2023.18371
  - Authors: Sunasir Dutta; Pyung Nahm; Luis Adrian Rios
  - Affiliations: University of Minnesota Carlson School of Management, Strategic Management and Entrepreneurship, Minneapolis, Minnesota, United States; University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Carlson School of Management, Minneapolis, Minnesota, United States; Purdue University Krannert School of Management, Strategic Management, West Lafayette, Indiana, United States
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Organization Science
  - Abstract:
    We study whether shocks from local natural disasters can leave durable organizational traces in young high-growth ventures. In principle, such firms may be relatively resilient to localized disruption because they are designed to scale beyond local markets and rely heavily on knowledge-based assets. We examine this possibility using a panel of 19,984 U.S. startups founded between 1985 and 2010 that subsequently received venture financing. We identify ventures whose founding counties experience natural disasters within 12 months of founding using FEMA Major Disaster Declarations. Ventures exposed to disasters at an early stage exhibit higher subsequent failure hazards and no general advantage in follow-on financing, yet show higher IPO hazards in favorable market windows, a pattern consistent with greater responsiveness to opportunities requiring substantial organizational reconfiguration. Descriptive employee-level evidence further documents differences in organizational structure and composition consistent with greater retained fluidity among exposed ventures. Drawing on organization design and imprinting theory, we interpret these patterns as consistent with the possibility that disasters function as forcing environments that entrench early-stage coordination features such as flatness, role fluidity, and broader expertise that would otherwise attenuate as ventures mature. These durable organizational traces may, in turn, shape ventures’ subsequent capacity for reconfiguration. We discuss implications for organizational adaptation, imprinting, entrepreneurial geography, and organizational responses to crises.

  - Published: 2026-10-06
  - Crossref journal: American Political Science Review
  - Abstract:
    Does exposure to crime mobilize or demobilize political participation? We report findings from a preregistered design, leveraging data from an original survey of 21 major U.S. cities and five waves of the Cooperative Election Study. We assess the relationships between direct victimization, local (ZCTA-level) crime rates, and perceived neighborhood safety and two outcomes: turnout (including validated turnout) and non-electoral participation. We find that direct victimization is significantly associated with more non-electoral engagement but lower turnout. The negative estimated effects of victimization on turnout exceed the magnitude of positive effects associated with common get-out-the-vote efforts. We find only limited evidence that crime rates are associated with participation. Perceived neighborhood safety is associated with higher rates of non-electoral, but not electoral, participation. Our findings offer new, comprehensive evidence on the relationship between crime exposure and political participation and illustrate the importance of distinguishing between types of exposure and forms of participation.

  - DOI: 10.1111/joms.70173
  - Authors: Rajeev J. Sawant; Barclay E. James
  - Affiliations: Florida Atlantic University; St. Mary's University
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management Studies
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Property rights theory (PRT) focuses on how allocation of residual control rights of ownership resolves incentive problems from incomplete contracts. However, when asset ownership is efficient for a transacting party, PRT ignores financial resource constraints preventing the party from purchasing it. We include creditors, who provide financial resources but are exposed to agency conflicts with equity‐owners, in property rights analysis. Multinational enterprises (MNEs) reduce agency conflict with creditors through contractual covenants and collateral. Yet contracts are incomplete. Under incomplete contracts, how do MNEs mitigate financing risk, the risk that supply of capital from creditors is delayed or entirely unavailable? We distinguish between incomplete contracts arising from weak property rights institutions and weak contracting institutions. We theorize that allocation of specified control rights through operating and maintenance (O&M) contracts to MNE‐owners mitigates financing risk in countries with weak property rights institutions because MNE‐owners possess high‐powered incentives to exert requisite effort to adapt operations to unexpected contingencies. In contrast, such allocation of O&M contracts to MNE‐owners increases financing risk in countries with weak contracting institutions because such allocation increases agency conflict between MNE‐owners and creditors. Empirical analyses of 363 project‐financed investments in 52 countries worth US$1.53 trillion over 1990–2018 support our theory.

- [Business Model Bundles: Implications for Innovation in the Circular Economy](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpim.70057)
  - DOI: 10.1111/jpim.70057
  - Authors: Miying Yang; Steve Evans
  - Affiliations: School of Management Cranfield University Bedford UK; Institute for Manufacturing University of Cambridge Cambridge UK
  - Published: 2026-10-05
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Product Innovation Management
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Business model (BM) innovation is essential to the transition towards a circular economy, yet customers often resist circular BMs. This study investigates how manufacturers can innovate BMs for circularity in ways that increase customer acceptance. Drawing on a multiple‐case study of four manufacturers offering 21 product‐service system BMs, we introduce a new concept, the business model bundle: a strategic offering that packages multiple BMs within one contract and gives customers flexibility to move between them over time. We identify three defining characteristics: the combination of different BMs, contractual flexibility, and temporal transition. We theorize the BM bundle as a new form of BM innovation that creates, delivers, and captures value through the configuration of existing BMs rather than the invention of new ones. Our evidence indicates that the flexibility offered by BM bundles can reduce customers' initial commitment and make previously resisted circular BMs more acceptable. However, bundles may lead towards either greater or lesser circularity and therefore require deliberate design. The study extends innovation management and BM research by introducing the BM bundle that could open a new avenue in the field of BM innovation, and by identifying flexibility as a mechanism linking BM bundles to firm‐perceived customer acceptance.
