- [Partisan Banks and Creditor Coordination Within Loan Syndicates](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679x.70097)
  - DOI: 10.1111/1475-679x.70097
  - Authors: Robert M. Bushman; Sang Woo Sohn; Jianxin (Donny) Zhao
  - Affiliations: University of North Carolina; City University of Hong Kong; Emory University
  - Published: 2026-11-16
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Accounting Research
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT We investigate how firm‐level partisanship influences interactions between banks in the syndicated loan market. Because politically divergent banks hold conflicting perceptions of economic conditions, ideological distance between banks impedes consensus and elevates expected coordination costs. We find that greater political distance between a lead arranger and a participant reduces the participant's likelihood of joining a syndicate and lowers its retained loan share relative to the lead. The relation between political distance and loan share differences intensifies during periods of high national political polarization. Moreover, greater political diversity within a syndicate is associated with less restrictive loan covenants and higher interest spreads, reflecting a trade‐off where lenders sacrifice contingent control to avoid costly renegotiation gridlock. Validating our disagreement mechanism, we show that following presidential elections, banks politically misaligned with the incoming administration issue significantly more pessimistic macroeconomic forecasts than aligned banks.

  - Abstract:
    The (post)pandemic period was marked by numerous port disruptions, leading to delays and rising costs for shippers. We build a fluid model in transient states to investigate how a disruption at one port can affect the disrupted port and its counterpart in another country in a circulatory container shipping system, where a stream of fleets moves goods back and forth between the two ports. Port disruption leads to two types of congestion: the inbound backlogs, which occur when ships are unable to enter the port, and the outbound backlogs, which arise when goods are unable to be loaded onto ships for transport to other ports. We analytically characterize the system’s recovery time, measured from the onset of the port closure until normal operations are restored, and the dynamics of inbound and outbound backlogs during recovery. We identify a whiplash effect in which outbound backlogs alternate between ports, creating oscillatory congestion patterns because of the disruption-induced uneven spatial distribution of ships. Our analysis reveals that simply increasing ship capacity may not expedite system recovery, highlighting the importance of coordination between port authorities and shipping companies when jointly planning capacity expansions. Furthermore, we examine several extensions of the model. We use the two-port system as a building block to analyze bipartite and cyclic network structures, and we also consider scenarios in which a port experiences multiple disruptions. In all cases, the whiplash effect persists, thereby demonstrating the robustness of our findings. Finally, we use our theory model prediction as a feature and apply machine learning techniques to predict the time that vessels spend in Shanghai Port after a typhoon disruption and show that using our model predictor, alongside all relevant vessel and voyage attributes as raw features, significantly reduces prediction errors by 10%–30% on average compared with not using it.     We show that a biodiversity risk premium has emerged in the U.S. corporate bond market following the United Nations Biodiversity Conference (COP15) in October 2021. COP15 placed biodiversity on the global regulatory agenda and raised transition risks for exposed firms.   - Abstract:
    The rise of social media platforms has enabled customers to rely on influencers and peer reviews to guide their purchasing decisions. As products increasingly focus on experiences, firms are leveraging influencer marketing as a crucial tool. This paper addresses key challenges of influencer marketing by considering different types of influencers and partnership schemes alongside the impact of social learning. We introduce a two-period model wherein, in the initial period, the firm unveils a new product experience of unknown quality alongside an influencer marketing campaign.   - Published: 2026-11-16

  - Abstract:
    Abstract Research Summary Startups use experimentation to reduce technological and market uncertainty, but experimentation is costly and unevenly funded. This paper examines how government‐funded experimentation relates to subsequent performance among high‐tech startups that receive federal awards. Using data on $32 billion in US government R&D contracts awarded to high‐tech startups between 2001 and 2018, I distinguish between technology experimentation, measured by the value of R&D contracts, and market experimentation, measured by the number of agencies awarding the R&D contracts. Market experimentation is positively associated with subsequent employment, sales, and acquisition, while technology experimentation shows weaker associations with these outcomes. The findings are consistent with startups using information from multiple government buyers to learn about market opportunities and improve subsequent performance. Managerial Summary High‐tech startups need to test both whether their technology works and where demand exists, but venture capital often leaves this experimentation unfunded. Using data on $32 billion in US government R&D contracts awarded to high‐tech startups from 2001 to 2018, this paper shows that startups funded by more government agencies subsequently have higher employment and sales and are more likely to be acquired. Larger R&D contract amounts have less consistent relationships with these outcomes. For managers, the findings suggest considering what different government customers can help the firm learn. For policymakers, they suggest that the diversity of a startup's public customer base is a vital margin to consider alongside the overall size of its R&D awards.

  - DOI: 10.1111/joms.70178
  - Authors: Yuping Zeng; Sangcheol Song; Soonkyoo Choe
  - Affiliations: Southern Illinois University Edwardsville; Saint Joseph's University; Yonsei University
  - Published: 2026-11-16
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Management Studies
  - Abstract:
    Abstract This study examines the differentiated influences of host culture heterogeneity on the learning outcomes of multinational enterprises' (MNEs) success and failure experiences in foreign direct investments (FDI). We posit that host culture heterogeneity among prior successful FDIs reduces the beneficial effect of an MNE's success experience on a subsequent FDI, particularly when the cultural differences between the subsequent FDI and the MNE's prior successes are significant. In contrast, host culture heterogeneity among prior failed FDIs enhances the learning benefits of failure experience, and this effect is more pronounced when the subsequent FDI is culturally distant from the prior failures. Using FDI data of South Korean MNEs between 1990 and 2016, we find support for our hypotheses, demonstrating that host culture heterogeneity has divergent impacts on the learning outcomes of MNEs' success versus failure FDI experiences. This study enhances our understanding of the conditions under which host culture heterogeneity may weaken or strengthen the learning benefits of an MNE's FDI experience.
