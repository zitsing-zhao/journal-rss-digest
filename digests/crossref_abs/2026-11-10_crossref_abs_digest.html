1. Presidential Address: Human Finance: Incorporating Insights from the Life Sciences
ULRIKE MALMENDIER
Journal of Finance
ABS 4* | 2026-11-09 | DOI 10.1111/jofi.70096
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Many of the key determinants of human behavior identified in the life sciences, such as stress response, impulse control, and mood regulation, remain absent from standard economic and finance models. This paper illustrates how such insights can enhance economic models in the context of inflation expectations. Combining a retrospective survey that elicits personal inflation histories with an experimental intervention that induces the reexperiencing of historic inflation, I show that physiological, emotional, and cognitive stress markers predict heightened inflation expectations and sharper memory recall, while also shaping personal economic narratives. These findings suggest novel policy tools to counter the persistent scars of economic distress and crisis experiences, for example, by designing experiences to provide predictability and control.

5. Growth Paths in Early Machine Learning Ventures: Technical Configuration and Competitive Advantage Over Time
Jack Fraser; Pinar Ozcan
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-10 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2022.17147
Imperial College Business School, Management and Entrepreneurship, London, England, United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland; University of Oxford, Saïd Business School, Oxford, Oxfordshire, United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland
Abstract:
Machine learning capabilities have become important strategic assets, yet little is known about how their technical characteristics affect the competitive advantage of the firms that build them. Through an inductive, multicase study tracking six early-stage machine learning ventures in an accelerator program, we explore how their phases of specialization and exploration may be constrained by properties of the underlying technology. Our findings show how training data, learning paradigms, and model outputs depend on one another and on the applications they serve, and how these relationships change over time. This interdependence shapes firms’ growth paths, alternately expanding or limiting the strategic options available to them. Characteristics that confer an early competitive advantage can constrain later growth. We discuss the implications for strategy research on competitive advantage and path dependence in machine learning ventures.
6. Generative <scp>AI</scp> and the architecture of knowledge work: Expertise bypass, exchange governability, and shifting bottlenecks
Michael G. Jacobides; Mingyu Dalbert Ma; Amartya Das; Matt Langione; Sesh Iyer
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-09 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70069
Strategy &amp; Entrepreneurship Area London Business School London UK; BCG Institute The Boston Consulting Group Inc Boston Massachusetts USA; Chubb Chubb Limited New York New York USA
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Research on GenAI and work has largely focused on tasks. We ask when task‐level effects alter knowledge‐work industry architecture: the allocation of activities across firm boundaries. Using a three‐stage abductive design—an executive roundtable, a panel of 626 firm websites (2017–2025), and 33 interviews plus two further roundtables with C‐level executives and corporate directors—we document divergent repositioning: professional services firms foreground relational value, while interface‐oriented technology firms emphasize scalable interfaces. We find that activity reallocation is shaped by two independently varying dimensions: expertise bypass and exchange governability. Their intersection yields four configurations: contestable reallocation, persistent bundles with selective insourcing, federated expert provision, and integrated expert provision. We extend industry architecture theory by explaining change when production and exchange conditions shift separately. Managerial Summary GenAI is not simply making knowledge work more efficient—it may be changing which organizations perform which work. We document that professional services firms are emphasizing relationship‐driven, judgment‐intensive work, while interface‐oriented technology firms are extending scalable delivery. Our framework explains why task exposure alone does not determine where work moves. We distinguish between expertise bypass—whether GenAI can produce an output without the expertise previously required—and exchange governability—whether that activity can be specified, verified, priced, and warranted across firm boundaries. Where both are present, work becomes contestable and may be reallocated. Where they diverge, highly exposed activities may remain bundled, while less exposed, human‐intensive work may move through federated specialist models. These conditions shape which organizational forms become viable.

8. Making ‘Gender‐Lens Investing’ Work: Cultivating Proto‐Infrastructure for Alternative Financial Futures
Danielle Logue; Markus A. Höllerer; Yi Zhao; Tanja Ohlson
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-10 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70177
UNSW Sydney; WU Vienna; IAE Business School; Frankfurt School of Finance and Management
Abstract:
Abstract This study examines how elites engage in prefigurative organizing and construct the institutional infrastructure for enacting alternative futures. We investigate their engagement in gender‐lens investing (GLI) within the global movement for gender equality in finance and investment. Using fuzzy‐set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) of 28 GLI mutual funds and exchange‐traded funds, we diagnose how fund managers combine available resources to prefigure GLI as investment products, identifying distinct configurations of symbolic, relational, and material resources through which GLI is enacted and made recognizable and actionable in the present. In doing so, we show that prefigurative efforts are not exclusive to grassroots activism, and demonstrate that the infrastructure supporting alternative practices is built through elites' efforts to recombine existing institutional resources into proto‐infrastructure. We therefore contribute a combinatorial perspective of prefigurative organizing that highlights the role of elites, and introduces proto‐infrastructure as a conceptual device for theorizing how prefigurative practices begin to acquire an incipient institutional form prior to the full consolidation of institutional infrastructure. This combinatorial perspective offers a new and timely lens for understanding GLI, impact investing, and the gender equality movement under current political backlash.

10. Leaders as Theorists
Teppo Felin; Robert E. Ployhart; Todd Zenger
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-10 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70182
University of Utah; University of Oxford; University of South Carolina
Abstract:
Abstract Research on leadership has long focused on two extremes, either on individual traits (or styles and behaviours) or on relationships and context. We argue that neither extreme explains where leadership originates from or how it produces the collective outcomes it is credited with. We propose a third view that is neither a compromise between nor a variant of these extremes. We argue that leaders are theorists. Leaders are forward‐looking actors who, at any level of an organization, formulate causal theories and frame problems. The value of leader theories is realized, amplified or constrained through social mechanisms. Theorizing is not one activity among many – it is the act that constitutes leading itself. The very factors foregrounded by recent work in leadership (like context, trust, and relationality) are not the starting point of leadership but emerge as leaders advocate, adopt, test, and scale theories. The leader‐as‐theorist agenda is normative and interventionist, highlighting how leader theorizing and causal reasoning can be improved and taught. We close by laying out a research agenda for studying leaders as theorists.

13. Issue‐Centric Orchestration of Climate Transition: The Case of Transition Risk Governance
Niina Hakala; Fatma Jemaa
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-09 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70187
University of Turku; EDHEC Business School
Abstract:
Abstract A decade ago, climate transition fell outside the mandates of financial regulators, central banks, accounting standard‐setting bodies, and financial‐market actors. Today, it is central to financial‐market governance aimed at steering business conduct toward a low‐carbon economy. Drawing on a longitudinal study of climate transition as a financial governance concern from 2010 to 2021, we show how governance authority over emerging sustainability concerns is assembled when no actor has an established mandate to address those concerns. Informed by Latour's concept of matter of concern, our analysis reveals how climate transition became constituted as transition risk, was stabilized through the private industry‐led Task Force on Climate‐related Financial Disclosures, and was translated across interconnected governance sites. We develop a three‐phase process model of issue‐centric orchestration – issue emergence, stabilization, and translation – unpacking how governance authority is progressively assembled through the trajectory of transition risk. The study contributes to public–private governance research by showing how private governance initiatives can reshape and extend, rather than displace or reduce, public authority. It further advances orchestration research by showing how interdependent governance arrangements form around shared concerns across governance domains and jurisdictions.

22. Walk the talk: how procurement professionals' interpretations shape social sustainability cascading
Svetlana Castre de Chabot; Salomee Ruel; Anicia Jaegler; Stefan Gold
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-11 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-11-2025-1123
KEDGE Business School Sustainability Center of Excellence, , ,; OCRE Research Lab, EDC Paris Business School , ,; De Vinci Research Center, De Vinci Higher Education , ,; Technical University of Munich TUM Campus Straubing for Biotechnology and Sustainability, , ,
Abstract:
Purpose Procurement professionals shape how firms translate their commitments to social sustainability into supplier-facing governance, which can vary markedly, even within the same firm. This study explains this variation by theorizing how procurement professionals interpret their role and translate engagement with suppliers into governance practices. Design/methodology/approach The study draws on constructivist grounded theory and interviews with 27 professionals across organizational contexts. The analysis combines iterative coding, constant comparison, theoretical sampling, and abductive engagement with sensitizing literature. Findings The study develops a process model that explains cascading depth from a people-centric perspective. The interviewed procurement professionals differed on whether they construed their remit to include social sustainability, whether supplier-facing action appeared worth pursuing, and whether more improvement-oriented engagement appeared feasible. This situated judgment shaped whether cascading is enacted predominantly through procedural compliance or through more generative supplier-facing practice. Prospective paradoxical sensemaking helped explain how more generative supplier-facing responses could remain actionable under competing commercial demands, as professionals saw anticipated social and commercial consequences as relevant to their judgment in the present. Originality/value The study reframes cascading depth as a matter of how procurement professionals enact governance mechanisms. It specifies interpretive and relational conditions that remain underexplored in the literature on sustainable behavioral supply chain management. It extends paradoxical sensemaking research on how tensions are framed to include how more generative responses may be established and sustained in practice.
23. Digital transformation of service ecosystems: an orchestration perspective
Bingyu Han; Liangjuan Qin; Liang Li; Wei Zhang
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-11 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-04-2026-0492
School of International Trade and Economics, University of International Business and Economics , ,; School of Artificial Intelligence and Data Science, University of International Business and Economics , ,; College of Management, University of Massachusetts Boston , , ,
Abstract:
Purpose This study investigates how digital transformation unfolds within logistics service ecosystems and how inter-actor operational connections and coordinating roles change over time from an orchestration perspective. Design/methodology/approach The authors conducted a case study of three international inland ports in China. Data were triangulated through interviews, observations and documents to capture ecosystem-level orchestration practices and transformation evolution. Findings The authors developed a process model that identifies three evolutionary stages of the digital transformation of service ecosystems: developing digital connectivity, tapping digital service potential and co-creating digital value. The model demonstrates how actors employ resource and network orchestration at each stage. The analysis reveals how these two forms of orchestration interact and how orchestration dynamics change over time. Originality/value This study shifts the focus of digital transformation research from a few focal firms to service ecosystems overall. It theorizes a three-stage transformation process, reveals the evolving interplay between resource and network orchestration, shows that different actors can assume orchestration leadership, and provides actionable recommendations for managers seeking to digitally transform logistics and supply chain service ecosystems.

26. Public Risk Judgments of Endocrine‐Disrupting Chemicals: Psychometric Structure, Exposure Pathways, and Cross‐National Differences
Nick Simonsen; Aleksandr Pravednikov; Carl‐Johan Lagerkvist; Sonja Perkovic
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-10 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70381
Department of Management Aarhus University, Aarhus, Denmark; Department of Economics Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, Uppsala, Sweden
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Endocrine‐disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are pervasive in everyday life and pose well‐documented public health risks, yet little is known about how lay perceptions of EDC risks are structured and how they vary across exposure pathways and regulatory contexts. Drawing on the psychometric paradigm of risk perception, we conducted a large cross‐national survey () in Denmark, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Participants evaluated 18 EDC‐related exposure scenarios across nine canonical risk attributes, ranked perceived health risks by exposure pathway (consumption, inhalation, and dermal contact), and reported perceived severity, susceptibility, overall health risk, institutional trust, and sociodemographic characteristics. Across all three countries, risk perceptions exhibited a robust two‐dimensional structure corresponding to affective‐dread and cognitive‐deliberate dimensions. However, the relative salience of these dimensions varied systematically: cognitive‐deliberate cues accounted for more variance in Denmark, whereas affective‐dread cues were more prominent in the United Kingdom and the United States. Perceived risk also varied consistently by exposure pathway, with consumption judged the most risky, followed by inhalation and dermal contact, a hierarchy that showed minimal cross‐country variation. At the individual level, greater knowledge of EDCs consistently predicted higher perceived susceptibility, while gender, year of birth, income, parenthood, and institutional trust showed context‐ and outcome‐specific associations. By extending the psychometric paradigm to chronic, diffuse chemical hazards embedded in everyday activities, this study demonstrates that EDC risk perceptions reflect shared affective‐cognitive dimensions while remaining sensitive to exposure pathways and governance contexts, offering theoretically grounded insights for risk communication and policy design.
27. When Scientific Uncertainty is Linked to Lower Information Avoidance: Motivational Pathways to Public Engagement With Climate Risk Information
Soo Jung Hong
Risk Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-09 | DOI 10.1111/risa.70411
Department of Communications and New Media Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences National University of Singapore Singapore Singapore
Abstract:
ABSTRACT This study examines how scientific uncertainty is associated with both appetitive and aversive motivations, as well as its direct and indirect associations with risk information avoidance (RIA). Drawing on Liu and Chen's (2026) appetitive–aversive motivational framework, based on the Planned Risk Information Avoidance (PRIA) model, we investigate how uncertainty relates to information avoidance through risk perception, perceived information insufficiency, informational efficacy, information overload, information‐seeking norms, and negative emotions. Using structural equation modeling with a US adult sample, the findings indicate that scientific uncertainty is negatively associated with information avoidance and is linked to multiple motivational pathways. Specifically, higher scientific uncertainty is associated with greater risk perceptions and information insufficiency, and with lower levels of information overload, which in turn correspond to reduced avoidance intentions. These findings provide insight into the role of scientific uncertainty in shaping RIA, highlighting its potential to reduce avoidance in the context of climate change.
