Affiliations: University of Virginia Charlottesville Virginia USA; NBER Cambridge Massachusetts USA; Université Paris 1– Panthéon‐Sorbonne Paris France; Paris School of Economics Paris France; CESifo Munich Germany; Université Paris‐Dauphine–PSL Paris France; CEPR London United Kingdom
ABSTRACT We study the impact of techies—engineers and other technically trained workers—on firm‐level productivity. We first report new facts on techies in the firm using French administrative data. Techies are STEM‐skill intensive and associated with innovation, technology adoption, management, and diffusion within firms. Using structural econometric methods, we estimate the effect of techies on firm‐level Hicks‐neutral productivity in manufacturing and nonmanufacturing. We find that techies raise productivity, and that this effect goes beyond R&D workers, extending to ICT and other techies. In nonmanufacturing firms, the impact operates mostly through ICT and other techies, not R&D workers.
Measuring the Stringency of Building Height Regulations
2026-08-04 | DOI: 10.1111/iere.70093 | International Economic Review
Authors: Yusuke Adachi
Affiliations: College of Economics Ritsumeikan University Kusatsu Shiga Japan
ABSTRACT This study proposes a novel methodology to measure the stringency of building height regulations. This measure gauges the difference in building heights with and without regulations. The primary challenge is to recover unobserved building heights in the absence of regulations. I develop a dynamic discrete choice model of building height decision‐making by parcel owners and conduct a counterfactual experiment that removes height regulations. An application to residential buildings in central Tokyo presents three findings. Without height restrictions, the number of stories would increase by 2.7%, and housing prices would change by 3.7%. Regulatory stringency is stronger near the central area, while the static model tends to overestimate it.
Climate Change and Economic Dynamics: Temperature‐Dependent Shock Propagation
2026-08-04 | DOI: 10.1111/iere.70097 | International Economic Review
Authors: Christian Glocker; Thomas Url
Affiliations: Austrian Institute of Economic Research Vienna Austria
ABSTRACT The gradual rise in temperatures motivates conceptualizing climate change as a phenomenon shaping the propagation of macroeconomic shocks, rather than as an independent shock. We formalize this in a theoretical model, showing that climate change induces a structural shift by steepening the aggregate supply curve, exacerbating the price effects of demand shocks while dampening the output response. Our empirical evidence is consistent with this prediction: higher temperatures raise the share of inflation variation attributable to demand shocks by up to 10 percentage points, underscoring the role of climate change in intensifying stagflationary dynamics rather than independently driving business cycles.

Abstract We estimate how the timing of parental job loss shapes children’s human capital by exploiting plant closures in Danish administrative data. We match treated children to observably similar untreated peers and difference out age-invariant selection using children whose parents are displaced after school completion. Job loss during infancy (ages 0–1) reduces ninth-grade mathematics achievement by 0.04 standard deviations and raises non-completion by one percentage point. Effects are smaller in later childhood. Treated infants are 1.4 percentage points less likely to enrol in general upper-secondary programs. We find that liquidity-constrained families drive these results, suggesting that transient income shocks create permanent educational scars.
