2. Juking the Stats: Policing, Misreporting, and Policy Evaluation
Michael Gibilisco; Carlo M Horz
Review of Economic Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1093/restud/rdag130
California Insitute of Technology; Washington University in Saint Louis
Abstract:
Abstract We analyze a game-theoretic model of crime and crime reporting to study the quality of crime statistics. An offender potentially engages in illicit behavior; an enforcer chooses effort and how to report outcomes. Because of signaling concerns, the enforcer may misreport. We show that multiple equilibria can exist and characterize when crime is under- or overreported. Increasing the enforcer’s costs of data manipulation can backfire, increasing misreporting in crime statistics. When calculating treatment effects of parameter changes, the effect on reported statistics will not equal the effect on true statistics, and the true effect can be under- or overestimated.
3. External Enablement Across Types of Environmental Change: Experimental Evidence on Onset Variance Effects
Jiyoung Kim; Per Davidsson; Denis A. Grégoire
Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1177/10422587261496319
University of Queensland, Queensland, Australia; Jönköping International Business School, Jönköping, Sweden; Australian Centre for Entrepreneurship Research, Queensland University of Technology, Queensland, Australia; HEC Montréal, Québec, Canada
Abstract:
A growing research stream applies the external enablement framework (EEF) to capture how macroenvironmental changes benefit entrepreneurial pursuits. To refine and nuance the EEF’s guidance of future research in this domain, we examine the provisionally suggested structural relationships among its constructs. Combining between-subjects experiments and think-aloud protocols, we show that variance in the characteristics of macroenvironmental changes—specifically, their gradual versus radical onset—influences expectations of enablement and does so similarly across types of change. Our results also uncover relationships between macroenvironmental changes and agents beyond what the EEF explicates. We discuss implications for future research on macroenvironmental impact on business.

5. Breaking Gender Norms: A Dynamic Perspective on Entering Gender-Atypical Occupations
Sophie Schellhammer; Florian Kunze
Journal of Management
ABS 4* | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1177/01492063261506258
University of Konstanz
Abstract:
Occupational gender segregation remains a defining feature of labor markets worldwide, and efforts to recruit individuals into gender-atypical occupations often fail because newcomers drop out shortly after entry. Understanding what drives this early attrition requires examining the day-to-day experiences of newcomers whose gender is underrepresented in their occupation, especially during the critical first weeks of organizational socialization. Integrating role congruity theory with a dynamic work-events perspective, we propose that gender-atypical newcomers experience an intensifying trajectory of perceived negative work events over time, and that this intensifying trajectory undermines their task mastery and heightens their turnover intention. Results from a longitudinal field study applying a repeated-measures design to 673 newcomers across 88 occupations over 12 weeks support these predictions. Newcomers working in gender-atypical occupations experienced a significant increase in negative work events over time, with this effect being significantly stronger for men in female-dominated occupations than for women in male-dominated occupations. Furthermore, steeper negative work event trajectories were associated with lower task mastery and higher turnover intentions. These findings advance dissimilarity research by uncovering the dynamic mechanism through which gender dissimilarity translates into adverse adjustment outcomes and contribute to the anchoring events literature by demonstrating the disproportionate and lasting influence of early negative experiences at the very outset of a professional career.

7. When GenAI Increases Inequality: Evidence from a University Debating Competition
Antonio Roldán-Monés
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01063
School of Politics, Economics and Global Affairs (IESPEGA), IE University, 28046 Madrid, Spain
Abstract:
Research on Generative AI (GenAI) and work reports both productivity-compressing and inequality-expanding effects, leaving open a central question for organizations: Who benefits from GenAI and on which specific tasks? I address this question through a randomized controlled trial in competitive university debating, where participants are randomly assigned access to GenAI. Although most previous experimental work examines the impact of GenAI on written activities and across individuals, this setting allows me to study the impact of GenAI on a knowledge-intensive spoken activity and to identify heterogeneous effects across subtasks. I find that GenAI expands performance inequality in this setting. Consistent with recent theories on GenAI and work, results show that high-ability participants experience significantly larger gains from GenAI usage than their lower-ability counterparts in judgment-intensive subtasks, whereas lower-ability individuals only improve their performance in less expert components of debating, where GenAI can be used as a substitute. If these inequality-expanding effects generalize to similar spoken settings where complex ideas must be internalized and explained, rather than simply written, they would have important implications for understanding the impact of GenAI on work and inequality. This paper was accepted by Maria Guadalupe, business strategy. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.01063 .
8. Scalable Just-in-Time Price Elasticity Estimation
Jingbo Wang; Yufeng Huang
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.04791
Department of Marketing, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong; Simon Business School, University of Rochester, Rochester, New York 14627
Abstract:
This paper presents a scalable, nonparametric approach to estimating point-wise price elasticities designed for real-time pricing decisions. A key challenge in these settings is correcting for price endogeneity while remaining computationally efficient. We address this by leveraging nonparametric control functions to decompose the identification of causal elasticities into a sequence of scalable intermediate prediction tasks. We implement this framework with bootstrap averaged (bagged) nearest neighbors, which admits a closed-form representation that enables scalable, real-time implementation. We establish uniform consistency of our estimator and show in Monte Carlo simulations that it robustly recovers local elasticities across a diverse range of data-generating processes. We demonstrate the method’s practical usefulness by applying it to the New York City ride-sharing market, recovering substantial spatial-temporal heterogeneity in price elasticity and updating citywide elasticity estimates in less than one minute, which provide a “just-in-time” input for algorithmic pricing systems. This paper was accepted by Raphael Thomadsen, marketing. Funding: J. Abstract:

10. Machine Learning Vast Dynamic Conditional Covariance Matrices: The Spirit of “Divide and Conquer”
Zhoufan Zhu; Ke Zhu
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08930
Wang Yanan Institute for Studies in Economics, Xiamen University, Xiamen 361005, China; and Department of Finance, School of Economics, Xiamen University, Xiamen 361005, China; Department of Statistics and Actuarial Science, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong
Abstract:
Large-scale portfolio selection requires accurate forecasts of high-dimensional dynamic conditional covariance matrices (CCMs). This task is challenging because the number of covariance elements grows quickly with the number of assets, whereas the available time-series observations are limited and noisy. In this paper, we propose a novel graph-based quantiled (GRAND) CCM estimator in the spirit of “divide and conquer.” By extracting information from the extensive asset features, the GRAND method leverages a new graph-based deep learning network, called the self-grouping autoencoder (SGA), to learn the conditional quantiles of returns. Using those learned conditional quantiles, the GRAND method further learns (co)variances of CCM element-wisely via the quantiled conditional moment (QCM) method, and finally, it makes the resulting assembled CCM estimator positive semidefinite through a projection. Because of the use of the SGA network and the QCM method in an element-wise way, the GRAND method is blessing dimensionality in learning the CCM of a large-scale portfolio, while keeping the computation manageable and maintaining causal semantics. From an extensive empirical analysis of the Chinese stock market, we show that the GRAND method can produce much better tangency portfolios than its competitors, and provide dynamic causal graphs to study variance and quantile connectedness of all stocks for interpreting systemic risk and forecasting the business cycle. This paper was accepted by Kay Giesecke, finance. 11. Democratic Organizing and Contemporary Managerial Talent Markets: Internal Promotion or External Hiring?
Aleksandra Gregoric; Takao Kato; Casper B. L. Larsen
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.19538
Copenhagen Business School, Department of Accounting, Center for Corporate Governance and Owner-Managed Firms, Frederiksberg, Denmark; Colgate University, Department of Economics, Hamilton, New York, United States; Copenhagen Business School, Department of Accounting, Center for Corporate Governance, Frederiksberg, Capital Region, Denmark
Abstract:
We develop and empirically examine a theoretical framework explaining the relationship between democratic organizing and firms’ propensity to staff management positions through internal promotion rather than external hiring. Conventionally, the advantages of promoting through internal labor markets (ILM) have rested on the assumption that workers value intra-firm careers and anticipate remaining with the same employer for a longer term. These advantages may prove harder to attain in contemporary labor markets, given that declining tenure and workers’ growing preference for inter-firm careers possibly reduce the information advantage of ILM and undermine internal candidate retention. We propose that firms with more democratic organizing are less susceptible to these ILM challenges, enjoy higher benefits from recruiting internally and are thus more likely to fill management positions through internal promotion. Using longitudinal employer-employee linked data for Danish firms from 2001 to 2017, we find that firms with worker board representation (WBR), one form of more democratic organizing, are more likely than firms without WBR to fill management positions through internal promotion rather than external hiring. This association is stronger where (i) worker representatives have broader intra-firm experience and are more likely to possess promotion-relevant information; (ii) more managers and professionals have peers serving as worker representatives on the board. These results are consistent with a higher economic viability of internal promotion in firms with more democratic organizing, and largely inconsistent with the conventional account of powerful workers distorting firms’ hiring choices. Interviews with worker directors and human resource professionals illustrate the proposed mechanisms.
12. XBRL adoption and SEC monitoring
Minjae Koo; Yinqi Zhang; Meiling Zhao; Yuping Zhao
Review of Accounting Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-26 | DOI 10.1007/s11142-026-10003-2
Abstract:
Abstract We examine how disclosure processing costs influence SEC monitoring of firm disclosures by leveraging the mandatory adoption of XBRL—a digital reporting language designed to reduce these costs. Post adoption, both the frequency and quality of SEC comment letters for 10-K filings increase. Additional tests suggest that XBRL enhances SEC monitoring through three channels. First, automated data extraction improves review efficiency, as reflected in more timely comment letters and greater XBRL benefits when the SEC faces tighter resource constraints or uses XBRL analytical tools. Second, standardized reporting strengthens the SEC’s benchmarking ability: firms using more standardized tags and tags more comparable to those of industry peers benefit more. Third, XBRL facilitates SEC scrutiny of complex reporting issues, yielding more frequent and higher-quality comments on noncore earnings accounts and for firms with more footnote tags or complex filings. Overall, our evidence suggests that reduced disclosure processing costs enhance the SEC’s regulatory monitoring.

15. Parsimonious theorizing
Diego Jannace; Saeid Kazemi; Arnaldo Camuffo
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70071
Entrepreneurship and Family Enterprise INSEAD Singapore Singapore; ION Management Science Lab‐SDA Bocconi Bocconi University Milan Italy; Department of Economics and Decision Sciences HEC Paris Paris France; Department of Management and Technology Bocconi University Milan Italy
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Entrepreneurs act on theories of value, and the theory‐based view favors simplicity. Yet a theory can be too thin to guide action or too heavy to carry, and simplicity cannot tell which. We model a theory as bundles linking attempted actions, uncertain implementation states, and external conditions to a focal outcome, and make its structure a choice variable. Parsimony then calls for optimal rather than minimal complexity: structure whose contribution to the value of acting justifies its carrying cost. Under the model's assumptions, optimal complexity rises with value at stake, representational capacity, and domain knowledge, and falls with bundle overlap. Theories also change. The entrepreneur's theory shapes her awareness, so some bundles are worth carrying for what they open, while commitments make revision path dependent. Managerial Summary Entrepreneurs often need to explain a future that does not yet exist: what they will try, what must work, and why customers, partners, or investors should care. We show how to weigh the value of linked actions, their possible results, and outside conditions against the effort of understanding, communicating, and testing them. More detail is worthwhile when the potential payoff is greater, the entrepreneur knows the business better, or the team can handle complexity; overlapping ideas add less. As evidence accumulates, the theory may need to expand, shrink, or be rebuilt. Some ideas are worth keeping because they reveal new possibilities. Earlier investments can make changes costly, so a failed test may call for reshaping the idea rather than abandoning it.

17. Moral Licensing Revisited: CSR and Cybersecurity‐related Disclosure After COP21
Vu Quang Trinh; Teng Li; Marwa Elnahass; Ngan Duong Cao
British Journal of Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1111/1467-8551.70128
Newcastle University Business School, Newcastle University Newcastle upon Tyne NE1 7RU UK; Essex Business School University of Essex Colchester CO4 3SQ UK; Department of Accounting and Finance King Fahd University of Petroleum &amp; Minerals Dhahran Saudi Arabia; Interdisciplinary Research Center for Hydrogen Technologies and Carbon Management King Fahd University of Petroleum &amp; Minerals Dhahran Saudi Arabia; University of Bath School of Management Bath BA2 7AY UK
Abstract:
Abstract Using a sample of U.S. S&P 100 firms, we examine the relationship between firms’ CSR and cybersecurity‐related disclosure, exploiting the 2015 Paris Climate Conference (COP21) as an institutional shock in a difference‐in‐differences design. We find that, prior to COP21, firms’ aggregated environmental and social (E&S) profiles, and environmental (E) profiles are negatively but not significantly associated with cybersecurity‐related disclosure, whereas social (S) profiles exhibit a significantly negative association. Drawing on moral licensing theory, we argue that firm leaders who perceive their firms as having accumulated sufficient moral capital through CSR engagement may feel less compelled to respond proactively to emerging stakeholder expectations in non‐traditional responsibility domains such as cybersecurity. Following COP21, however, firms with stronger aggregated E&S, E, and S profiles increase their cybersecurity‐related disclosure. We interpret COP21 as an institutional inflection point that intensified scrutiny and strengthened evaluative standards surrounding corporate sustainability, thereby diminishing the protective value of previously accrued moral credentials. Under heightened accountability, firm leaders appear more inclined to reaffirm a responsible organizational identity through greater transparency and responsiveness to stakeholder information demands. Overall, our evidence suggests that greater cybersecurity‐related disclosure may help firms signal their commitment to cybersecurity and reinforce their socially legitimate identity.
18. The practical logic of professional aspiration among young Chinese actors
Jinzhang Zhou
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261492924
Capital Normal University, Beijing, China
Abstract:
Amid China’s economic transformation and the expansion of digital platform capitalism, young actors increasingly navigate conditions of precarity. Existing research often focuses on structural issues like economic hardship or critiques of platform capital, overlooking how these practitioners form their identities and find meaning. Drawing on theories of precarity and aspiration, this article uses qualitative data to reveal a practical logic of professional aspiration articulated through three concepts: ‘ zhi ’ (oriented persistence), ‘ shi ’ (situational momentum) and ‘ bian ’ (co-creative transformation). This framework allows actors to navigate highly unstable labour markets and construct meaningful work–life structures. The study contributes both empirically and theoretically: first, it offers an analytical perspective rooted in Chinese practical wisdom for understanding the subjectivity and agency of workers in precarious employment; second, it enriches precarity studies by demonstrating how the structural conditions of work can be reconciled with the complexity and diversity of lived experience.
19. Sorting inequality: The dynamics of occupational segregation and the Black–White earnings gap in Canada
Antong Hou; Rupa Banerjee; Senanu Kwasi Kutor
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261484735
Faculty of Arts and Sciences, Harvard College, Cambridge, MA, USA; Ted Rogers School of Management, Toronto Metropolitan University, ON, Canada
Abstract:
Wage inequality between Black and White workers has remained persistent in recent decades across Western countries. This study examines how changes in occupational sorting and within-occupation wage disparities have contributed to the Black–White earnings gap over the past two decades in Canada. Using Social Dominance Theory as a framework, the analysis reveals that the wage gap linked to occupational sorting increased in the private sector for both Black men and women and increased in the public sector for Black men. The within-occupation wage gap also increased in the private sector for Black men. These findings suggest that efforts to reduce Black workers’ wage disparities have achieved only moderate success, primarily in narrowing within-occupation wage gaps, particularly for Black women in the public sector. The study highlights the limitations of current employment equity legislation in addressing structural and systemic inequalities, particularly those that sustain hierarchy-enhancing mechanisms such as differential occupational sorting.
20. Desired and undesired identities: A study of Irish peacekeepers
Angela McGinn; Andrew D. Brown
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261497981
Department of Adult and Community Education, Maynooth University, Ireland; School of Management, University of Bath, UK
Abstract:
Based on an interview study of 48 Irish Defence Forces officers, this article analyses how peacekeepers constructed both positively valenced desired and negatively valenced undesired occupational identities. It demonstrates how interviewees drew on their ‘Irishness’ – their shared understandings of their history, pragmatism and character – as orienting cues to construct desired identities as neutral, flexible and empathetic, and undesired identities as lacking confidence, rule-bending and needing to be liked. This study makes two principal sets of contributions. First, it argues that desired and undesired identities are constructed in relation to each other and are mutually constitutive. Second, it theorises officers’ work on desired identities as a project of moralisation that was challenged by their construction of undesired identities.
21. Agency, care and constraint: Maternal self-employment and the reframing of the adaptive woman
Lauren Bari; Siva Shaangari Seathu Raman
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-25 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261497986
Department of Work and Employment Studies, University of Limerick, Ireland; School of Healthcare Management, Royal College of Surgeons, Dublin, Ireland
Abstract:
Recent years have seen growth in freelance and solo self-employment among women, particularly those with children, reflecting changing labour market conditions and the persistent demands of care. Drawing on interviews with self-employed mothers, this paper examines solo self-employment as a distinctive site of gendered adaptation and revisits Hakim’s concept of the adaptive woman in contemporary labour markets. Across social positions, participants described similar experiences of the mental load, gendered expectations and continual negotiation of work around caregiving. Yet rather than renouncing ambition, participants described continually seeking ways to integrate meaningful work with maternal care. The paper therefore extends Hakim’s concept by reframing adaptation not as a stable preference defined by compromise but as an enduring social process through which women organise productive work around care.

Abstract This article reconceptualizes the formation of territory by examining early modern Japan, showing that territory can emerge through bottom-up processes without centralization. Contrary to dominant narratives based on European history that portray territory as a product of top-down projects, the Japanese case reveals that multiple levels of political actors—the Tokugawa shogunate, individual domains, and village communities—held competing spatial ideologies and engaged in struggles over how space should be organized and governed, and as a result, territory emerged without centralization.
