  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT We explore whether bank regulatory oversight affects the ability of environmental enforcement to reduce industrial emissions. A major objective of environmental enforcement is to reduce industrial emissions by encouraging firms to employ greener production processes and technologies. However, such changes often require external financing obtained from banks, and prior research shows that regulatory oversight of banks can affect bank lending. We find that, on average, the reduction in air pollution in response to county‐level environmental enforcement is attenuated in counties exposed to increased bank regulatory oversight, as measured by severe enforcement actions.     To better understand how prosocial behavior can be effectively encouraged, we study four unresolved issues: whether small rewards also motivate prosocial behavior, how short-term effects differ from longer-run behavioral adjustments, whether incentives induce substitution away from other prosocial activities, and whether reminders about existing rewards meaningfully influence behavior.   - Affiliations: SKEMA Business School, Université Côte d’Azur, 92150 Paris, France
  - Published: 2026-10-12
  - Crossref journal: Operations Research
  - Abstract:
    Why Larger Service Systems Benefit More from Scale How much extra demand can a service system handle when capacity is increased while keeping the same target level of customer blocking? In the paper “Convexity of Inverse Blocking Curves in Loss Systems,” Benjamin Legros establishes an exact economies-of-scale result for loss systems. The paper proves that the maximum supportable demand is an increasing and globally convex function of the number of servers. In practical terms, as a system becomes larger, each additional server can support progressively more additional demand while maintaining the same blocking target. Equivalently, the extra capacity required to accommodate additional demand decreases as the system grows. The result provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for resource pooling in capacity-constrained services. It applies to the classical Erlang-B loss model and extends to systems with a finite waiting room, with potential relevance for telecommunications, healthcare, emergency response, and other service operations.

  - Published: 2026-10-13
  - Crossref journal: Econometric Theory
  - Abstract:
    A standard assumption in the Bayesian estimation of linear regression models is that the regressors are exogenous in the sense that they are uncorrelated with the model error term. In practice, however, this assumption can be invalid. In this article, using the exponentially tilted empirical likelihood (ETEL) framework, we develop a Bayes factor test for endogeneity that compares a base model that is correctly specified under exogeneity but misspecified under endogeneity against an extended model that is correctly specified in either case. We provide a comprehensive study of the log-marginal ETEL. The methods are illustrated with simulated data and problems concerning the causal effect of automobile prices on automobile demand and the causal effect of potentially endogenous airplane ticket prices on passenger volume.

- [Enterprise Architecture Threat Modeling for Cybersecurity Risk Management](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.70377)
  - DOI: 10.1111/risa.70377
  - Authors: Branko S. Bokan; Joost Santos
  - Affiliations: The George Washington University Washington District of Columbia USA
  - Published: 2026-10-12
  - Crossref journal: Risk Analysis
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Organizations invest heavily in cybersecurity capabilities, yet the relationship between deployed defensive portfolios, practitioner judgments of coverage, and empirically observed adversary behavior remains difficult to assess. This study examines that relationship by integrating practitioner elicitation with threat‐informed cybersecurity architecture analysis. A model‐derived analytical baseline was constructed using the Cybersecurity Architecture Review (CAR) methodology aligned with MITRE ATT&CK version 17, quantifying protect, detect, and respond coverage for 12 commonly deployed cybersecurity capability categories. Survey data from 65 cybersecurity practitioners were used to characterize capability portfolios, perceived coverage, and factors influencing capability‐selection decisions. Results show uneven modeled coverage across the ATT&CK lifecycle and substantial functional overlap among capability categories. Across 910 respondent‐tactic observations, practitioner‐estimated coverage exceeded corresponding respondent‐specific modeled coverage by approximately 27 percentage points on average, although the magnitude and direction of perception‐model divergence varied by tactic. Most subgroup comparisons by professional role, responsibility, and experience did not identify statistically significant differences, while government respondents exhibited slightly higher mean divergence than nongovernment respondents in one pairwise comparison. Capability‐selection decisions reflected multiple organizational and external influences, with formal internal threat modeling reported less frequently than peer practices, regulatory or compliance requirements, independent research, and analyst or consultant reports. The findings demonstrate how threat‐informed architecture analysis can complement practitioner judgment by making modeled coverage, gaps, overlap, and divergence explicit within organizational cybersecurity risk governance.
