1. The Anti‐SLAPP Statutes and Corporate Environmental Policies
KEVIN C.W. CHEN; ZHIHONG CHEN; CHENGZHU SUN
Journal of Accounting Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/1475-679x.70095
Hong Kong University of Science and Technology; Hong Kong Polytechnic University
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We examine whether legal protection for civic participation affects corporate pollution. Polluters may deter public discipline through strategic lawsuits. Anti‐SLAPP (Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation) statutes mitigate this threat by enabling prompt dismissal and recovery of legal costs. Using a difference‐in‐differences design, we find that enacting an anti‐SLAPP statute in a state significantly reduces toxic emissions from that state by increasing local public pressure. The reduction is greater when the statutes provide stronger protection, when local stakeholders have stronger incentives and abilities to discipline polluters, and when local public pressure increases more after the enactment. Plants lower emissions by improving abatement rather than by reducing output or relocating emissions. Our paper underscores the role of legal protection in environmental governance and informs policy debates over anti‐SLAPP legislation.

10. Digging Into the Subsoil: Considering and Theorizing Human Services Delivery at the Sub‐Micro‐Level of the Public Service Ecosystem
Kate Broadhurst; Steven Parker
Public Administration Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1111/puar.70234
Loughborough Business School Loughborough University Loughborough UK; Department for Public Leadership and Social Enterprise Open University Milton Keynes UK
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Public services are often delivered in partnership with families and communities, yet these relationships receive limited attention within public service ecosystem (PSE) theory. Responding to call to develop the sub‐micro‐level of the PSE, we introduce the concept of subsoil sensitivity to foreground the family and community ecosystems that underpin public service value creation. These informal systems are active sites of value creation and destruction with sensitivities arising from their alignment or misalignment with public services. We propose legislative and policy nodes as connectors that translate macrolevel policy into microlevel practice. Drawing on child protection vignettes, we show how nodes create the conditions for interaction, while subsoil dynamics influence whether value is cocreated or codestroyed amid power asymmetries and differing belief systems. The paper extends the sub‐micro‐level of the PSE and offers a framework for examining family and community ecosystems in public service delivery.
11. Agricultural Trade and Industrial Development
Angus C. Chu; Yuichi Furukawa; Pietro F. Peretto; Rongxin Xu
International Economic Review
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/iere.70123
Department of Economics University of Macau Macau China; Faculty of Economics Chuo University Tokyo Japan; Department of Economics Duke University Durham North Carolina USA; School of Economics Huazhong University of Science and Technology Wuhan China
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Is agricultural productivity conducive to economic development? We develop a two‐country open‐economy Schumpeterian growth model with endogenous takeoff. With both agricultural trade and a subsistence requirement, higher domestic agricultural productivity has ambiguous effects on the economy's takeoff and its transitional growth rate if domestic and imported agricultural goods are substitutes. Without the subsistence requirement, higher domestic agricultural productivity delays industrialization and lowers transitional growth by increasing domestic demand for agricultural labor. This specialization force works in the opposite direction of the change in domestic consumption pattern governed by the subsistence requirement, which tends to release labor from agriculture. Without agricultural trade, the specialization force is absent and the subsistence requirement on agricultural consumption implies that higher domestic agricultural productivity reallocates labor from agriculture to industry, hastening industrialization and raising transitional growth. Using cross‐country panel data, we find that agricultural productivity has a positive effect on economic growth but this positive effect weakens and even becomes negative when reliance on agricultural imports is sufficiently high. Simulating the calibrated model, we find that improvement in domestic agricultural productivity accounts for about 30% and 15% of the changes in TFP growth in China and Japan, respectively, and more so for their main trading partner, the United States.

15. The Political Economy of Propaganda: Evidence from US Newspapers,
Sebastian Ottinger; Max Posch
Journal of the European Economic Association
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1093/jeea/jvag069
CERGE-EI ,; University of Exeter ,
Abstract:
Abstract This study examines whether racial outrage in the media can result from the incentives of elites who seek to divide society for political gain, focusing on the late 19th century Southern United States. At this time, the Democratic Party had strong political incentives to incite racial outrage, and local Democratic elites exercised considerable influence over newspapers, the only mass media at the time. Using high-frequency historical newspaper data and difference-in-differences and triple-difference estimation strategies, we find evidence consistent with Democratic elites orchestrating a racist propaganda campaign to sway white voters. Supporting analyses are consistent with Democrats benefiting from anti-Black propaganda in the subsequent elections.
16. Nonparametric modelling of continuous-time Markov chains with scalable exact and approximate gradients
Filippo Monti; Xiang Ji; Yucai Shao; Marc A Suchard
Journal of the Royal Statistical Society. Series B: Statistical Methodology
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1093/jrsssb/qkag138
Department of Biostatistics, University of California Los Angeles , Los Angeles, CA ,; Department of Statistics, Iowa State University , Ames, IA ,; Department of Human Genetics, University of California Los Angeles , Los Angeles, CA ,
Abstract:
Abstract Inferring the infinitesimal rates of continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) is a central challenge in many scientific domains. This task is hindered by 3 factors: quadratic growth in the number of rates as the CTMC state space expands, strong dependencies among rates, and incomplete information for many transitions. We introduce a new Bayesian framework that flexibly models CTMC rates by incorporating covariates through Gaussian processes. This approach improves inference by integrating new information and deepens our understanding of CTMC stochastic behaviour by shedding light on potential external drivers. Unlike previous approaches limited to linear covariate effects, our method captures complex nonlinear relationships, enabling fuller use of covariate information and more accurate characterization of their influence. For efficient posterior inference, we employ Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. To avoid the prohibitive cost of repeated matrix-exponential derivatives under existing approaches, we develop 2 scalable strategies for exact and approximate gradient computation. Given N observations and K CTMC states, these strategies reduce the cost from O(NK3), with a substantial constant, to O(K3+NK2), with cheaper constants. We demonstrate the method in Bayesian phylogenetic and phylogeographic inference, showing strong performance on synthetic and real datasets and empirical quadratic scaling in K, even when N&lt;K.
17. Learning‐by‐twinning: Organizational digital twins and the future of search
Prithwiraj (Raj) Choudhury; Yingxin Du; Megan Lawrence
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70061
London School of Economics and Political Science London UK; Indiana University Bloomington Indiana USA
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Entrepreneurs and managers often need to evaluate strategic choices, such as redesigning an incentive plan or entering an unfamiliar market, before committing scarce resources. Existing offline search tools, including mental models, analogical reasoning, model building, and simulation, allow managers to weigh alternatives without acting, but each is limited by what a single decision maker can specify, recall, or imagine. We argue that advances in generative AI, combined with the growing availability of firm‐specific digital trace data, are transforming this tradeoff. We introduce the organizational digital twin (ODT), an infrastructure combining firm‐specific data, AI‐based behavioral simulation models, and governance rules which facilitates a new form of offline search that we call learning‐by‐twinning: search conducted by observing how a strategic choice unfolds within a digital replica of the firm. We identify three mechanisms through which learning‐by‐twinning creates value, namely expanded search, safer experimentation, and richer system‐level feedback as well as specify certain conditions under which these benefits may attenuate. Managerial Summary Strategic entrepreneurship depends on discovering and refining opportunities through search and experimentation under uncertainty. Enabled by advances in generative artificial intelligence and organizational data infrastructures, we theorize that a new form of entrepreneurial search—learning‐by‐twinning—allows managers to conduct systematic virtual experimentation prior to real‐world implementation. We introduce the concept of the organizational digital twin (ODT) as the technological infrastructure that makes this search and experimentation possible: an evolving simulation environment built from firm‐specific digital trace data and multi‐agent AI models capable of reproducing organizational routines, interactions, and decision processes. We argue that learning‐by‐twinning alters the traditional tradeoffs of offline search by expanding the set of alternatives managers can explore, enabling low‐risk experimentation, and revealing second‐order organizational consequences before strategic commitments are made.

19. Are the Hedges of Funds Green?
Huan Kuang; Bing Liang; Mila Getmansky Sherman; Tianyi Qu
Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1017/s0022109026103457
Bryant University College of Business; University of Massachusetts Amherst Isenberg School of Management Finance; Baylor University Finance Department
Abstract:
Abstract Over the past decade, hedge funds have increasingly embraced green investing. We develop a novel return-based measure of fund greenness and find that greener funds earn higher average returns, consistent with capturing a positive green premium, and are associated with lower risks, especially during adverse periods. We further show that performance gains are greatest among directional funds, whereas risk reduction is most pronounced among nondirectional funds. We also provide evidence that managers of these funds exhibit superior green investment skills through green stock selection and green factor timing. Finally, investors reward greener hedge funds only when they demonstrate strong performance.
20. Co‐Constructing the Illusion of Progression: How Boundary Blurring and Affirming Reproduce Social Exclusion in Elite Professions
Stefanie Gustafsson; Laura Empson
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70184
University of Bath; City University London; Copenhagen Business School
Abstract:
Abstract Social exclusion is a persistent problem in elite professions. While scholarship has emphasized structural and cultural barriers, less is known about the relational practices and mechanisms through which social exclusion is sustained. Drawing on interviews with 84 lawyers, paralegals, and managers in elite UK law firms, we develop a conceptual model of how the blurring and affirming of social and symbolic boundaries between higher‐status and lower‐status groups reproduces social exclusion. We show how both groups blur and affirm boundaries across three domains: professional work, professional space, and systems of professional advancement. Boundary blurring makes symbolic boundaries more permeable, positioning lower‐status actors as prospective professionals, while boundary affirming maintains social boundaries around professional membership. We identify and theorize the illusion of progression as a relational mechanism emerging from intertwined blurring and affirming, constructing lower‐status roles as pathways into the profession even though progression is rare. The illusion perpetuates liminality by sustaining hope, individualizing failure and undermining lower‐status groups' collective challenge to social exclusion. Our findings advance understanding of the relational mechanism through which social exclusion persists in elite professions, show how boundary blurring can sustain rather than threaten status distinctions, and help explain why organizational diversity and inclusion efforts have produced little substantive change.
21. The Ubiquity of Business Groups around the World: A Property‐Rights Theory
Ruth V. Aguilera; Rafel Crespi‐Cladera; Alfredo Martín‐Oliver; Bartolomé Pascual‐Fuster
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70179
Northeastern University; Universitat de les Illes Balears
Abstract:
Abstract Business groups (BGs) are collections of legally independent firms connected by ownership ties and are widely present in both developing and developed economies. We develop property‐rights theory (PRT) to explain this prevalence, extending its scope from the allocation of rights over a bundle of assets to the legal partitioning of assets as another allocative mechanism of property rights. Our conceptual framework complements traditional theories understanding BGs primarily as responses to institutional voids. Drawing on a large global dataset of firms, we examine how firm asset specificity and ownership sharing influence the controlling owners' decision to organize their economic activities as a BG or as a standalone firm. We find that the likelihood of adopting a BG organizational form over a standalone entity increases when (1) controlling owners rely on more firm‐specific assets, and (2) ownership is shared or potentially shared. Moreover, our findings are robust across three commonly observed firm strategic configurations: focused, vertically integrated, and diversified. Our study expands the PRT by developing a theory of property‐rights architecture, moving forward from the institutional voids perspective to explain BGs as efficient governance responses to asset and ownership complexities, hence contributing also to the strategic management and international business literatures.
22. Does the transition to self-employment benefit mental health? Evidence from the UK
Liming Li; Karen Glaser; Ben Geiger
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261475931
Department of Global Health &amp; Social Medicine, King’s College London, UK
Abstract:
There is limited research evidence on the mental health consequences of self-employment transitions from wage employment. This study fills this gap by investigating this issue using a difference-in-differences design with matching methods employing data from the UK Household Longitudinal Study (2009–2021). The results suggest that transitions to self-employment are associated with a small but significant decrease in psychological distress, signalling improvements in mental health. These effects are stronger among workers with poorer initial mental health when compared to their non-self-employed counterparts. Along with the self-employment transitions, workers also experience declines in socio-economic status but improvements in job autonomy, job satisfaction and satisfaction with leisure time. Overall, these findings highlight the potential protective effects of self-employment on workers’ mental health; however, caution is needed in considering the mental health premium of self-employment transitions.
23. From a distance: How the social organisation of labour conditions personal attachment to work
Anne Gravelle Antoni; Hamza Asshidi; Janna Rose; Nathalie Louisgrand
Work, Employment and Society
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1177/09500170261469828
Grenoble Ecole de Management, Grenoble, France; Université Grenoble Alpes, France
Abstract:
Employees are more often than not personally invested in their work. Such affective investment has been both praised as a potential source of self-fulfilment and condemned as a form of labour extraction. However, we aim to examine how employees in white-collar jobs navigate the multiple work and labour facets of their employment. Using the COVID-19 pandemic as a revelatory context, with the unexpected collapse of work-life articulations, we draw upon 50 semi-structured interviews of employees in a French private organisation of higher education. We show how individuals interrogated their investment in their employment as labour, yet not into their work as subjective endeavour. Moreover, we analyse how the material and social context of the social organisation of labour conditions individuals’ personal attachment to work. Finally, we discuss the struggle for a meaningful experience of working in higher education after decades of new public management.

25. How does a complementor develop capabilities in a platform-based outsourcing ecosystem
Nan Zou; Chong Ma; Ilan Oshri; Ji-Ye Mao
Journal of Information Technology
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1177/02683962261501973
University of International Business and Economics; University of Auckland; ShanghaiTech University
Abstract:
There is ongoing interest in understanding how capabilities are developed by various actors in platform ecosystems. While much of the existing research has focused on the capabilities of platform owners, we know relatively little about how complementors build the capabilities necessary for their participation in ecosystems. In this paper, we argue that some platform ecosystems involve outsourcing contractual relationships between platform owners, complementors, and clients. This introduces a distinct setting—platform-based outsourcing ecosystems—which invites insights from the IS outsourcing literature. Traditionally, the IS outsourcing literature has assumed that vendors develop capabilities primarily through experiential learning in dyadic and mediated settings, with evidence for learning between vendors in outsourcing ecosystems. Building on this foundation, we conducted a case study of a complementor operating on the AliCloud platform to examine how capability development unfolds in such ecosystems. Our analysis reveals a learning process shaped by changing contractual relationships: the complementor begins with vicarious learning under subcontracting from the platform owner, transitions to experiential learning under direct contracting with clients, and finally adopts reciprocal experiential learning as a sub-platform owner working with other complementors. In each phase, the complementor assumes a distinct role that reflects its contractual relationships with other actors in the ecosystem. We theorise this learning sequence in a process model that explains how shifts in contractual positioning trigger corresponding shifts in learning mechanisms and capability formation. The paper offers implications for both the IS outsourcing and platform ecosystem literatures, highlighting how complementor roles and learning approaches evolve in response to contractual dynamics.

30. Rethinking the Anatomy of Passion: A Biopsychosocial Configurational Perspective on Antecedents of Work and Entrepreneurial Passion
Olivia Aronson; Marina Astakhova; Alexander Hamrick; Charles Murnieks; Henry Switz
Journal of Organizational Behavior
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1002/job.70133
Robins School of Business University of Richmond Richmond Virginia USA; Bloch School of Management University of Missouri‐Kansas City Kansas City Missouri USA; University of Richmond Richmond Virginia USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Despite over two decades of passion research, our understanding of how passion is construed remains fragmented and dominated by additive approaches that treat antecedents as isolated predictors (both theoretically and empirically). Drawing on the biopsychosocial (BPS) framework, we challenge prior research by reconceptualizing passion as a configurational phenomenon emerging through interdependent BPS conditions which combine in complex ways to form equifinal pathways to passion. In doing so, we comparatively analyze configurations associated with harmonious and obsessive passion across the two most frequently studied domains in the management literature: work and entrepreneurship. To empirically examine this configurational perspective, we employ fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), which is well equipped to test the equifinal, compensatory, and interdependent nature of configural constructs. Using two time‐lagged samples of employees and entrepreneurs, we identify multiple distinct configurations that not only differentiate harmonious from obsessive passion but also distinguish passion across the work and entrepreneurial domains. Our results demonstrate that passion is largely domain‐contingent, such that work and entrepreneurial passion are each associated with mostly unique BPS configurations rather than universally operating antecedents. Our study advances passion research by shifting attention from isolated net effects toward a holistic view of passion as a system of interdependent BPS predictors.

33. Urban densification to support climate adaptation: balancing costs and agglomeration benefits in the Netherlands
Yashvant R Premchand; Peter Mulder
Journal of Economic Geography
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1093/jeg/lbag077
Department of Spatial Economics, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam , De Boelelaan 1105 , Amsterdam 1081 HV,; TNO , Anna van Buerenplein 1 , The Hague 2595 DA,; Copernicus Institute for Sustainable Development, Utrecht University , Princetonlaan 8a , Utrecth 3584 CB,
Abstract:
Abstract Where should we build new housing under growing climate hazards? This study develops a framework that balances the economic benefits of density against the geographically varying costs of climate adaptation. We apply it to the Netherlands, where demand for new housing is high, but much of the land lies in floodplains or subsidence-prone areas. Agglomeration benefits are proxied by land values, while adaptation costs are derived from engineering estimates of flood protection and soil subsidence. Combining these data allows us to map spatial trade-offs and identify where development remains welfare-enhancing. Our findings show that dense cities continue to generate strong net welfare gains, even in places with high costs, while low-density settlements generate a welfare loss for new housing. We identify density thresholds above which housing development becomes feasible.
