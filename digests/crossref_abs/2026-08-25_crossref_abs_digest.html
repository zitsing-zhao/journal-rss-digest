Leveling the Playing Field? Generative AI and Entrepreneurial Storytelling
2026-08-23 | DOI: 10.1177/10422587261472523 | Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice
Authors: Karl Taeuscher; Michael Lounsbury
Affiliations: University of Manchester, UK; University of Alberta, Edmonton, AB, Canada
Cultural entrepreneurship research emphasizes the importance of storytelling and other cultural skills, yet recent work suggests that generative artificial intelligence (AI) levels the playing field for entrepreneurs lacking such skills. In this conceptual article, we challenge this view by outlining how the widespread use of AI tools likely leads to systematic changes in the institutional norms and expectations that shape entrepreneurial storytelling. Anchored in evidence-informed assumptions about average entrepreneurs’ use of AI, our theorization suggests that widespread AI adoption will lead to a homogenization in entrepreneurial storytelling—creating an evaluative environment that may require even greater cultural skills to navigate.

Local Shocks and Regional Dynamics in an Aging Economy
2026-08-24 | DOI: 10.1111/iere.70101 | International Economic Review
Authors: Yuta Suzuki
Affiliations: Hitotsubashi University Kunitachi Tokyo Japan
ABSTRACT This paper studies how population aging shapes regional adjustment to local shocks. I develop a dynamic spatial general equilibrium model that incorporates aggregate demographic change, life‐cycle variation in migration and employment choices, and forward‐looking investment in local capital. Applying the model to Japan, I find that aging can mitigate welfare losses from a negative local shock. Although older populations are less mobile, reduced outmigration sustains local capital demand and slows capital disinvestment, limiting declines in labor productivity. An extension illustrates the model's use for post‐disaster policy evaluation, highlighting a potential tradeoff between spatial equity and aggregate welfare.

ABSTRACT We develop a growth model that endogenizes the occupational choice of workers with different ability levels to study how digitalization affects labor income inequality. The framework incorporates digitalization's multifaceted effects on the economy: (i) it influences economic growth through automation, and (ii) it affects labor income inequality through labor productivity (demand side) and labor time modulation (supply side). We examine how these channels influence wage inequality between skilled and unskilled workers, within each skill group, and economy‐wide. The analysis reveals that digitalization's impact on labor income inequality—whether exacerbating or mitigating disparities—depends on the inequality metric employed.
