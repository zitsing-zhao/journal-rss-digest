1. Board–CEO Trust and Concealing Adverse News
Kee-Hong Bae; Sadok El Ghoul; Zhaoran Gong; Omrane Guedhami; Lixin Su
Accounting Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.2308/tar-2024-0298
York University; The University of Texas at El Paso; University of South Carolina
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We investigate how generalized trust, a culturally rooted belief in the trustworthiness of unfamiliar others, influences board oversight. Using an ancestry-based measure of board–CEO trust based on ancestral origins and bilateral trust scores across countries, we find a significant positive relation between board–CEO trust and stock price crash risk, indicating higher bad-news hoarding. This relation remains robust across alternative crash risk measures, sample specifications, and trust measures. Firms with high board–CEO trust exhibit more asymmetric stock market reactions to good news versus bad news and a higher likelihood of material restatements. Analyses of insider trading patterns show that directors in high-trust firms are less informed than CEOs about adverse developments, supporting lax board monitoring rather than collusion between directors and CEOs. Overall, the evidence suggests that culturally inherited trust can weaken board oversight and reduce corporate transparency, consistent with an underexplored governance friction even within well-structured boards. Data Availability: Data used in this study are available from public sources identified in the study. JEL Classifications: G14; G34; G41; M12.

8. Political uncertainty, social legitimacy, and nonmarket strategy
Jonathan Doh
Journal of International Business Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1057/s41267-026-00922-2
Abstract:
Abstract The Decade Award–winning article by Darendeli and Hill (2016) presents a novel yet nuanced approach to the question of whether and how foreign firms can strategically navigate a volatile and unpredictable political environment. Their 2026 retrospective builds on their earlier contribution by showing how their insights have contributed to a number of contemporary IB research streams, including those addressing political risk, political action, and political ties. Going beyond their earlier contribution, in the retrospective, they describe the relational and physical pillars of social legitimacy (Darendeli & Hill, 2026). They then assess the efficacy of their original perspective in an environment characterized by heightened geopolitical conflict and political polarization. Finally, they develop a model that delineates four strategic approaches to legitimacy building in host countries. In this commentary, I offer perspectives that complement and extend their contributions by discussing (1) the nature of political uncertainty and the use of real options in responding to that uncertainty, (2) the literature on substantive versus symbolic CSR and how that might further extend some of their insights, and (3) research regarding when and how multinational enterprises provide public goods and services typically provided by government. I conclude by outlining how Darendeli and Hill’s (2016, 2026) insights have relevance for contemporary practice and policy.

10. The Seeds of State Capture: Merit and Patrimonialism in the Colonial Bureaucracy
SARAH BRIERLEY; NOAH NATHAN; GEORGE KWAKU OFOSU; TINGXUAN ZHU
American Political Science Review
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-06 | DOI 10.1017/s0003055426101993
London School of Economics and Political Science; Massachusetts Institute of Technology
Abstract:
Bureaucracies vary in the extent to which hiring is meritocratic versus patrimonial. Such outcomes are typically seen as the result of politicians’ behavior. However, politicians often delegate hiring to existing bureaucrats. When political leaders are socially distant from communities they seek to govern and these areas are peripheral to politicians’ core interests, bureaucrats’ private preferences can dominate hiring decisions instead. Colonial and other forms of external state-building typify cases where patrimonialism created by bureaucrats, not politicians, can emerge. Digitizing individual-level records across six decades of British rule in Ghana, we find evidence of patrimonialism rather than meritocracy in the hiring of African staff, with large inequities across ethnic groups. Our results demonstrate the underexplored agency of local bureaucrats in state-building and highlight conditions under which bureaucrats’ preferences, rather than only political principals’, shape state institutions. They also challenge conventional wisdom about why, and when, newly-independent African states became “neopatrimonial.”
11. The Influence of Police Unions in Local Elections: Evidence from U.S. Cities’ Spending and Performance
MARIA CARRERI; EDOARDO TESO; RUI YU
American Political Science Review
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-06 | DOI 10.1017/s0003055426101956
Bocconi University; Amazon
Abstract:
Despite growing attention to local policies governing public safety issues, and mounting reports of police union spending to local candidates, the role of police unions in local politics remains understudied. We address this gap by assembling a novel, large-scale dataset on campaign finance in U.S. mayoral elections, covering 2,303 races across 1,073 cities from 2001 to 2019. We document that police unions frequently donate to mayoral candidates—especially in mid-sized and large cities—targeting likely winners and incumbents, and without displaying clear partisan bias. Using a within-city design that accounts for heterogeneous treatment effects in the presence of staggered treatment timing, we find that the election of a police-union-backed mayor leads to an expansion in police department size. However, we do not find improvements in police performance in these cities, suggesting that unions’ political involvement, while providing additional resources, might also be associated with reduced incentives for effort.
12. Using Financial Accounting Information to Estimate the Income Shifting of <scp>US</scp> Multinationals
Scott D. Dyreng; Robert Hills; Kevin S. Markle
Contemporary Accounting Research
ABS 4 | 2026-11-06 | DOI 10.1111/1911-3846.70099
Duke University Durham North Carolina USA; University of Georgia Athens Georgia USA; Michigan State University East Lansing Michigan USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Existing estimates of how much income US multinational corporations (MNCs) shift out of the United States differ dramatically, and most cannot identify which specific firms do the shifting. We use data from firms' public financial reports to derive firm‐year estimates of US MNCs' outbound income shifting. We find that aggregate US outbound income shifting is substantially lower than widely cited macroeconomic‐based estimates, with the gap driven primarily by double counting of foreign income in macroeconomic data rather than by differences in firms' sensitivity to tax rates. Income shifting is also highly concentrated among a small number of firms, primarily in intangible‐intensive industries such as computers and pharmaceuticals, while most MNCs shift little or no income. This concentration implies that size‐based thresholds embedded in the OECD's Pillar 2 and the US corporate alternative minimum tax will capture the most active income shifters but may also impose compliance burdens on many firms that shift minimal income. These findings have direct implications for policy‐makers designing international tax rules, tax authorities prioritizing audit resources toward high‐risk income shifters, and investors and lenders assessing MNCs' tax‐related exposures.

14. ON THE CCE ESTIMATION OF THREE-DIMENSIONAL PANELS WITH MULTI-LEVEL FACTORS
Sainan Jin; Xun Lu; Liangjun Su
Econometric Theory
ABS 4 | 2026-11-06 | DOI 10.1017/s0266466626100735
Tsinghua University; The Chinese University of Hong Kong
Abstract:
Abstract We consider the common correlated effects estimation of a three-dimensional panel data model with heterogeneous slope coefficients and multi-level interactive fixed effects. We study the asymptotic properties of the heterogeneous slope coefficient estimators and the mean group (MG) estimators. We find that bias correction is indispensable for the MG estimator and show that the MG estimators exhibit different convergence rates depending on the degree of heterogeneity in the slope coefficients. We propose a bias correction procedure and inference method that is uniformly valid irrespective of the degree of slope heterogeneity. We conduct Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the performance of our estimators and inference methods in finite samples. We apply our new methods to international trade data and find significant heterogeneity in the gravity model even after we control for the multi-level factors.
15. Fisher Dynamics, Monetary Policy, and Household Indebtedness
ANDREAS FAGERENG; MAGNUS A. H. GULBRANDSEN; MARTIN B. HOLM; GISLE J. NATVIK
Journal of Money, Credit and Banking
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1111/jmcb.70105
Abstract:
Abstract Growth in household debt‐to‐income ratios reflects nominal debt changes or “Fisher dynamics” due to interest income and expenses, real income growth, and inflation. Using two decades of microdata on all Norwegian households, we decompose these channels' roles in how debt‐to‐income evolves over time and in response to monetary policy shocks. On average, debt changes outsize Fisher dynamics, except among highly leveraged households. After interest rate hikes, the channels pull in opposite directions, but the debt changes dominate, so debt‐to‐income ratios decline. This holds across subgroups, including highly indebted households, recent movers, and those facing liquidity constraints or high unemployment risk.
16. Large Funds and Corporate Bond Market Fragility
Yong Chen; Mengqiao Du; Zheng Sun
Review of Finance
ABS 4 | 2026-11-06 | DOI 10.1093/rof/rfag053
Texas A&M University; National University of; University of California , Irvine
Abstract:
Abstract The corporate bond fund industry is characterized by a high concentration of capital, with a substantial portion of assets managed by large funds. We examine the relation between large-fund trading exposure and corporate bond volatility across market conditions. Using a comprehensive sample of U.S. bond funds and corporate bonds, we show that bond return volatility decreases (increases) with trading fund size when market liquidity is high (low). Exploring the underlying mechanisms, we find that large funds are more likely to reach for yield by holding illiquid bonds. Further analysis suggests that while large funds supply liquidity during normal times, their liquidity-supplying activity weakens during market stress. Finally, large funds tend to sell illiquid bonds when facing investor outflows during illiquid markets. Overall, our findings reveal an important yet subtle relation between fund size and bond market fragility, akin to a “dam effect.” JEL Classification: G10, G12, G23

19. The strength of weird ties: positive genetic interactions have greater scientific impact but are under-represented in the literature
Mengyi Sun; David M. McCandlish
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.519
Simons Center for Quantitative Biology, Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, Cold Spring Harbor, NY, 11724, USA
Abstract:
Abstract Genetic interactions, where mutating two genes together affects a trait differently than expected from their individual effects, often reveal how genes work together. When the trait is cell growth, systematic studies show that negative interactions (worse than expected) usually link functionally related genes and are widely considered informative, whereas positive interactions (better than expected) tend to link seemingly unrelated genes and are often deemed less informative. However, the sociology of science suggests that transformative discoveries often arise from unexpected, cross-domain connections. To evaluate these competing perspectives, we linked large-scale yeast interaction data to the literature on each gene pair and its citations. Contrary to prevailing assumptions, papers on positively interacting gene pairs tend to be more highly cited. Yet positive interactions are significantly under-represented in the literature relative to their experimental prevalence. This gap persisted after a technology detecting both types equally well became available, implicating factors beyond mere discoverability. Research building on positive interactions also forms a more interconnected body of work. These findings reveal the underappreciated value of positive interactions. More broadly, they show how science-of-science insights can identify biases in biomedical research and provide concrete guidance on what to study next.

Doctoral Program of Information Systems, Universitas Diponegoro. Imam Bardjo st., Semarang, Central Java 50241, Indonesia; School of Computer Science, Nusa Putra University, Sukabumi, West Java 43152, Indonesia
Abstract:
Abstract Existing studies on the rapid growth of Indonesian journals focus on metrics but lack the in-depth analysis of their publisher characteristics. This study examines Indonesian journal publishers across four databases: Indonesian Digital References (GARUDA), covering most Indonesian journals; Science and Technology Index (SINTA), covering accredited national journals; Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ), covering open-access journals; and Scopus, covering reputable international journals. 21. Stratification and Integration in the Key Research Specialty of Women in Science and Engineering, Over Time
Mary Frank Fox; Gerhard Sonnert; Emma Menardi; Derek McNutt
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.520
Jimmy and Rosalynn Carter School of Public Policy Georgia Institute of Technology Atlanta, Georgia 30332, USA; Center for Astrophysics Harvard University Cambridge, Massachusetts 02138, USA; Department of Sociology Princeton University Princeton, New Jersey 08544, USA
Abstract:
Abstract We examine the features of focal and non-focal groups of authors (measured by thresholds of their publication counts) in the research specialty of women in science and engineering. This specialty is important because of the ways in which it relates to science, gender, and society. The study combines a bibliometric approach with a social-structural perspective. Our research questions focus on the features of the groups of authors–-metrics of levels of stratification and integration-in a consequential 45-year period (1965-2010 for articles, and through 2021 for citations). The study employs a systematic, multi-stage design that finds and verifies 3,174 articles belonging to the specialty out of 23,000 retrieved; and identifies the authors of these articles and their features. The results are contrary to our expectation of this specialty being “exceptional” in relatively low stratification among authors. Findings point to divisions by focal and non-focal groups; substantial inequality in skewed publication productivity, citations, and affiliations; integration among the focal group in co-authorship and clusters; and complexities of stratifiation and integration over time. The study suggests fruitful continuing inquiries and demonstrates the potential of combining a bibliometric approach and social-structural perspectives in understanding authors in this, and other specialties.
22. Open Editors Plus: A Comprehensive Dataset of Editorial Board Composition Across 15,000 Academic Journals
Basile Chretien
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.521
Neuropresage Team (PhIND, UMR-S U1237), Normandie Université, UNICAEN, INSERM, GIP Cyceron, Caen, France
Abstract:
Abstract Editorial boards serve as central gatekeepers in scholarly publishing, yet comprehensive, enriched data on their composition has remained scarce. We introduce Open Editors Plus, a dataset of 922,097 editorial board positions across 15,168 journals from 48 publishers, covering 745,125 unique editors in 189 countries. Each record is enriched with algorithmically inferred gender, institutional geolocation via the Research Organization Registry, bibliometric indicators from OpenAlex, and journal indexing status across five major databases. Among editors with gender-resolvable names (primarily European-origin; 18% of records are unresolved; 82% resolved), 33% are women, with Editors-in-Chief at 24.9%. Geographic concentration is extreme (Gini = 0.90); the top 10 countries hold 71% of positions. One in four editors serves on multiple boards, and multi-board editors have substantially higher h-indices. The dataset is released under CC0 on Zenodo (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21620278) with an interactive explorer at openeditors-plus.org.
23. Investigating individual writing style as a contributor to gender gaps in science and technology
Kara Kedrick; Ekaterina Levitskaya; Russell J. Funk
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.512
Institute for Complex Social Dynamics, Carnegie Mellon University; Coleridge Initiative; Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota
Abstract:
Abstract Gender gaps in how scientific work is evaluated are well documented, but their sources remain debated. We ask whether an overlooked factor—the linguistic style of the writing itself—is gendered and consequential. Drawing on a framework that distinguishes informational features (which emphasize facts) from involved features (which emphasize relationships), we analyze single-authored abstracts of academic papers and patents across all fields of science and technology. Women’s writing is systematically more involved than men’s—richer in relational, audience-oriented features and higher in the balance of involved to informational language—a difference that holds across scientific fields, in collaborative and single-authored work, and throughout the full text of a large open-access biomedical corpus, not only their abstracts. This stylistic signature also shapes how work is received—papers whose abstracts are more involved are cited more by women and less by men, and the association persists even when papers are compared against their most content-similar alternatives, indicating a gendered signal independent of topic. That a near-costless feature of writing—word choice rather than substance—leaves a systematic, gendered trace on who cites scientific work points to a subtle channel through which evaluation bias may persist, in tension with the universalist ideal that ideas be judged independently of their author.
24. Great Expectations? Researchers in Germany are Optimistic about the Potential of Grant Lotteries
Christophe Heger
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.517
Deutsches Zentrum für Hochschul- und Wissenschaftsforschung (DZHW)
Abstract:
Abstract In this study I investigate researchers’ expectations towards the potential advantages of grant lotteries in a representative sample of scientists at German universities and higher education institutions provided by the Scientists Survey of the German Centre for Higher Education Research and Science Studies (DZHW). I describe the saliency of the two debates on bias in grant peer review and on grant lotteries, as well as researchers’ levels of experience with grant peer review. I use both descriptive statistics and analytical models to investigate which characteristics shape scientists’ relative grant lottery optimism. I find that experienced and more senior scientists are significantly less optimistic about grant lotteries. Saliency and reviewer workload are positively associated with optimism. I conclude that, even though saliency of the issue remains sparse, researchers are generally optimistic about grant lotteries, yet also aware of some of their potential disadvantages.
25. Gendered collaboration networks and their consequences on conflicts between academics
László Lőrincz; Brigitta Németh; Tamás Felföldi
Quantitative Science Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-05 | DOI 10.1162/qss.a.513
Institute of Data Analytics and Information Systems & ANETI Lab, Corvinus Institute for Advanced Studies, Corvinus University of Budapest, H-1093 Budapest, Fövám tér 8, Hungary; ANETI Lab, Institute for Economics, HUN-REN Centre for Economic and Regional Studies, H-1097 Budapest, Tóth Kálmán utca 4, Hungary; Doctoral School of Economics, Business and Informatics, Corvinus University of Budapest, H-1093 Budapest, Fövám tér 8, Hungary; Corvinus University of Budapest, H-1093 Budapest, Fövám tér 8, Hungary
Abstract:
Abstract As a major site of knowledge production, academia depends heavily on the quality of interactions among coworkers in collaboration. Negative ties, such as conflicts or avoidance between researchers, can impede the progress of research projects, and adversely affect individual career advancement. Our study reveals that these negative relations are not “gender-neutral” in the academic sector; they are experienced substantially more often by women. Using a large survey of early-career researchers in Hungary linked to administrative scientometric records, we model the number of conflict ties as a function of network position, gender, and field-level diversity. Among women, brokerage in co-authorship networks is associated with more conflicts, whereas embeddedness in cohesive collaboration groups is associated with fewer. These associations are not observed for men, whose conflict ties are more strongly related to publication-related success. Women also report fewer conflicts in fields where women are better represented, although evidence that field diversity moderates the brokerage-conflict relationship is limited. The findings show that collaboration-network advantages are not socially neutral in academia: brokerage may provide professional benefits, but it appears to entail higher interpersonal costs for women.

Abstract:
