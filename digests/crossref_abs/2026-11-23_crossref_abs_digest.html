2. Collateral Demand in Wholesale Funding Markets
Jamie Coen; Patrick Coen; Anne-Caroline Hüser
Review of Financial Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhag099
Imperial College London , U.K; Warwick Business School , U.K; Bank of England , U.K
Abstract:
Abstract Repo markets are systemically important funding markets, but are also used by firms to obtain the assets provided as collateral. We build and structurally estimate a model of trade between heterogeneous firms in the sterling gilt repo market, and show that this collateral demand depresses repo market activity and can impede the transmission of monetary policy. We quantify how well-designed central bank repo facilities can improve outcomes: if the central bank were to lend out the assets on its balance sheet following quantitative easing, transmission and total firm surplus would improve by 20% and 38%, respectively.
3. Short-horizon capital in a bank-centered financial system: Japanese mutual fund launches relative to the U.S.
Toshiki Honda; Alan Guoming Huang; Daisuke Miyakawa; Sho Nishiuchi; Russ Wermers
Journal of International Business Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1057/s41267-026-00916-0
Abstract:
Abstract The median time from launch to peak assets under management (AUM) is nine months for Japanese active domestic equity mutual funds, versus 4.6 years for comparable U.S. funds. Japanese funds also grow much less from launch to peak, as strong initial inflows quickly give way to persistent outflows, while U.S. funds continue to attract net inflows for years. We link this compressed life cycle to Japan’s bank-centered, concentrated distribution system and front-loaded sales compensation. A larger launch size and higher sales load each predict earlier peak AUM in Japan, but not in the U.S.; within Japan, stronger distribution networks are associated with larger launches and earlier peaks, while longer pre-inception sales periods are associated with larger launch sizes and higher sales loads. The evidence shows that the same market-based financial product can follow different life cycles when embedded in different national systems of intermediation and compensation. Japan’s prolonged weak equity-market environment provides an additional context in which investors may reassess newly launched funds as realized performance becomes observable. Our findings suggest that incentives rewarding initial sales can mobilize capital rapidly without generating durable asset retention in an institutional environment such as Japan’s.
4. Adapting to the new abnormal: multinational corporations’ foreign subsidiary investment and country resilience to climate change
Chang Hoon Oh; Jorge Rivera; Jennifer Oetzel
Journal of International Business Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1057/s41267-026-00912-4
Abstract:
Abstract As climate change (CC) increasingly disrupts infrastructure, supply chains, labor availability, and public services, multinational corporations (MNCs) face growing uncertainty when selecting locations for foreign expansion. We examine whether host-country CC resilience has become an important determinant of international investment and how firm capabilities shape this relationship. Drawing on socio-ecological resilience theory and the resilience and turbulence framework, we analyze Global Fortune 500 firms investing across 155 countries between 2003 and 2017. We find that MNCs establish more wholly owned subsidiaries in countries with higher CC resilience, indicating that resilience is emerging as a strategic location-specific advantage. This relationship is stronger for firms with well-developed ESG capabilities and prior experience operating in resilient countries, but weaker for firms with greater financial slack. Our findings suggest that managers should incorporate CC resilience into location decisions alongside traditional economic and institutional criteria while considering their firms’ resource and capability profile to assess how effectively they can leverage host-country CC resilience. For policymakers, the results indicate that strengthening CC resilience can improve not only adaptation to CC but also national competitiveness in attracting MNC foreign direct investment. Overall, CC resilience is becoming a strategic determinant of where MNCs choose to invest.
5. Stockouts and the Success of New Products
Diego Aparicio; Duncan I. Simester
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2023.02602
Georgetown University, Washington, District of Columbia 20057; MIT Sloan School of Management, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02142
Abstract:
We investigate how stockouts of existing products influence the success of new product introductions. Our findings reveal that stockouts on incumbent items at the time a new product is launched are associated with higher sales of the new product for at least six months. This positive spillover is specific to new products and is most potent at the start of the product life cycle. To establish causality, we use instrumental variables based on historical stockouts and contemporaneous stockouts in other stores. Analysis of individual-level transaction data corroborates a trial-and-adoption mechanism: Stockouts of existing items prompt consumers to trial the new product, leading to increased repeat purchasing. By identifying inventory as a driver of product trial, this research highlights the importance of strategically timing new product launches and documents a previously unrecognized tradeoff for inventory policies. 6. Proximity-Based Delivery Policies: Efficiency, Coverage, and Fairness
Zhongbin Wang; Shiliang Cui; Yongjian Li; Qian Zhang
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.02206
College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, China; McDonough School of Business, Georgetown University, Washington, District of Columbia 20057; Business School, Nankai University, Tianjin 300071, China; School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an 710049, China
Abstract:
The growing demand for rapid food and grocery delivery has prompted many restaurants and retailers to develop their own on-demand delivery services. Although hiring additional couriers can expand delivery capacity, it is often prohibitively costly. As a cost-effective alternative, we propose a proximity-based delivery (PBD) policy that prioritizes the delivery of orders from customers located closer to the vendor, in contrast to the traditional first-come-first-served (FCFS) approach. We show that PBD can substantially reduce average delivery delays without requiring additional couriers; however, it may raise fairness concerns, because distant customers experience longer wait times, potentially shrinking market coverage and exacerbating service disparities. To address this issue, we introduce an incentive-compatible PBD (IP) policy, where nearby customers are incentivized to pay for priority, whereas distant customers receive compensation to offset longer delays. We demonstrate that this mechanism can restore fairness to levels comparable to FCFS while preserving efficiency, expanding market demand, and enhancing consumer surplus, particularly in moderately dense markets with moderately delay-sensitive customers. We further show that vendors can enhance these benefits through optimal pricing and benefit from less extreme staffing adjustments due to a “pull-to-center” effect. Our findings highlight the operational, economic, and ethical dimensions of proximity-based delivery, and offer a practical framework for balancing speed, profitability, consumer welfare, and equity. Finally, we conduct a mini case study using empirical data to demonstrate the real-world applicability of our approach. This paper was accepted by Jeannette Song, operations management. Funding: Z. 7. Optimal Incentive Design for Decentralized Dynamic Matching Markets
Chen Chen; Pengyu Qian; Jingwei Zhang
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01346
New York University Shanghai, Shanghai 200124, China; Questrom School of Business, Boston University, Boston, Massachusetts 02215; School of Data Science, School of Management and Economics, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, Guangdong 518172, China
Abstract:
In decentralized dynamic matching markets, agents can benefit from sharing resources, but they will collaborate only if properly incentivized. Motivated by applications such as multihospital kidney exchanges and job-hunting markets, this paper proposes novel monetary and nonmonetary mechanisms to incentivize collaboration in such decentralized dynamic markets. We study a model in which multiple self-interested agents manage local multiway dynamic matching problems. Jobs of different types arrive stochastically at each agent, expire after a limited time, and yield rewards when matched. An agent’s job backlog and actions are the agent’s private information, and each agent aims to maximize its long-run average matching reward. We design simple mechanisms that incentivize agents to submit all jobs upon arrival, thereby enabling centralized matching. Our first mechanism, the marginal-value (MV) mechanism, reimburses agents based on the MV of their submitted jobs. This can be implemented in a nonmonetary way by randomly selecting an agent (with a specified probability) to perform a match and collect the associated matching reward. We show that under the MV mechanism, full job submission constitutes an approximate Nash equilibrium—that is, the gain from unilateral deviation vanishes as the number of agents grows. To further eliminate incentives for deviation, we propose a refined mechanism, the MV-plus-credit (MVC) mechanism, and show that, when the number of agents exceeds a constant threshold, full job submission constitutes a stronger oblivious equilibrium. Numerical experiments based on kidney exchange data demonstrate that the gains from deviation under our mechanisms are small even in moderately sized markets. 8. Learning from a Biased Sample
Roshni Sahoo; Lihua Lei; Stefan Wager
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08073
Columbia Business School, Columbia University, New York 10027; Graduate School of Business, Stanford University, Palo Alto, California 94305
Abstract:
The empirical risk minimization approach to data-driven decision making requires access to training data drawn under the same conditions as those that will be faced when the decision rule is deployed. However, we may be concerned that our training sample is biased in the sense that some groups (characterized by either observable or unobservable attributes) may be under- or over-represented relative to the general population; and in this setting, empirical risk minimization over the training set may fail to yield rules that perform well at deployment. We propose a model of sampling bias called conditional [Formula: see text]-biased sampling, where observed covariates can affect the probability of sample selection arbitrarily much but the amount of unexplained variation in the probability of sample selection is bounded by a constant factor. Applying the distributionally robust optimization framework, we propose a method for learning a decision rule that minimizes the worst-case risk incurred under a family of test distributions that can generate the training distribution under conditional [Formula: see text]-biased sampling. We apply a result of Rockafellar and Uryasev to show that this problem is equivalent to an augmented convex risk minimization problem. We give statistical guarantees for learning a model that is robust to sampling bias via the method of sieves, and propose a deep learning algorithm whose loss function captures our robust learning target. We empirically validate our proposed method in a case study on prediction of mental health scores from health survey data and a case study on intensive care unit length-of-stay prediction. 9. Information Design with Supplemental Investment
Lin Nan; Lin Qiu; Xiaoyan Wen; Ronghuo Zheng
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.00524
Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907; Texas Christian University, Fort Worth, Texas 76129; University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712
Abstract:
This paper features a setting with two-dimensional information asymmetry on a firm’s fundamental (good or bad type) and the nature of its supplemental investment (improving upside potential, or mitigating downside risk). We examine the firm’s optimal design of the information system when the system can only address the dimension of fundamental. We show that, with only single-dimensional information asymmetry regarding the firm’s fundamental, greater informativeness on the firm’s fundamental type always improves efficiency. However, with two-dimensional information asymmetry, increasing informativeness about firm fundamental no longer guarantees higher efficiency and can be detrimental. The optimal information system trades off reducing information asymmetry and preserving firms’ incentives to invest, and it could be uninformative or optimistically biased, depending on the level of credit risk, which in our setting is higher when supplemental investment is more likely to improve upside rather than protect downside. 10. Deep Tangency Portfolio
Guanhao Feng; Liang Jiang; Junye Li; Yizhi Song; Yuanzhi Wang
Management Science

Abstract:
We develop a deep learning framework to directly estimate tangency portfolio weights by augmenting benchmark factors with a long-short deep factor derived from high-dimensional characteristics. Guided by a Sharpe ratio maximization objective, the deep factor plays two key roles: (i) providing a natural hedge against benchmark risks and (ii) capturing multimarket signals to enhance the performance of the benchmark. In an empirical application to U.S. corporate bonds using 132 firm-level bond, equity, and option characteristics, the deep factor achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio exceeding one, and the deep tangency portfolio benchmarked against the market factor yields a Sharpe ratio over two, outperforming portfolios spanned by common observable or latent factors. These results demonstrate that bond risk signals are fundamentally dense, requiring the nonlinear integration of multimarket information to span the efficient frontier. 12. Corruption Perceptions, the Pendleton Act, and Sovereign Borrowing Costs
John A. Dove; Noah J. Trudeau
Public Administration Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-22 | DOI 10.1111/puar.70236
School of Creative Enterprise and Market Systems, Sorrell College of Business Troy University Troy Alabama USA
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Significant research suggests that systems of bureaucratic patronage tend to promote corruption and reduce the efficient production of publicly provided goods. However, much less work has been done to consider how market actors perceive such selection processes and especially reforms implemented to reduce such corruption. This study rectifies this by evaluating how the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act impacted federal borrowing costs. If patronage resulted in increased corruption and bureaucratic inefficiency, this should be reflected in relatively higher bond yields. If the Pendleton Act was successful in curbing this behavior, then this should be rewarded by market actors in the form of lower borrowing costs. Using interrupted time series analysis with and without control, we find that the passage of the Pendleton Act did, in fact, result in lower borrowing costs for the federal government of between 63 and 84 basis points depending on specification. Implications are discussed.

Peking University Guanghua School of Management; Southwestern University of Finance and Economics China Center for Behavioral Economics and Finance; Xiamen University School of Management
Abstract:
Abstract We investigate “spiritual insurance”—a mental strategy for coping with risk, where individuals engage in good deeds, including donations, in exchange for perceived blessings and protection. Using bank transactional data, we show that higher income uncertainty and health shocks lead to increased donations, particularly to religious charities. Moreover, individuals who donate to religious charities tend to reduce insurance expenditures. 20. How Do Employees Navigate Destructive Leadership Behaviour? A Focus on SMEs
Afsaneh Bagheri; Safoora Pitsi; Gholamreza Polooee
British Journal of Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-22 | DOI 10.1111/1467-8551.70116
Lincoln International Business School University of Lincoln Lincoln LN6 7TS UK; Excelsia University College Pennant Hills NSW 2120 Australia; University of Wollongong Wollongong NSW 2500 Australia; Faculty of Entrepreneurship University of Tehran Tehran Iran
Abstract:
Abstract Destructive leadership has detrimental effects on employee and organizational performance, yet we know little about how employees respond to such leadership behaviour, particularly in small‐ and medium‐sized enterprises (SMEs), where close interpersonal relationships and limited organizational support constrain employees’ responses. Drawing on Leader‐Member Exchange (LMX) theory, we examine the coping strategies employees adopt to navigate destructive leadership behaviour and the mechanisms that shape these strategies. We conducted 26 semi‐structured interviews with employees across diverse SMEs and job roles and analysed the data thematically. We identify three coping strategies: (i) adaptation, (ii) resistance and (iii) exit. Employees’ choice among these strategies depends on their assessment of their tolerance of personal and professional risks and their perceived power to influence or change their situation. These evaluations determine whether employees accommodate their leaders’ destructive behaviour, resist it or disengage entirely by leaving the organization. Our findings extend LMX theory by highlighting employees’ active role in evaluating their interactions and adapting their responses according to the associated risks and their power to influence or change their situation. The study also highlights the contextual dynamics of SMEs and offers insights for organizations seeking to recognize the effects of destructive leadership and support employees.
21. The Reversal of the Gender Gap in Automation: Evidence From the US Labor Market

Department of Economics Brown University Providence Rhode Island USA; Faculty of Economics and Business University of Chile Santiago Chile; Business School Adolfo Ibáñez University Santiago Chile
Abstract:
ABSTRACT This paper documents a reversal in the US gender gap in the probability of automation, 1976–2019. Women initially had higher automation probabilities than men, but the gap narrowed and reversed. The aggregate change occurs mainly within sectors, from clerical and retail sales to professional and technical occupations. The reversal is associated with education and occupational work‐hour structure. 22. Beyond Union Membership: Wage Formation and Collective Institutions
Fredrik B. Kostøl; Elin W. Svarstad
Industrial Relations
ABS 4 | 2026-11-23 | DOI 10.1111/irel.70056
Department of Industrial Economics and Technology Management Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) Trondheim Norway; Fafo Institute of Labour and Social Research Oslo Norway
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Using linked Norwegian employer‐employee register data for 2016–2023, we examine how union membership, workplace union density, and collective agreement coverage relate to wages. Union membership, workplace union density, and collective agreement coverage represent distinct dimensions of collective presence and should not be treated as conceptually equivalent. We find that union members earn more than non‐members irrespective of coverage, while the relationship between wages and union density depends on membership and coverage.
