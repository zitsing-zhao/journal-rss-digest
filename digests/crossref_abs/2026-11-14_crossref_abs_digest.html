4. What’s at Stake? Understanding the Role of Home Equity in Flood Insurance Demand
Yanjun (Penny) Liao; Philip Mulder
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01302
Resources for the Future, Washington, District of Columbia 20036; Department of Risk and Insurance, University of Wisconsin–Madison, Madison, Wisconsin 53715
Abstract:
Changing flood insurance demand shifts climate risk across homeowners, lenders, and governments. We show that flood insurance take-up follows rising and falling home equity over the housing boom and bust across markets with different price dynamics. To isolate the causal effect of risk shifting on insurance demand, we study flood insurance policy renewals over the housing bust, exploiting granular differences in purchase timing that led policyholders to experience differential changes in skin-in-the-game exposure to flood risk for the same change in home equity. When homeowners have less equity exposure to flood losses, they are less likely to renew their policies. Lenders, in turn, have little incentive to require flood insurance when they can sell their mortgages to federally backed mortgage purchasers. This paper was accepted by Tomasz Piskorski, finance. 5. The Economics of Waste Valorization
Yuefeng Li; Zhongbin Wang; Shenghai Zhou
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08500
School of Economics and Management, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, Chengdu 611731, China; College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, China; School of Business, Central South University, Changsha, Hunan 410083, China; and Laboratory of Intelligent Governance for Complex Social Systems, Changsha, Hunan 410083, China
Abstract:
As the spread of waste imposes substantial environmental and economic burdens, a newer operational strategy, waste valorization, is gaining attention. This approach transforms production-generated byproduct waste into valuable, distinct products, creating intricate production and demand interdependencies that traditional strategies like remanufacturing and coproduction cannot fully capture. In this study, we examine the impacts of these interdependencies within a two-stage stochastic production decision model to determine optimal production quantities under waste valorization. Our findings reveal that a higher waste valorization rate does not always benefit the manufacturer, especially if the waste incurs disposal costs. When product demands are uncorrelated, the manufacturer’s optimal profit can exhibit an inverse U-shaped relationship with the valorization rate, attaining its maximum at a moderate level that maintains a perfect balance in production. Conversely, when product demands are correlated, the manufacturer employs a “pool-to-center” strategy, using different pooling policies to smooth production quantities and minimize disparities with respect to the valorization rate. Surprisingly, as demand randomness or correlation intensifies, the optimal profit becomes M-shaped, with profit maximum shifting toward either low or high valorization rates, a phenomenon we term “go-to-extreme.” Although waste valorization can yield both economic and environmental benefits, we caution that a more efficient waste valorization process may lead to negative outcomes for both the manufacturer and the environment. This analysis not only sheds light on how waste valorization contributes to waste reduction and economic value, but also provides insights for production decision making. This paper was accepted by Caroline Flammer, sustainability. 6. The Data-Driven Censored Newsvendor Problem
Chamsi Hssaine; Sean R. Sinclair
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.09021
Department of Data Sciences and Operations, University of Southern California, Marshall School of Business, Los Angeles, California 90089; Department of Industrial Engineering and Management Sciences, Northwestern University, Evanston, Illinois 60208
Abstract:
We study a censored variant of the data-driven newsvendor problem, where the decision maker must select an ordering quantity that minimizes expected overage and underage costs based only on offline censored sales data rather than historical demand realizations. Our goal is to understand how the degree of historical demand censoring affects the performance of any learning algorithm for this problem. To isolate this impact, we adopt a distributionally robust optimization framework, evaluating policies according to their worst-case regret over an ambiguity set of distributions. This set is defined by the largest historical order quantity (the observable boundary of the data set), and contains all distributions matching the true demand distribution up to this boundary while allowing them to be arbitrary afterward. We demonstrate a spectrum of achievability under demand censoring by deriving a natural necessary and sufficient condition under which vanishing regret is an achievable goal. In regimes in which it is not, we exactly characterize the information loss due to censoring: an insurmountable lower bound on the performance of any policy, even when the decision maker has access to infinitely many demand samples. We then leverage these sharp characterizations to propose a natural robust algorithm that adapts to the historical level of demand censoring. We derive finite-sample guarantees for this algorithm across all possible censoring regimes and show its near-optimality with matching lower bounds (up to polylogarithmic factors). We moreover demonstrate its robust performance via extensive numerical experiments on both synthetic and real-world data sets. This paper was accepted by Wolfram Wiesemann, data science. 7. Rethinking Distribution Shifts: Empirical Analysis and Modeling for Tabular Data
Tianyu Wang; Jiashuo Liu; Peng Cui; Hongseok Namkoong
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08298
Department of Industrial Engineering and Operations Research, Columbia University, New York, New York 10027; Department of Computer Science and Technology, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100190, China; Decision, Risk, and Operations Division, Columbia University, New York, New York 10027
Abstract:
Different distribution shifts require different interventions, and algorithms must be grounded in the specific shifts they address. However, methodological development for robust algorithms typically relies on structural assumptions that lack empirical validation. Advocating for an empirically grounded data-driven approach to algorithm development, we build an empirical testbed comprising natural shifts across 8 tabular data sets, 172 distribution pairs over 45 methods, and 90,000 method configurations encompassing empirical risk minimization and distributionally robust optimization (DRO) methods. We find that Y|X-shifts are most prevalent in our testbed, in stark contrast to the heavy focus on X (covariate)-shifts in the machine learning literature, and that the performance of robust algorithms is no better than that of vanilla methods. To understand why, we conduct an in-depth empirical analysis of DRO methods and find that underlooked implementation details—such as the choice of underlying model class (e.g., LightGBM) and hyperparameter selection—have a bigger impact on performance than the ambiguity set or its radius. We illustrate via case studies how a data-driven understanding of distribution shifts can provide a new approach to algorithm development. This paper was accepted by Wolfram Wiesemann, data science. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.08298 .
8. Insurers’ Investments and Insurance Prices
Benjamin Knox; Jakob Ahm Sørensen
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.09038
Division of International Finance, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Washington, District of Columbia 20551; Finance Department, Bocconi University, Milan 20136, Italy
Abstract:
We develop a theory in which stable funding from insurance underwriting allows insurers to earn the liquidity premium on illiquid assets and pass part of it back to policyholders through lower prices. Consistent with the model’s predictions, we show empirically that (1) insurance prices are lower when the liquidity premium is higher, for both life insurers and property and casualty insurers. (2) In the cross-section, insurers with more stable funding earn higher expected returns and set lower prices. (3) It is specifically the part of expected returns driven by stable funding that passes through to prices. The findings highlight the value of insurance underwriting as a stable source of funding, with benefits that flow back to policyholders. This paper was accepted by Bo Becker, finance. 9. Comparing Factor Models Under Regime Switching
Shamim Ahmed; Cesare Robotti; Daniel Tsvetanov; Xiaoxia Ye
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.04942
University of Liverpool Management School, University of Liverpool, Liverpool L69 7ZH, United Kingdom; Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, United Kingdom; Norwich Business School, University of East Anglia, Norwich NR4 7TJ, United Kingdom; Nottingham University Business School, University of Nottingham, Nottingham NG8 1BB, United Kingdom
Abstract:
We compare factor pricing models using the “test-asset irrelevance” result but under regime switching. We show that the unconditional squared Sharpe ratio measure is no longer appropriate for model comparison when returns are subject to regime shifts. However, conditional on a given regime, model comparison can be performed using the conditional squared Sharpe ratio metric. We find that the Fama-French six-factor model that employs a cash-based profitability factor, the Barillas-Shanken six-factor model, and the Stambaugh-Yuan four-factor model are jointly the best-performing models under the bull regime. However, no model performs significantly better than others under the bear regime. Importantly, these findings are robust to regime uncertainty. We also find that unconditional model rankings closely mirror those in bull markets, overstating the superiority of the models identified as best performing in expansions by ignoring the performance convergence across models that emerges in downturns. This underscores the importance of evaluating factor models conditional on market regimes. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. 10. Banking on a Corporate Rebrand
Matthew Jaremski; Kris James Mitchener; Kilian Rieder
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.03635
Department of Economics and Finance, Huntsman School of Business, Utah State University & NBER, Logan, Utah 84322; Department of Economics, Leavey School of Business, Santa Clara University, NBER, CEPR, CESifo & CAGE, Santa Clara, California 95053; Economics and Research Department, Oesterreichische Nationalbank (Eurosystem) & CEPR, Vienna 1090, Austria
Abstract:
Firms often rebrand to counter negative shocks, but can it work? Using a historical natural experiment, we analyze a large sample of companies from the same industry (banking) that shared very similar names, but overwhelmingly decided to change them in response to a common, negative news shock. U.S. entry into World War I in 1917 created a sudden anti-German backlash against anything that invoked an association with the now enemy. The shock itself was thus orthogonal to bank fundamentals and pre-existing trends, but pushed banks to reconsider their brands. After 1917, the few German-named banks that kept their tainted names saw significant declines in assets, deposits, and market share relative to other banks. However, German-named banks that rebranded mitigated much of the negative shock. Specifically, German-named banks that adopted a new non-ethnic brand name avoided between 75% and 85% of the anti-German effect, and those that adopted a new patriotic brand name were able to neutralize it completely. Overall, we find that “crisis rebranding” paid off in our historical setting regardless of the chosen brand, with patriotic rebranding proving the most effective at offsetting the exogenous shock. This paper was accepted by Jean-Pierre Dube, marketing. Funding: K. J. 11. Wrongdoers or Saviors? CEO Activism, Media Partisanship, and the Media’s Construction of CEO Infamy and Celebrity
G. Alessandra Alessandra Rizzi; Vilmos F. Misangyi
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.19062
Texas Christian University, Management and Leadership, Fort Worth, Texas, United States; The Pennsylvania State University, Department of Management & Organization, University Park, Pennsylvania, United States
Abstract:
This study advances research on social evaluations by theorizing how partisan media construct CEO infamy through dramaturgical logics distinct from those underlying CEO celebrity. Moving beyond conceptualizations of infamy as merely negative celebrity, we argue that infamy emerges through simple melodramatic narratives casting CEOs as wrongdoers, whereas celebrity involves more complex dramatized narratives portraying CEOs as saviors. Focusing on CEOs’ liberal sociopolitical activism—a visible, ideologically-charged action that departs from conventional executive roles—we theorize that this behavior generates divergent constructions in partisan media: infamy in conservative outlets and celebrity in liberal outlets. We further argue that activism more readily generates infamy than celebrity because melodramatic narratives are simpler to construct and negative deviance is more readily perceived than positive deviance. We examine two moderators of these relationships. Firm performance differentially shapes these constructions, with poorer performance intensifying conservative-media infamy and stronger performance amplifying liberal-media celebrity. CEO social media engagement, conversely, attenuates both by constraining traditional media’s narrative discretion and reducing activism’s newsworthiness. We develop an LLM-based annotation system capturing CEO-directed sentiment, role attributions, and dramaturgical narratives in media articles. Analyzing 91,608 articles covering 705 S&P 500 CEOs across 45 ideologically polarized U.S. media outlets between 2016 and 2022, we find broad support for our hypotheses across our main and extensive supplemental analyses. Our findings advance understanding of CEO infamy and social evaluations more generally by revealing how partisan media construct divergent social evaluations of the same executive behavior through distinct narrative processes.
12. Incumbents’ Alliances and (Non-)Adaptation During the Pre-Commercialization Stage of a New Technology
Jung-Hyun Suh; J.P. Eggers
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.18899
The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Management, Shatin, New Territories, Hong Kong; New York University, Stern School, Mgmt & Orgs, New York, New York, United States
Abstract:
Research on incumbent adaptation through alliances typically assumes that incumbents can entice partners to share proprietary knowledge in a new technology by offering access to valuable complementary assets. This assumption fits the commercialization stage of a new technology, when both proprietary knowledge and complementary assets hold high value. However, during the pre-commercialization stage—when proprietary knowledge in the new technology is valuable but incumbents’ complementary assets cannot yet be fully deployed to commercialize the new technology—this logic may not hold. We develop a framework that explains how stage-dependent shifts in the value of both proprietary knowledge and complementary assets alter the bargaining power between incumbents and knowledge-possessing firms, thereby reshaping the adaptive potential of alliances. Using data on the semiconductor industry during the emergence of nanotechnology, we found that alliances between semiconductor incumbents and nanotechnology-capable partners are less likely to form and, even when formed, are less likely to focus on the explicit transfer of proprietary knowledge in emerging nanotechnology. Moreover, these alliances are more likely to be structured as joint ventures, especially when the risk of knowledge leakage is heightened. Joint venture arrangements, in turn, impede semiconductor incumbents’ post-alliance nanotechnology invention more than contractual arrangements. Taken together, we develop insights into how incumbents may struggle to use alliances for adaptive learning during the pre-commercialization phase of a new technology.
13. Can Voluntary Corporate Transparency Curb Corruption? Community-Level Evidence from Oil and Gas Fields in Africa
Shirley Tang; Daniel W Elfenbein; Tatenda M Pasipanodya
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2025.20487
Boston University, Strategy and Innovation, Boston, Massachusetts, United States; Washington University in St Louis, John M. Olin School of Business, St. Louis, Missouri, United States; Louisiana State University System, Management, Baton Rouge, Louisiana, United States
Abstract:
Voluntary corporate transparency seeks to promote accountability through disclosure, but substantive reporting does not by itself guarantee societal benefits. We examine whether this form of self-regulation can help curb corruption where formal oversight is weak, focusing on corporate support for the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (EITI), a multistakeholder initiative that promotes disclosure of company payments to host governments. We link geolocated data on African oil and gas fields and their operators to Afrobarometer surveys and satellite nighttime-light measures and estimate difference-in-differences models that track changes in outcomes around the same fields before and after operators endorse the EITI. We find that corporate EITI support is associated with lower perceived corruption and less reported bribery, greater civic engagement, greater perceived freedom to speak, and higher nighttime-light intensity, a proxy for local economic activity. The association with lower perceived corruption is stronger around fields with nearby NGO or aid-project activity, among residents living closer to fields, and among frequent users of traditional news media. Supplementary analyses provide indirect evidence that supporting firms were more transparent before mandatory disclosure laws took effect, and show that they had fewer joint-venture partners and larger ownership stakes after endorsement. Archival cases from Nigeria and Ghana illustrate how civil-society organizations and journalists use extractive-industry information to question firms and public authorities. The findings point to local governance benefits of voluntary transparency commitments and are consistent with an information-to-accountability account in which these benefits depend on both substantive reporting and local opportunities to scrutinize and use the information.
