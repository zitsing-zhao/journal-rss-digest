1. Partisan Banks and Creditor Coordination Within Loan Syndicates
Robert M. Bushman; Sang Woo Sohn; Jianxin (Donny) Zhao
Journal of Accounting Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1111/1475-679x.70097
University of North Carolina; City University of Hong Kong; Emory University
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We investigate how firm‐level partisanship influences interactions between banks in the syndicated loan market. Because politically divergent banks hold conflicting perceptions of economic conditions, ideological distance between banks impedes consensus and elevates expected coordination costs. We find that greater political distance between a lead arranger and a participant reduces the participant's likelihood of joining a syndicate and lowers its retained loan share relative to the lead. The relation between political distance and loan share differences intensifies during periods of high national political polarization. Moreover, greater political diversity within a syndicate is associated with less restrictive loan covenants and higher interest spreads, reflecting a trade‐off where lenders sacrifice contingent control to avoid costly renegotiation gridlock. Validating our disagreement mechanism, we show that following presidential elections, banks politically misaligned with the incoming administration issue significantly more pessimistic macroeconomic forecasts than aligned banks.

4. Whiplash: Congestion Dissipation in a Disrupted Circulatory Shipping System
Ming Hu; Chaoyu Zhang
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.05427
Rotman School of Management, University of Toronto, Toronto, Ontario M5S 3E6, Canada; College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong
Abstract:
The (post)pandemic period was marked by numerous port disruptions, leading to delays and rising costs for shippers. We build a fluid model in transient states to investigate how a disruption at one port can affect the disrupted port and its counterpart in another country in a circulatory container shipping system, where a stream of fleets moves goods back and forth between the two ports. Port disruption leads to two types of congestion: the inbound backlogs, which occur when ships are unable to enter the port, and the outbound backlogs, which arise when goods are unable to be loaded onto ships for transport to other ports. We analytically characterize the system’s recovery time, measured from the onset of the port closure until normal operations are restored, and the dynamics of inbound and outbound backlogs during recovery. We identify a whiplash effect in which outbound backlogs alternate between ports, creating oscillatory congestion patterns because of the disruption-induced uneven spatial distribution of ships. Our analysis reveals that simply increasing ship capacity may not expedite system recovery, highlighting the importance of coordination between port authorities and shipping companies when jointly planning capacity expansions. Furthermore, we examine several extensions of the model. We use the two-port system as a building block to analyze bipartite and cyclic network structures, and we also consider scenarios in which a port experiences multiple disruptions. In all cases, the whiplash effect persists, thereby demonstrating the robustness of our findings. Finally, we use our theory model prediction as a feature and apply machine learning techniques to predict the time that vessels spend in Shanghai Port after a typhoon disruption and show that using our model predictor, alongside all relevant vessel and voyage attributes as raw features, significantly reduces prediction errors by 10%–30% on average compared with not using it. This paper was accepted by Elena Katok, operations management. Funding: This work was supported by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada [Grant RGPIN-2021-04295]. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05427 .
5. Do Fund Managers’ Commitments to Responsible Investing Affect Private Information Acquisition, Information Environment, and Corporate Sustainability?
Sumit Agarwal; Johan Sulaeman; Yue Zhang; Zhe Zong
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08443
NUS Business School and Sustainable and Green Finance Institute (SGFIN), National University of Singapore, 119245, Singapore; International School of Business & Finance and Advanced Institute of Finance, Sun Yat-sen University, Zhuhai 519082, China; China Zheshang Bank, Hangzhou 310020, China
Abstract:
We investigate the effects of mutual funds’ commitments to responsible investing on their information acquisition activities and the corresponding externalities for other stakeholders. After committing to responsible investing (via United Nations Principles for Responsible Investment (UNPRI)), fund managers increase private information acquisition activities, that is, corporate site visits, particularly focusing on environmental and social issues. These visits generate negligible private investment value for the visiting funds. However, the information environment at the stock level improves following these visits with lower consensus analyst forecast errors. In addition to the information production role of these visits, they also generate positive externalities for other stakeholders through incremental improvements in the sustainability practices of the firms receiving sustainability-focused site visits from UNPRI fund managers. Collectively, the paper highlights the private costs and social benefits of responsible investing. This paper was accepted by Will Cong, finance. Funding: S. Agarwal and J. 6. Biodiversity Risk in the Corporate Bond Market
Sevgi Soylemezgil; Cihan Uzmanoglu
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.07660
Binghamton University, Binghamton, New York 13902
Abstract:
We show that a biodiversity risk premium has emerged in the U.S. corporate bond market following the United Nations Biodiversity Conference (COP15) in October 2021. COP15 placed biodiversity on the global regulatory agenda and raised transition risks for exposed firms. After COP15, a one standard deviation increase in a firm’s biodiversity footprint is associated with a 7% increase in its secondary market yield spreads, whereas this relation is insignificant before COP15. The biodiversity effect is also present in primary bond markets, most pronounced for medium-term bonds and distinct from climate-related mechanisms. Furthermore, a higher biodiversity footprint is associated with a lower likelihood of issuing medium-term bonds after COP15, consistent with firms managing their biodiversity-related risks. Evidence from firms’ biodiversity dependencies suggests that physical biodiversity risks are also priced in the bond market. This paper was accepted by Bo Becker, finance. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.07660 .
7. Advertising by Recruiting Influencers
Maya Jalloul; Vasiliki Kostami
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.07919
Paris School of Business, 75005 Paris, France; HEC Paris, 78351 Jouy-en-Josas, France
Abstract:
The rise of social media platforms has enabled customers to rely on influencers and peer reviews to guide their purchasing decisions. As products increasingly focus on experiences, firms are leveraging influencer marketing as a crucial tool. This paper addresses key challenges of influencer marketing by considering different types of influencers and partnership schemes alongside the impact of social learning. We introduce a two-period model wherein, in the initial period, the firm unveils a new product experience of unknown quality alongside an influencer marketing campaign. The campaign relies on two types of influencers that differ in popularity and engagement levels and two partnerships: sponsored advertising and public relations. Customers base their purchasing decisions on their original (heterogeneous) beliefs, influencer-driven information, and reviews from early adopters in the second period. When customers are equally likely to follow any given influencer, the firm should choose a single influencer type and partnership. Consumer reviews complement influencer marketing when products are initially undervalued, increasing the returns to influencer investment. Moreover, within a societal structure characterized by distinct classes, in which followers and influencers often belong to the same class, a mix of influencer types and partnership schemes may become optimal. Our study provides guidance on selecting influencer types, partnership schemes, and social media platforms based on engagement, popularity, audience composition, and platform characteristics. This paper was accepted by Jeanette Song, operations management. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.07919 .

12. The old lady of economic history: 100 years of the <i>Economic History Review</i>
Martina Cioni; Giovanni Federico; Michelangelo Vasta
Economic History Review
ABS 4 | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1111/ehr.70165
Department of Economics and Statistics University of Siena Siena Italy; Social Sciences Division New York University, Abu Dhabi, UAE and CEPR; Department of Economics and Statistics University of Siena, Siena, Italy, and CEPR
Abstract:
Abstract This article traces the evolution of the Economic History Review over its first century and places it within the broader transformation of economic history as a discipline. Drawing on a new database covering 2761 articles published in the journal between 1927 and 2025 by 2011 authors from 571 institutions in 50 countries, it analyses changes in editorial leadership, research topics, historical periods, geographical coverage, methods, authorship, and citation patterns. The findings suggest that the journal evolved from a predominantly UK outlet into one of the leading international journals in economic history. Over time, it converged towards the model of the major international field journals, with citation networks revealing a shift from stronger links with history to closer connections with economics. At the same time, however, the journal retained a distinctive profile, preserving a more historical orientation than the leading American journals. The article also documents a marked process of internationalization, reflected in both the geographical scope of published research and the growing presence of scholars from continental Europe. Finally, citation analysis shows that articles addressing long‐run and geographically comparative questions achieve the highest scholarly impact.
13. Government‐funded experimentation: How startups use R&amp;D contracts to resolve uncertainty
Larisa Cioaca
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70063
Freeman School of Business Tulane University New Orleans Louisiana USA
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary Startups use experimentation to reduce technological and market uncertainty, but experimentation is costly and unevenly funded. This paper examines how government‐funded experimentation relates to subsequent performance among high‐tech startups that receive federal awards. Using data on $32 billion in US government R&D contracts awarded to high‐tech startups between 2001 and 2018, I distinguish between technology experimentation, measured by the value of R&D contracts, and market experimentation, measured by the number of agencies awarding the R&D contracts. Market experimentation is positively associated with subsequent employment, sales, and acquisition, while technology experimentation shows weaker associations with these outcomes. The findings are consistent with startups using information from multiple government buyers to learn about market opportunities and improve subsequent performance. Managerial Summary High‐tech startups need to test both whether their technology works and where demand exists, but venture capital often leaves this experimentation unfunded. Using data on $32 billion in US government R&D contracts awarded to high‐tech startups from 2001 to 2018, this paper shows that startups funded by more government agencies subsequently have higher employment and sales and are more likely to be acquired. Larger R&D contract amounts have less consistent relationships with these outcomes. For managers, the findings suggest considering what different government customers can help the firm learn. For policymakers, they suggest that the diversity of a startup's public customer base is a vital margin to consider alongside the overall size of its R&D awards.
14. The Impacts of Host Culture Heterogeneity on Learning from Success Versus Failure in Foreign Direct Investments
Yuping Zeng; Sangcheol Song; Soonkyoo Choe
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-16 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70178
Southern Illinois University Edwardsville; Saint Joseph's University; Yonsei University
Abstract:
Abstract This study examines the differentiated influences of host culture heterogeneity on the learning outcomes of multinational enterprises' (MNEs) success and failure experiences in foreign direct investments (FDI). We posit that host culture heterogeneity among prior successful FDIs reduces the beneficial effect of an MNE's success experience on a subsequent FDI, particularly when the cultural differences between the subsequent FDI and the MNE's prior successes are significant. In contrast, host culture heterogeneity among prior failed FDIs enhances the learning benefits of failure experience, and this effect is more pronounced when the subsequent FDI is culturally distant from the prior failures. Using FDI data of South Korean MNEs between 1990 and 2016, we find support for our hypotheses, demonstrating that host culture heterogeneity has divergent impacts on the learning outcomes of MNEs' success versus failure FDI experiences. This study enhances our understanding of the conditions under which host culture heterogeneity may weaken or strengthen the learning benefits of an MNE's FDI experience.
15. Partner selection in project-based software development: the roles of performance, collaboration experience and network governance
Yingchao Lan; Tingting Yan; Brett Massimino
International Journal of Operations and Production Management
ABS 4 | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1108/ijopm-04-2026-0531
University of Nebraska-Lincoln , , ,; Texas Tech University , , ,; Virginia Commonwealth University , , ,
Abstract:
Purpose This study examines supplier selection in project-based software development, where buyers rely on external developers for specialized capabilities while facing substantial safeguarding risks. Adopting a network-governance perspective, we investigate how network overlap – the extent to which a publisher and developer share common partners – moderates the relationships between developer performance, prior collaboration experience and selection outcomes. Design/methodology/approach Using a longitudinal dataset from the electronic video game industry (7,583 products, 4,322 unique developers and 3,016 unique publishers, over 16 years), we model developer selection using a conditional logit framework. Findings Developers with stronger historical product performance, confidentiality performance and greater prior collaboration experience are more likely to be selected. Network overlap substitutes for prior collaboration experience while strengthening the association between confidentiality performance and selection likelihood, highlighting the contingent role of network governance in partner selection decisions in project-based software development. Originality/value This study advances supplier-selection research by examining observed partner selection in project-based software development. It integrates firm-level indicators, dyadic experience and network overlap, showing that network governance can substitute for prior collaboration while complementing confidentiality performance.
