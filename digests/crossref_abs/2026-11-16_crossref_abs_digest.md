- [Exploration Optimization for Dynamic Assortment Personalization under Linear Preferences](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2022.0205)
  - DOI: 10.1287/opre.2022.0205
  - Authors: Fernando G. Bernstein; Sajad Modaresi; Denis Saure
  - Affiliations: Duke University, The Fuqua School of Business, Durham, North Carolina, United States; University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Kenan-Flagler Business School, Chapel Hill, North Carolina, United States; University of Chile, Industrial Engineering, Santiago, Chile
  - Published: 2026-11-16
  - Crossref journal: Operations Research
  - Abstract:
    We study dynamic assortment personalization, where an online retailer adaptively selects assortments based on customer attributes to learn preferences and maximize revenue. We assume that product utilities depend linearly on customer attributes through an unknown coefficient matrix, leading to an exploration–exploitation trade-off. We identify price-driven and linearity-driven exploration efficiencies, showing that not all products need to be offered to all customer profiles to recover optimal assortments. We establish an instance-dependent regret lower bound characterized by a linear program, which we call the Regret Lower Bound (RLB) problem. Because the RLB has a complex structure and depends non-trivially on unknown parameters, we introduce an alternative formulation, called the Exploration-Optimization problem, that yields an interpretable exploration structure and can be formulated as a tractable mixed-integer linear program. We develop learning policies that select efficient exploration sets by solving either problem and establish a regret upper bound for the Exploration-Optimization policy. Using data from a large Chilean retailer, we demonstrate that our policies substantially outperform Thompson sampling in terms of both regret and computational efficiency, while remaining scalable in practice.

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT This paper studies how human capital shapes both the level and direction of automation. We develop an endogenous Schumpeterian growth model in which skilled labor is allocated across production, horizontal automation of new tasks, and vertical upgrading of existing automated tasks. - [Unethical Leadership and Employee Creative Unethicality—Do Dark Personalities Matter?](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8551.70126)
  - DOI: 10.1111/1467-8551.70126
  - Authors: Janja Annabel Tardios; Farhad Uddin Ahmed; Md Imtiaz Mostafiz; Puteh Noraihan A. Rahman; Shlomo Y. Tarba; Kumud Wijayaratna; Hakeem A. Owolabi
  - Affiliations: Brunel Business School, Brunel University London Uxbridge UK; School of Business, Maynooth University Maynooth Ireland; University of Leicester School of Business, University of Leicester Leicester UK; Miyan Research Institute, International University of Business Agriculture and Technology (IUBAT) Dhaka Bangladesh; School of Liberal Studies (CITRA‐UKM), Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) Bangi Malaysia; Birmingham Business School, University of Birmingham Birmingham UK; Greater Manchester Business School, University of Bolton Bolton UK
  - Published: 2026-11-15
  - Crossref journal: British Journal of Management
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Recent research has shown that employee creativity may also be unethical; however, our understanding of such behaviour is limited. Existing literature suggests that certain leadership behaviours foster creativity; hence, employee creative unethicality may be similarly underpinned by unethical leadership—that is, leadership that rejects accepted norms. Building on the focus‐strand logic of self‐regulation theory, we argue that the degree of unethical leadership has an inverted U‐shaped relationship with employee creative unethicality, and that this relationship is conditional on employee Dark Triad personality traits. A time‐lagged survey of 468 employees from 26 firms in Bangladesh, India and Malaysia confirms the hypothesized inverted U‐shaped effect and finds that this relationship is positively moderated by employee Machiavellianism and psychopathy. The findings contribute to understanding when creative unethicality escalates within organizations by showing that unethical leadership does not invariably translate into employee creative unethicality. Under high unethical leadership, employees guided by self‐regulation may instead pursue more ethical solutions. However, when employees possess Machiavellian or psychopathic traits, their self‐regulatory tendencies are compromised, thereby increasing the likelihood of creative unethicality under unethical leadership.

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT The introduction of robots has produced uneven benefits for workers with different skills, occupations, and genders. We examine a qualitative case study of a Norwegian auto‐supply plant to ask why gender inequalities often persist on the shop floor, even in a gender‐progressive institutional context. The introduction of CNC machines in the plant made machining more like the female‐dominated assembly line in skill and strength demands, yet machining remained male‐dominated, with higher pay and greater investment in automation compared to the assembly line. The authors argue that persistent assumptions about economic value and gendered institutions and practices explain these unequal outcomes.

- [One Meeting That Should Not Be an Email: Manager–Direct Report One‐on‐One Meetings and Employee Engagement](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70134)
  - DOI: 10.1002/job.70134
  - Authors: Jon Gray; Jonathan Flinchum; Steven Rogelberg; Matthew J. Gerson; Nicholas J. Dilley; Liana Kreamer
  - Affiliations: University of North Carolina Charlotte North Carolina USA; Florida Institute of Technology Melbourne Florida USA
  - Published: 2026-11-15
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Organizational Behavior
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Building and maintaining an engaged workforce is a priority for organizations worldwide. While the antecedents and benefits of an engaged workforce are well documented, organizations continue to struggle to keep employees engaged. These ongoing difficulties point to the need for alternative, practical ways to foster engagement at work. To this end, we examine whether workplace meetings, specifically one‐on‐one (1:1) meetings between managers and direct reports, can foster engagement. Grounded in job demands and resources (JD‐R) theory, self‐determination theory (SDT) and Kahn's engagement theory, we hypothesized that 1:1 meeting quantity and quality promote engagement by satisfying employees' basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. We tested a moderated mediation model using two time‐separated online surveys ( N = 303). Results indicated that meeting quality, reflected in a managers' task‐ and relations‐oriented behaviors in 1:1 s, was more consistently associated with engagement than meeting quantity. These relationships remained after accounting for managers' use of the same behaviors outside 1:1 meetings, suggesting that 1:1 meetings provide a unique and meaningful context for individualized, synchronous support. Overall, the results identify 1:1 meetings as a practical tool managers can use to promote direct reports' engagement and advance both meeting science and engagement literature.

- [Negotiating Competing Expectations—Chiefs and Mineral Governance in Ghana](https://doi.org/10.1111/gove.70161)
  - DOI: 10.1111/gove.70161
  - Authors: Joel Agbesinyale
  - Affiliations: Department of Global Political Studies Malmö University Malmö Sweden
  - Published: 2026-11-15
  - Crossref journal: Governance
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT This paper examines what shapes chiefs' discretionary decision‐making and how they exercise authority within hybrid governance arrangements, focusing on Ghana's artisanal and small‐scale mining (ASM) sector. Existing scholarship recognizes that traditional authorities can strengthen or undermine state governance but offers limited explanations for variation in their practices. Drawing on interviews with chiefs, miners, and state officials, the paper develops a discretionary governance framework that integrates hybrid governance, access theory, and discretion. It argues that overlapping customary and statutory authority creates a discretionary space for chiefs to navigate competing expectations. Chiefs exercise this discretion through diverse governance practices, including restricting access to mining sites, mobilizing communities, and collaborating with state institutions, as well as facilitating access to land, negotiating informal payments, and shielding unauthorised miners from enforcement. These practices can reinforce, negotiate, circumvent, or undermine formal mining regulation. The paper advances debates on traditional authority and hybrid governance while challenging assumptions that constitutionally empowering chiefs necessarily strengthens mineral governance.
