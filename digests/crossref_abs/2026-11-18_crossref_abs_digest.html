2. Earnings Instability
Peter Ganong; Pascal Noel; Christina Patterson; Joseph Vavra; Alexander Weinberg
Quarterly Journal of Economics
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1093/qje/qjag059
University of Chicago and National Bureau of Economic Research ,; University of Chicago ,
Abstract:
Abstract This paper uses high-frequency administrative data to show that most U.S. workers experience substantial month-to-month fluctuations in pay, even within ongoing employment relationships. Monthly earnings instability is pervasive but is masked in annual data. Moreover, this instability is unequally distributed: lower-income, hourly workers face more instability than higher-income, salaried workers. Firm-driven fluctuations in hours account for a large share of this instability. Monthly earnings instability, in turn, increases spending volatility and job separations. Short-term earnings risk is therefore a significant feature of the labor market and falls disproportionately on the most financially fragile workers.
3. From Design to Disclosure
S Nageeb Ali; Andreas Kleiner; Kun Zhang
Review of Economic Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1093/restud/rdag125
Pennsylvania State University; University of Bonn; Auburn University and University of Queensland
Abstract:
Abstract We study the role of hard information in contractual and market settings in which the receiver can flexibly adjust allocations and transfers in response to the sender's disclosure. These settings include monopoly pricing, insurance contracting, and policy negotiations. Across these settings, the sender is worst off if she reveals her type completely because the receiver would then adjust the terms of trade to extract her surplus. Taking this feature as our central departure from the literature, we characterize the entire set of equilibrium payoffs in these disclosure games. We prove an equivalence result: a payoff profile can be approximated by an equilibrium of the disclosure game if and only if it can be achieved through information design. Thus, there exist equilibria in which the sender obtains approximately her commitment payoff.

6. Online Review Generation in the Digital Age: A Meta-Analytic Study of Online Review Behavior
Hong Hong; DaPeng Xu; Xiaoquan (Michael) Zhang
Information Systems Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1287/isre.2024.1510
Harbin Institute of Technology, School of Management, Harbin, China; Tsinghua University, Shenzhen International Graduate School, Shenzhen, China; Chinese University of Hong Kong, Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, Shatin, N.T., CUHK Business School, Hong Kong
Abstract:
A large body of literature on online review generation has investigated various antecedents to online review characteristics. However, the mixed and even contradictory findings in this domain have hampered a clear understanding of consumers’ online review behavior for both researchers and practitioners, thereby complicating actionable strategies. To address this issue, we conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis of 154 independent samples from 152 empirical papers, synthesizing the relationships between 28 antecedent factors and 12 review features, which we categorize into rating-based (evaluative) and non-rating (expressive) dimensions. Our results indicate that rating attributes are dominantly driven by stable reviewer tendencies and market-level reputation cues, whereas non-rating features—such as text length and image inclusion—are primarily influenced by reviewer social embeddedness and platform engagement. Crucially, we identify that inconsistencies in prior literature can be explained by critical boundary conditions, including platform type (business-hosted vs. third-party), goods type (service vs. tangible), and cultural background (Asia vs. North America). By distinguishing between evaluative and expressive review components, our study reconciles mixed findings and uncovers underlying mechanisms such as expectation-based evaluation and self-presentation. The findings provide a robust theoretical foundation for empirical model specification and offer actionable insights for retailers and platforms to optimize reputation management and system design. Ultimately, our work offers a holistic framework for understanding online review behavior and proposes a rich agenda for future research in the digital marketplace.
7. Decision Considerations Influencing Information Systems Modularity: A Multimethod Study of LEGO
Stefan Henningsson; Ben Eaton; Lorenz Törmer; Suprateek Sarker
Information Systems Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1287/isre.2022.0466
Copenhagen Business School, Digitalization, Frederiksberg, Capital Region, Denmark; Copenhagen Business School, Department of Digitalization, Frederiksberg, 2000, Denmark; University of Virginia McIntire School of Commerce, Information Technology, Charlottesville, Virginia, United States
Abstract:
Abstract. The maturing of concepts such as microservices and open application programming interfaces (APIs) has radically increased the practicality of fine-grained modularization in Information Systems (IS) architecture. These are critical technical advancements, given the central role of modularity in the ability of IS to transform and meet future organizational needs efficiently. Nevertheless, reports from practice suggest that, when presented with new opportunities for modularization, organizations face multifaceted decision criteria and do not necessarily embrace new approaches as may be expected. While emergent IS literature observes that organizations’ decisions on modularity in IS may involve competing concerns, understanding of the considerations involved and how they influence such decisions remains limited. To examine these considerations, we intensively study the experience of LEGO, a global toy company. Specifically, we sequentially apply multiple methods consisting of an exploratory inductive analysis of LEGO’s approach to IS modularity decisions over 36 months, which then informs a crisp-set qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) of 31 episodes of LEGO’s situated decision-making on IS modularity. Through the analysis, we find the rationale for a purposeful choice between two forms of modularity that differ in granularity of decomposition, rather than a linear pursuit of maximizing decomposability in IS architecture. We bring to the fore a perspective of IS modularity as situated decisions reconciling competing demands in pursuit of the right type of modularity, in the right place to achieve the right effect. Our findings add nuance to modularity in the context of IS architecture and explain the complex causality of IS modularity decisions.
8. The Asymmetry in Responsible Investing Preferences and Beliefs
Jacquelyn Humphrey; Shimon Kogan; Jacob S. Sagi; Laura T. Starks
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.06826
Business School, The University of Queensland, Brisbane, Queensland 4072, Australia; Arison School of Business, Reichman University, Herzliya 4610101, Israel; Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, North Carolina 27599; McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712
Abstract:
Empirical stylized facts in the literature concerning “sin” versus “angel” stocks display marked asymmetries in investor behavior. Through a set of experiments, we examine whether such biases can be micro-founded via individuals’ preferences and belief formations. With respect to portfolio allocation decisions, we find that the responses to negative environmental and social externalities are several times larger in magnitude than their responses to comparable positive externalities, ceteris paribus. Negative externalities also modestly increase pessimism about future investment performance, whereas positive externalities have little discernible effect on beliefs. This asymmetry is pervasive across our sample, exhibits heterogeneity across individuals, and is comparable to or greater in magnitude than estimates observed in loss aversion analyses. These findings help rationalize several existing stylized empirical facts documented in the responsible investing literature and carry implications for the growing body of theoretical work modeling the role of nonpecuniary investor preferences in asset pricing. 9. Opinion Trumps Fact: Entry and Competition in Online Political News
Aaron Chatterji; Sharique Hasan; William D. Miles; Dror Shvadron
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01487
Fuqua School of Business, Duke University, Durham, North Carolina 27708; and NBER, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02138; Fuqua School of Business, Duke University, Durham, North Carolina 27708; Rotman School of Management, University of Toronto, Toronto, Ontario M5S 3E6, Canada
Abstract:
This paper examines how new entrants influenced content strategies in the U.S. online political news business from 2014 to 2021. Using a novel measure of article opinionation that captures the extent to which an article displays editorializing and persuasive language, we provide correlational evidence that new entrants produce articles that are more opinionated than those of incumbents, even when reporting on the same event. We then show that there is an economic rationale for this content strategy: opinionated articles receive more engagement on Twitter, and this engagement translates to increased webpage visits, crucial for advertising revenue. Consistent with these incentives, we provide evidence that news providers increase the average opinion level in their articles in response to competition and advertiser pressure. These shifts are accompanied by a possibly unintended consequence: opinionated content is associated with greater anger and hate speech in social media engagement. 10. Human Performance at Solving 0-1 Knapsack Problems: Results from a Large-Scale Field Experiment
Sumit Agarwal; Joel Goh; Wenlan Qian; Xin Zou
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2023.00747
NUS Business School, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119245; NUS Business School, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119245; and NUS Global Asia Institute, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119076; and NUS Institute of Operations Research and Analytics, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117602; NUS Business School, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119245; and NUS Institute of Real Estate and Urban Studies, National University of Singapore, Singapore; School of Business, Hong Kong Baptist University, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong SAR
Abstract:
The 0-1 knapsack problem is a fundamental problem of discrete choice that permeates many diverse fields and applications, but relatively little is known about what instances human solvers find more difficult than others. This paper reports the results of the first large-scale randomized controlled field experiment investigating this question, conducted in partnership with a global mobile gaming platform. A total of 100,000 different knapsack instances were predesigned for this study and randomly assigned to each player attempt at the knapsack problem. For our base-case analysis, there were 17,950,838 distinct player accounts who made a total of 150,284,195 submitted solution attempts. Contrary to expectations, players performed better on problems that were rated as more difficult by two traditional measures of knapsack difficulty (the Pearson correlation between value and weight vectors and Sahni complexity), but performed worse on problem instances that featured large optimality gaps from a suite of heuristic solution algorithms. These suggest that optimality gaps can serve as promising measures of human-perceived knapsack difficulty and potentially could be used to measure human-perceived problem difficulty for other types of discrete choice problems. We found evidence that players improved their performance with repeated attempts at the knapsack task overall, but less-experienced players of the mobile game improved more quickly than more-experienced players. Through the construction of a new measure of human-perceived difficulty rating for each knapsack instance, we found evidence that players who were assigned to instances with a higher difficulty rating in the past tended to perform better on future attempts. 11. Does Feedback Enhance Diversity in Selection Decisions?
Jose A. Cervantez; Sophia L. Pink; Linda W. Chang; Aneesh Rai; Katherine L. Milkman
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01479
The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19104; Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742
Abstract:
Some organizations provide employees with feedback on the demographics of people they have chosen to spotlight, hire, and promote, hoping to spur more diverse future selections. However, it is an open question whether purely descriptive feedback accomplishes this. We theorize and show that descriptive feedback highlighting low past selection rates of women or racial minorities (e.g., “5% of previous experts interviewed were women”) triggers a motivation to respond without prejudice, increasing the subsequent selection of members of underrepresented groups. Conversely, providing descriptive feedback about low selection rates of people with other attributes (e.g., areas of expertise, geographical location) that do not evoke image or reputational concerns has less influence on future decisions. We demonstrate these patterns across seven preregistered experiments (total n = 6,502), including one framed field experiment (Study 2). One study identifies motivation to respond without prejudice as a mediator of the effect of descriptive feedback about past choices on future selections of women and racial minorities. Another study shows that when women are overrepresented, the effect of descriptive feedback reverses in direction, increasing the subsequent selection of men (due to concerns about fairness), but this effect is significantly smaller in magnitude than the feedback effect produced when women are underrepresented. Practically, the effects of descriptive feedback were 45%–80% as large as simulating a strict quota requiring that at least one woman or racial minority be selected for a given opportunity, highlighting that descriptive feedback is a light-touch but potentially powerful intervention for promoting diversity. This paper was accepted by George Wu, behavioral economics and decision analysis. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.01479 .

Abstract:
We analyze novel data on corporate contributions to ballot initiatives at the U.S. state level. Firms make large contributions to ballot measure campaigns in favor of or against specific initiatives. They contribute significantly more to business initiatives compared with ideological initiatives. Corporate campaign spending is systematically associated with the likelihood that a ballot measure passes. Contributing firms experience changes in sales growth following the passage of important ballot measures. Following successful (failed) initiatives, all firms in affected industries exhibit significantly more positive (negative) cumulative abnormal returns around the election, but contributing firms do more so than peers. Abstract:
We study a setting in which personal acquisition of information is costly and maintaining personal contacts to share information socially is also costly. 14. Influence Without Authority: Bottom-Up Normalization of Organizational Corruption
Patricia Helena Hein; Shahzad (Shaz) Ansari
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2023.18274
Western University, Ivey Business School, London, Canada; University of Cambridge, Judge Business School, Cambridge, Cambridgeshire, United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland
Abstract:
Research on organizational corruption has largely portrayed normalization as a top-down, management-driven process. We know less about how employees with limited formal authority can escalate wrongdoing beyond isolated acts and drive its normalization. Drawing on a qualitative study of a German recruitment firm’s practices during the COVID-19 pandemic, we develop a three-phase model of bottom-up normalization of organizational wrongdoing. Less powerful actors first engage in tolerated rule-bending to demonstrate competence and indispensability and build influence through informal networks. When disruption threatens this influence and legitimate alternatives are unavailable, they may escalate to more serious wrongdoing to safeguard their standing by framing new opportunities as extensions of familiar rule-bending. Normalization then depends on their ability to sustain a cohesive coalition, secure support from actors with formal authority, and contain resistance. We make three contributions. First, we advance a relational view of corruption by showing how central positions in informal networks enable actors lacking formal authority to mobilize support across hierarchical levels and normalize wrongdoing. Second, we show how organizational corruption can become a means of building and preserving social influence, fostering a sense of accomplishment rather than guilt or shame. Third, we identify a disruption-driven pathway to normalization in which threats to less powerful actors’ social influence precipitate abrupt escalation from tolerated rule-bending to more serious wrongdoing.
15. Silent female representation and tech entrepreneurial project evaluation: The role of evaluators' genders
Yan “Anthea” Zhang; Lun Li; Jizhen Li; Xiwei Yi
Strategic Management Journal
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1002/smj.70143
School of Business Rice University Houston Texas USA; Business School Beijing Normal University Beijing China; School of Economics and Management Tsinghua University Beijing China; Guanghua School of Management Peking University Beijing China
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary We examine how the nature of female representation in tech entrepreneurial projects may be related to the evaluations of the projects. We argue that silent female representation —i.e., women are present but do not speak in representing projects—may reinforce the stereotyped view of women, resulting in lower project evaluations relative to male‐only projects, whereas active female representation may not lead to evaluation penalty. We further predict that the negative association between silent female representation and project evaluation is stronger when the evaluators are women than when they are men. Conducted in a unique setting wherein female and male evaluators on panels, independently and confidentially, rated funding applications represented by various mixes of male and female entrepreneurs, our study provides evidence that is consistent with these predictions. Managerial Summary It is widely recognized that female entrepreneurs face greater challenges than their male counterparts when seeking external financial resources in the tech sector. This funding disparity may start from evaluation disparity. We find that projects with silent female representation—i.e., women are present but do not speak—receive lower evaluations than male‐only projects, whereas projects with active female representation receive evaluations comparable to male‐only projects. We also find that female evaluators are more likely than male evaluators to penalize projects with silent female representation. Our research offers valuable insights into strategies for fostering gender equality in traditionally male‐dominated fields.
16. Should birds of a feather flock together: How public criticism shapes pressures for sustainability conformity and differentiation
Nareuporn (Bell) Piyasinchai; Matthew Grimes
Strategic Management Journal
ABS 4* | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1002/smj.70144
Department of Organization Studies Tilburg University Tilburg the Netherlands; Cambridge Judge Business School University of Cambridge Cambridge UK
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary While firms face pressure to engage in Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) practices, it is unclear how they should specifically engage given the breadth of sustainability‐related concerns. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness and social evaluations research, we argue that public criticism directed toward an industry creates oscillating pressures for firms to conform with or deviate from industry ESG norms. Using data on 8176 global companies from 2013 to 2022, we find that although ESG conformity benefits investor evaluations, once industry peers' public criticism accumulates beyond a threshold, ESG differentiation is rewarded, particularly when firms increase material ESG practices and reduce immaterial ones. Our study reveals how public criticism dynamically reshapes the evaluative processes by which investors determine firm legitimacy, bridging optimal distinctiveness and social evaluations literatures. Managerial Summary Our research indicates that firms often adopt similar ESG practices as their industry peers, which usually enhances investor evaluations. However, this conformity can backfire and become a liability when industry peers face significant criticism. In such scenarios, firms that adopt distinctive ESG practices compared to the industry tend to receive greater rewards, especially when differentiation involves increasing material ESG practices and reducing immaterial ones. Therefore, while adopting ESG norms can be beneficial, managers should be vigilant of public opinion and ready to deviate from those norms when necessary.
17. The Impact of Mandatory Audit Firm Rotation on Competitive Pricing Strategies for New Audit Engagements: Evidence From the European Union
Ditmir Sufaj; Simon Dekeyser; Marleen Willekens
Contemporary Accounting Research
ABS 4 | 2026-11-18 | DOI 10.1111/1911-3846.70102
KU Leuven Leuven Belgium
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Mandatory audit firm rotation was introduced in the European Union (EU), in part, to make the audit market more dynamic by bringing audit engagements to market more frequently. However, it creates a countervailing economic force: By limiting the duration of auditor–client relationships, mandatory rotation shortens the period over which auditors can recoup first‐year fee discounts through fees earned in subsequent years. Consistent with this argument, we find that first‐year fee discounts decline substantially after mandatory firm rotation took effect in the EU, particularly in countries with stricter limits on auditor tenure. Thus, greater opportunities to compete for new engagements do not necessarily translate into greater first‐year fee discounts. Our findings highlight that regulators should consider not only how frequently audit engagements are brought to market but also how limits on auditor tenure shape the pricing incentives associated with competing for new clients.

19. The entrepreneur as ecosystem architect: Economic foundations for strategic entrepreneurship in the age of <scp>AI</scp>
David J. Teece
Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal
ABS 4 | 2026-11-17 | DOI 10.1002/sej.70066
Graduate School University of California, Berkeley Berkeley California USA; Berkeley Research Group Emeryville California USA
Abstract:
Abstract Research Summary This paper develops an alternative foundation for understanding strategic entrepreneurship in the age of artificial intelligence (AI) by joining entrepreneurship research with the profiting from innovation model and the dynamic capabilities framework. It argues that the economic function of the entrepreneur is increasingly that of an ecosystem architect, exercising judgment under uncertainty to orchestrate complementary assets and capabilities across organizational boundaries. AI dramatically lowers the cost of building software thereby lowering product development costs but may raise the barriers to scaling and sustaining competitive advantage.
