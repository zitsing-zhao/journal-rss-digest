- [Are client ties pre‐entry resources? Performance implications of client tie diversification](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.70119)
  - DOI: 10.1002/smj.70119
  - Authors: Heejung Byun
  - Affiliations: Management and Organization Department, Smeal College of Business The Pennsylvania State University University Park Pennsylvania USA
  - Published: 2026-08-23
  - Crossref journal: Strategic Management Journal
  - Abstract:
    Abstract Research Summary This study investigates client tie diversification, where firms enter markets with existing clients. Recognizing the theoretical basis for both positive and negative performance implications, the paper adopts a question‐driven approach and discovers a strong negative correlation between client tie diversification and firm performance. Using data from the US federal lobbying industry during the creation of the Homeland Security issue market post‐9/11, I find that lobbying firms entering the new market with existing clients underperform compared to other firms. This underperformance is associated with over‐embeddedness with clients and agency costs from dispersed client tie ownership among lobbyists. These findings challenge the notion that client ties are readily fungible and highlight relational and agency‐related complexities that can lead firms to pursue value‐destroying diversification. Managerial Summary Do firms perform better when they diversify into new markets with their existing clients, or by pursuing new opportunities independently? This study found that firms that enter new markets alongside their current clients often underperform compared to those that do not. While it might seem beneficial to leverage existing client relationships, this approach can push firms into areas where they lack capabilities, create inefficiencies, and complicate relationships with other clients, leading to weaker performance. Managers should be cautious about letting client needs drive diversification decisions. Prioritizing current clients may offer short‐term gains but can limit the firm's ability to explore new markets and achieve long‐term growth. Strategic choices should balance client requests with the firm's own strengths and future prospects.

- [“Menopausal, Depleted, and Nostalgic”: The Bittersweet Effects of Menopause for Women Leaders](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70126)
  - DOI: 10.1002/job.70126
  - Authors: Olga Epitropaki; Maria Kakarika
  - Affiliations: Durham University Business School Durham UK
  - Published: 2026-08-23
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Organizational Behavior
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Menopause is a natural but underexamined female experience in work settings, with significant implications for women in leadership positions. Drawing on self‐regulation perspectives, we develop a conceptual model examining how menopausal symptom severity shapes women leaders' experiences at work. We propose that severe menopausal symptoms act as a regulatory stressor that depletes leaders' psychological resources, triggering nostalgia for a premenopausal self. Nostalgia, in turn, can give rise to dual regulatory responses, enhancing empathy for subordinates on the one hand and fostering psychological withdrawal on the other. In Study 1 ( N = 341), using critical incidents, we found that menopausal symptom severity increased leader empathy and psychological withdrawal through a serial mediation of depletion and nostalgia. Study 2, employing a causal chain experiment with novel manipulations, supported the positive effects of (a) menopause severity on depletion (Study 2a, N = 203), (b) depletion on nostalgia (Study 2b, N = 132), and (c) nostalgia on empathy but not on psychological withdrawal (Study 2c, N = 115). We discuss implications for understanding women leaders' health and bodily experiences at work.

- [Gender Similarity in Hierarchical Work Relationships: A Meta‐Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.70123)
  - DOI: 10.1002/job.70123
  - Authors: Caitlin M. Porter; Cassondra Batz‐Barbarich; Nicole Alonso; Feigu Zhou
  - Affiliations: Department of Entrepreneurship and Management, Bloch School of Management University of Missouri‐Kansas City Kansas City Missouri USA; Department of Economics, Business, &amp; Finance, Mason School of Business Lake Forest College Lake Forest Illinois USA; Department of Business and Management Luiss University Rome Italy; Department of Management, Stephens College of Business University of Montevallo Montevallo Alabama USA
  - Published: 2026-08-23
  - Crossref journal: Journal of Organizational Behavior
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT Hierarchical work relationships, such as mentoring and supervisor relationships, provide access to key resources that promote employee well‐being and effectiveness. As such, scholars have investigated factors that enable relational success, with gender similarity being a key consideration. Despite abundant theoretical explanations for the importance of gender similarity in promoting high‐quality relationships and improved work outcomes, the empirical evidence is inconclusive. To add conceptual and empirical clarity to this literature, we meta‐analytically summarize the associations between gender similarity and a range of interpersonal (i.e., perceived similarity, relationship quality, and support) and work outcomes (i.e., work attitudes, performance, and turnover intentions). Moreover, we draw from social identity theory, as a basis for understanding when—or for whom—gender similarity in hierarchical work relationships is beneficial. Findings reveal that gender similarity promotes perceived similarity and relationship quality, but not work‐related outcomes. Moreover, junior women report higher perceived similarity and support in gender similar dyads than men. Finally, junior men report higher relationship quality in gender similar dyads, whereas junior women report comparable relationship quality across gender similar and dissimilar dyads. We discuss the implications for gender similarity and managing hierarchical work relationships.

  - Published: 2026-08-23

  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT The complex nature of top public managerial work is widely acknowledged in theory but rarely examined empirically. Existing research has mostly analyzed bivariate relationships among core components of top managerial work in government, including managers' attributes, practices, outcomes, and contexts. Drawing on a systems‐based framework, we investigate how effective top managerial work unfolds as a complex relational practice. We analyze seven cases of long‐serving, distinguished Chilean senior executives through a comprehensive and process‐oriented lens, based on 27 semi‐structured interviews with the managers and members of their personal and professional networks. Our findings indicate how the relational dynamics in which senior public executives engage shape their managerial attributes and practices. Specifically, strategizing, motivation building, and technical expertise are best understood as emerging primarily through interactions within agencies and with external sociopolitical environments. These results qualify perspectives that explain senior public management effectiveness by overemphasizing executives' individual attributes and views, and underscore the need for personnel policies and governance environments that equip managers with the capacities called for by a systems approach to top public management.

- [Does Gender Budgeting Improve Women's Well‐Being, Equality, and Empowerment?](https://doi.org/10.1111/padm.70077)
  - DOI: 10.1111/padm.70077
  - Authors: Marjorie De la Cruz; Milena I. Neshkova; Yoon‐Jung Choi
  - Affiliations: Department of Public Policy and Administration, School of International and Public Affairs Florida International University Miami Florida USA
  - Published: 2026-08-23
  - Crossref journal: Public Administration
  - Abstract:
    ABSTRACT The United Nations' fifth Sustainable Development Goal calls for gender equality and empowerment. Gender budgeting (GB) has been proposed as a potential mechanism to reduce gender disparities. This is a novel approach to budgeting that considers the impact of public spending on women and other underrepresented groups. Despite its growing popularity, spreading to over 80 countries since its launch in the 1980s, the effectiveness of GB remains less understood. This study tests whether GB has led to tangible improvements in women's well‐being, equality, and empowerment. We use an event study research design with staggered adoption to analyze data across 47 countries from 2000 to 2023. Our analyses of average and heterogeneous treatment effects reveal short‐ and long‐term impacts; notably, we find significant reductions in maternal mortality ratios and adolescent birth rates, as well as improvements in women's political empowerment.
