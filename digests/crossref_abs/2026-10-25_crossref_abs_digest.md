- [What Do “Most People” Believe? Race, Gender, and the Generalized Other](https://doi.org/10.1177/00031224261495117)
  - DOI: 10.1177/00031224261495117
  - Authors: Natasha Quadlin; Trenton D. Mize; Long Doan; Bianca Manago
  - Affiliations: University of California, Los Angeles; Purdue University; University of Maryland; Vanderbilt University
  - Published: 2026-10-24
  - Crossref journal: American Sociological Review
  - Abstract:
    When people form beliefs in social life, they often calibrate their own thinking against what they anticipate “most people” think. Although prior research demonstrates the importance of these inferences, scholars have yet to examine who is best proxied by “most people.” Given persistent inequalities in U.S. society that advantage White men, as well as theories of cultural hegemony, we hypothesize that “most people’s” imagined beliefs are closely proxied by those of White men. We test these ideas using an original survey experiment conducted with a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults ( N = 2,089) in which we adapt 64 core items from the General Social Survey. Respondents were randomly assigned to answer these questions either from the perspective of most people, or from the perspective of designated race-gender groups. Contrary to our hypothesis, we find strong and consistent evidence that White men are not seen as standard-bearers for most social issues. Instead, perceptions of what most people believe are highly aligned with the imagined beliefs of White women. Importantly, this pattern emerges not because White women are seen as influential, but because White women are perceived as less extreme than other groups. We also observe variation across respondents, such that respondents tend to center their own race-gender group in conceptualizing most people’s beliefs (with White women typically close behind).

- [Patently peculiar: Patents and innovation in the United Kingdom of the Netherlands](https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.70163)
  - DOI: 10.1111/ehr.70163
  - Authors: Homer Wagenaar; Christopher L. Colvin
  - Affiliations: Belgian Competition Authority Brussels Belgium; Queen's University Belfast Belfast UK
  - Published: 2026-10-24
  - Crossref journal: The Economic History Review
  - Abstract:
    Abstract We study the patent system of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands (1815–30), a post‐Napoleonic state which brought together an early industrializing region that subsequently became Belgium with a later‐industrializing one comprising what is now the Netherlands. The system formally distinguished between patents of invention and patents of importation, protecting both domestic inventions and the introduction of foreign technologies. It also granted administrators considerable discretion over patent fees, terms, and conditions. Using a new hand‐collected dataset covering all patent applications, including those granted, withdrawn, and rejected, we analyse application outcomes across sectors, regions, and patent types. We show that administrators systematically differentiated between patents of invention and importation in ways consistent with a technology policy that encouraged the domestic introduction of useful foreign technologies. We also find that administrative decisions reflected assessments of novelty, utility, and applicant characteristics, without evidence of systematic discrimination. Despite high fees, the system remained broadly accessible across social classes. Patents of invention were more commonly associated with technically skilled inventors, whereas patents of importation were more often sought by commercially oriented applicants. Overall, the regime combined accessibility with substantive screening and illustrates how discretionary patent administration could support technology transfer during a formative period of industrialization.
