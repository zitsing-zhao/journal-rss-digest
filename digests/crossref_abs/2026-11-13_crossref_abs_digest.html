2. Climate Risk Disclosure and Risk Sharing in Financial Markets
Kevin Smith
Review of Financial Studies
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhag009
Abstract:
Abstract In this paper, I model how climate risk disclosure impacts climate risk sharing among investors. I show that this disclosure can improve risk sharing by helping investors identify stocks that effectively hedge climate risk, and I analyze the disclosure’s impact on investors’ equilibrium portfolio choices. I then study firms’ voluntary disclosure incentives and analyze when disclosure mandates enhance risk sharing. Finally, I demonstrate that, by facilitating risk sharing, climate risk disclosure reduces the climate risk premium among stocks held by climate-focused investors. Hence, disclosure can lower the valuations of firms with low climate exposures and the overall market. (JEL: D82, D84, G12, G14)
3. The Workplace GenAI Usage Divide: A Genetics-Informed Cognitive and Metacognitive Account
Shuhua Sun; Shiyang Gong; Yinliang (Ricky) Tan
Information Systems Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/isre.2025.2709
Tulane University Freeman School of Business, Management, New Orleans, Louisiana, United States; Beijing Normal University, Business School, Beijing, China; China Europe International Business School, Freeman School of Business, Shanghai, China
Abstract:
Generative AI (GenAI) is increasingly deployed in organizations, yet employees differ substantially and persistently in how frequently and intensively they use it. This workplace “usage divide” raises a fundamental but underexplored question: Why do employees differ systematically in their use of GenAI, even under common formal access? We argue that this divide reflects a broader challenge for IS research: explaining the use of systems that are probabilistic rather than deterministic, and general-purpose yet uneven and evolving in their capabilities. In workplaces where employees remain accountable for task performance, these properties make the use of GenAI not a routine adoption decision but a judgment process in which employees assess the system’s credibility and determine where task-relevant expertise lies. Drawing on molecular genetics and metacognition research, we theorize that genetic propensity, indexed by the educational attainment polygenic score (PGS)—an aggregate genetic index associated with educational attainment—helps account for systematic variation in these judgments and, ultimately, persistent differences in workplace GenAI use. Specifically, we develop a parsimonious two-pathway model linking educational attainment PGS to GenAI use: a cognitive pathway involving formal educational attainment and perceptions of GenAI credibility, and a metacognitive pathway involving strategic mindset and metaknowledge of where task-relevant expertise lies. We test our model using a multisource dataset combining primary genetic data, survey measures, and 21 months of objective usage logs. The results show that educational attainment PGS, a broad distal genetic index, is associated with both GenAI use frequency and interaction intensity and provide theory-consistent evidence for the proposed pathways. Overall, our study offers a micro-foundational account of the persistent GenAI usage divide and advances IS theory on the use of probabilistic, general-purpose systems in the workplace.
4. Spatial Competition in Fast-Charging Networks
Philipp Artur Artur Kienscherf; Yixin Lu; Long He; Wolfgang Ketter
Information Systems Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/isre.2024.1246
University of Cologne, Faculty of Management, Economics and Social Sciences, Koln, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany; The George Washington University, School of Business, Washington, District of Columbia, United States; University of Notre Dame Mendoza College of Business, Information Technology, Analytics, and Operations, Notre Dame, Indiana, United States
Abstract:
Despite the steady growth of fast-charging networks under government subsidies, the impact of spatial and ownership structures of charging points on pricing strategies of charging point operators (CPOs) and consumer welfare is poorly understood. Given EVs’ limited range, the competition in a fast-charging network is inherently regional. It is unclear whether and under what circumstances CPOs’ competition can lead to regional monopoly, which may hinder the transition to electric mobility. In this paper, we develop a novel framework to examine the competitive dynamics in fast-charging networks that serve regional demand, incorporating delay costs arising from congestion and locational attractiveness of charging stations. At the core of our framework is a spatial competition model, which characterizes the strategic interactions between CPOs and consumers. To overcome tractability issues associated with large networks that arise from real-world settings, we propose a computationally efficient method to optimize CPOs’ pricing decisions. We instantiate the framework in Germany’s Deutschlandnetz, whose awarded regional network comprises 900 charging stations. Using demand projections for 2030, we conduct a series of counterfactual simulations. Our analysis generates four sets of findings. First, price exhibits a strong negative correlation with the local competitiveness level (measured by the density of charging stations), but shows no clear dependence on absolute demand levels. Second, CPOs’ profits are shaped by both local competitiveness and absolute demand. Third, a uniform, stringent price cap may create inefficiencies in network utilization and discourage infrastructure investment, particularly in less lucrative regions. Finally, spatial and ownership constraints in tender rules may fail to ensure competitive outcomes in large-scale charging networks, allowing CPOs to strategically select locations that strengthen their market power ex post. Taken together, these findings suggest that policymakers should enable regionally equitable access to fast charging while ensuring efficient network utilization.
5. Redeploying the Jockeys: The Intermediary Role of Venture Capital in Entrepreneurial Labor Markets
Zhongchen Hu
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-12 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2023.01626
The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen 518172, China
Abstract:
This paper explores how venture capitalists (VCs) add value by examining their role as labor market intermediaries. Using novel data, I show that VCs create internal labor markets (ILMs) to facilitate entrepreneurial mobility across their portfolio firms. Survey evidence suggests that VCs leverage private information about founders’ abilities to strategically match talent to portfolio firms, creating value for the recipient companies. Consistent with this mechanism of asymmetric learning, I find that (a) former VC partners continue to influence founder mobility, (b) ILM intensity positively predicts VC fund returns, and (c) the ILM effect is stronger when information asymmetry is greater. This paper was accepted by Lin William Cong, finance. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.01626 .
6. Effortful vs. Opportunistic Consumption of Discretionary Time: How Time-Reporting Decisions Influence Extra-Role Effort
Sophie Maussen
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-12 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.00955
Department of Accounting, Corporate Finance and Taxation, Ghent University, 9000 Ghent, Belgium
Abstract:
Employees often work under incomplete contracts, performing both contracted in-role tasks and valuable but noncontracted extra-role activities. Once employees have completed in-role tasks, discretionary time remains, which they may allocate to extra-role activities or consume opportunistically. This study examines how managers’ time-reporting decisions, as a less intrusive form of control, affect subordinates’ effort-allocation decisions. I propose two opposing predictions. First, managers’ control implementations may signal distrust, resulting in more opportunism and thus less extra-role effort. Second, I identify value signaling as a novel theoretical mechanism through which time-reporting decisions may increase extra-role effort. The main experiment manipulated two factors: the type of time report (aggregated versus disaggregated) and its implementation source (managerial choice versus random assignment) to isolate signaling effects from monitoring effects. Results reveal that when managers choose not to implement disaggregated time reporting—that is, reports that make discretionary time salient—subordinates significantly reduce their extra-role effort. Structural equation modeling shows that value signaling outweighs distrust signaling in driving employees’ effort-allocation decisions. In contrast, when the time-reporting format is randomly assigned, effort remains unchanged, underscoring the signaling role of managerial choice. An additional online experiment shows directionally consistent results with the main experiment. These findings reveal a hidden cost of not controlling and contribute to the literature on incomplete contracts, slack resources, and input-based controls in modern work contexts. This paper was accepted by Ranjani Krishnan, accounting. Funding: This work was supported by the Bijzonder Onderzoeksfonds UGent [Grant BOF/STA/202009/047]. Supplemental Material: The data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00955 .
7. Deregulation, Market Structure, and the Evolution of U.S. Banking
Emilio Bisetti; Stephen A. Karolyi; Stefan Lewellen
Management Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-12 | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2025.01128
Department of Finance, School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, New Territories, Hong Kong SAR; Department of Finance, Costello College of Business George Mason University, Fairfax, Virginia 22030; Department of Finance, The Pennsylvania State University, State College, Pennsylvania 16802
Abstract:
We construct novel network-based measures of U.S. state-level bank deregulation intensity that allow us to separately identify the effects of deregulation on competition and investment opportunities. In contrast to existing studies, we find that increased competition leads to higher deposit funding costs and a reduction in banks’ net interest margins and profitability. In response, banks shift their business models and persistently increase their risk taking. Our findings resolve conflicting evidence in the literature and support theories in which reductions in bank charter values lead to higher bank risk taking. This paper was accepted by Camelia Kuhnen, finance. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.01128 .
8. The Complexity of Scheduling Pickup and Delivery Jobs
Tianyu Wang; Desheng Wu; David L. Olson
Operations Research
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/opre.2026.2625
Beihang University, School of Economy and Management, Beijing, Beijing, China; UCAS, School of Economy and Management, Beijing, China; Lincoln, United States
Abstract:
Scheduling pickup and delivery jobs stands as a critical and challenging problem commonly encountered in time-sensitive logistics and transportation. Often, a large number of requests involving shipments from the same location are aggregated into either a fixed number of job queues, referred to as the queueing mode, or into a fixed number of sets, referred to as the pooling mode. Due to the time-sensitive nature, the objective of scheduling jobs by location and routing drivers is to minimize customers’ waiting times. While request aggregation is widely observed in platform economies and related research, the computational complexity of scheduling pickup and delivery jobs has not been studied. This paper presents a comprehensive complexity analysis of this problem. We prove that the problem is strongly NP-hard in the queueing mode, but is polynomially solvable in the pooling mode. Furthermore, we prove that both modes become polynomially solvable when minimizing total distance, as does the queueing mode with fixed number of drivers or scaled-length edges. Finally, we provide approximation guarantees and extend the analysis to bipartite graphs, identifying solvable variants with relaxed map-size constraints to enhance the applicability.
9. The Emergence of Psychological Safety or its Mirage
Patricia Satterstrom; Michaela Kerrissey
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.19549
New York University, Wagner, New York, New York, United States; Harvard University T H Chan School of Public Health, Health Policy and Management, Boston, Massachusetts, United States
Abstract:
Leaders often strive to foster psychological safety in teams, and yet it may not emerge. This study builds grounded theory on how psychological safety emerges, or not, on teams. We draw on longitudinal ethnographic data from a matched pair case study of two teams over two years, including verbatim meeting transcripts that elicit the micro-processes affecting psychological safety and interviews that elucidate team members’ interpretations. We identify an illusion initially present on both teams in which surface markers of psychological safety existed but team members nonetheless continued to avoid interpersonal risk—what we call a psychological safety mirage. This occurred because external pressures on leaders spurred them to take self-protective actions that inadvertently reinforced interpersonal risk, despite their continued efforts to invite contributions. One team was eventually able to break from this mirage through a three-phase de-risking process that facilitated psychological safety emergence: (1) collectively recognizing the underlying risk faced by team members, (2) de-risking contributions by offering opportunities to build skills, through which safety was demonstrated and reinforced, and (3) enacting psychological safety, with all team members contributing, reinforcing, and sharing in the benefits of reduced interpersonal risk. This paper advances theory on psychological safety by conceptualizing a team-level de-risking process model that facilitates its emergence and by characterizing its mirage.
10. How Firms Respond to Carbon Taxation
Arzi Adbi; Xiaoteng Wu
Organization Science
ABS 4* | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1287/orsc.2024.19313
National University of Singapore Business School, Strategy & Policy, Singapore, Singapore; Peking University Guanghua School of Management, Organization and Strategic Management, Beijing, China
Abstract:
Although environmental regulations can incentivize firms to reduce emissions, how firms adapt may depend not only on their organizational characteristics but also on the physical realities of their assets. This paper examines how multinational spread, an organizational feature in which firms own and manage similar production activities across countries, and the physical asset characteristics of production facilities jointly shape firms’ responses to carbon taxation. Using global, real-time satellite data on carbon emissions from 882 steel plants across 67 countries over 96 months (January 2015 to December 2022), we analyze operational adjustments firms can make to reduce carbon emissions: producing more cleanly by reducing emissions intensity of production or producing less by reducing asset utilization. We first examine how firms adapt to Canada’s carbon tax using a difference-in-differences (DID) design and then examine the impact of carbon taxes across countries using a stacked DID design. Our analysis reveals that, following Canada’s carbon tax, firms without multinational spread reduce carbon emissions primarily by lowering asset utilization; in contrast, firms with multinational spread additionally reduce emissions intensity of production. Importantly, our analysis of carbon taxes across countries shows that multinational spread alone does not dictate firm adaptation. Adaptation is fundamentally influenced by the physical technology on the ground: firms with multinational spread reduce emissions intensity of production and increase asset utilization at their clean (electric arc furnace) plants relative to dirty plants. Our study thus sheds light on how firms pursue different adaptation strategies at production facilities in response to carbon taxation.

13. Government Guarantees and Dynamic Debt Runs
Jacopo Magnani; Yabin Wang
Review of Finance
ABS 4 | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1093/rof/rfag055
Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) , Department of Economics
Abstract:
Abstract This study quantifies the impact of public guarantees on the stability of bank wholesale funding. We study the Chinese market for negotiable certificates of deposit (NCDs) around the first bank failure in two decades, the Baoshang event, which struck investors’ belief in government guarantees on wholesale bank debt. We document a sharp increase in NCD spreads, issuance failures, and runs, with weaker banks becoming more exposed to rollover risk after the event. Using a model of dynamic debt runs, we estimate that the perceived guarantee fell from 54% of creditor losses before the event to approximately zero after the event. The decline is largest for small banks, low-rated banks, and rural commercial banks. Our structural model also allows us to compare alternative policies aimed at stabilizing creditors’ willingness to roll over short-term debt.
14. Explaining Coexisting Trust and Distrust Transitions: The Case of Senior Doctors in the <scp>UK NHS</scp>
Branko Božič; Stacey Bushfield; Graeme Martin
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70190
NEOMA Business School; Edinburgh Napier University Business School; University of Dundee School of Business
Abstract:
Abstract The emergence of distrust in organizations damages working relationships and leads to poor organizational performance. We examine how senior doctors in the UK NHS transitioned from low trust/low distrust to low trust/high distrust in their managers and employer. Drawing on qualitative data from three time periods, we show, counter‐intuitively, how a positive crisis response by doctors' leaders during COVID‐19 intensified a long‐term downward trust–distrust trajectory. This response temporarily restored confidence and elevated expectations of a renewed psychological contract among doctors. When those expectations were later violated, scepticism and value incongruence grew, inciting negative expectations and a descent into distrust. We make three contributions to trust–distrust theory. First, we identify a previously unexplained pathway from coexisting low trust/low distrust to low trust/high distrust through a temporary state of high trust/low distrust. Second, we explain how ideological contract violations, perceived betrayals of core values and professional identities, drive the development of distrust. Third, we extend punctuated‐threshold explanations of distrust emergence by demonstrating that distrust can arise not only from accumulating negative experiences but also from the collapse of newly restored positive expectations. This has broader implications for the leadership of change, particularly the management of hope and expectations during crisis.
15. Constant Connectivity as Everyday Practice: Theorizing Through Problematization
Matthew B. Perrigino; Roshni Raveendhran; Ji Woon Ryu
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70188
Baruch College; University of Virginia; Portland State University
Abstract:
Abstract Despite a significant amount of research on constant connectivity , defined as the opportunity to remain connected to work at any time, from any place, we assert that there remains a disconnect between how constant connectivity is studied and how it unfolds in everyday occurrences. Using the miner‐prospector continuum, we problematize the extant literature to evaluate this assertion and develop new theory. Our mining reveals that constant connectivity studies rely on notions of time and place that insufficiently demarcate work–nonwork boundaries; prescribe constant connectivity as either wholly incompatible with enacting nonwork roles or inherently interfering with specific nonwork activities; and operationalize constant connectivity with vague ‘technology use’ constructs. In prospecting, we conceptualize constant connectivity as a set of sociomaterial practices that capture how: technology affords opportunities to engage in, and disengage from, work activity; constant connectivity and nonwork role enactment are compatible via the pursuit of work and nonwork activities in parallel or sequentially; and work–nonwork boundaries are socially constructed in ways that transcend time and place. Taken together, we extend considerations of constant connectivity beyond knowledge workers to any occupation (skilled trades; physical labour) in which individuals use technology to conduct aspects of work at any time, from any place.
16. AI and Stress
Thang Dang; Phuong Huynh
British Journal of Industrial Relations
ABS 4 | 2026-11-13 | DOI 10.1111/bjir.70084
Department of Economics University of Turku Turku Finland; University of Economics Ho Chi Minh City (UEH) Ho Chi Minh City Vietnam; University of Turku Turku Finland
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Given the spread of artificial intelligence (AI) tools at work, understanding their health implications is critically important. We use rich survey data from Finland and apply propensity score matching that accounts for observable differences between regular AI users and non‐users to estimate the relationship between reported AI usage at work and workers’ reported stress. Regular AI use is associated with a 4.4 percentage point higher probability of reporting stress, equivalent to a 25.6% increase relative to baseline. Comparing two competing explanations, we find that this pattern is more consistent with heavier work demands than with concerns about job loss: AI use is associated with more unpaid work outside working hours and, less precisely, with intense work absorption, but not with job insecurity. Exploratory subgroup estimates are larger and individually significant for men, non‐supervisors, unionized workers and employees in medium‐sized workplaces, although the differences across subgroups are not statistically significant.
