  - Authors: Shuhua Sun; Shiyang Gong; Yinliang (Ricky) Tan

  - Published: 2026-11-13

  - Abstract:
    Generative AI (GenAI) is increasingly deployed in organizations, yet employees differ substantially and persistently in how frequently and intensively they use it. This workplace “usage divide” raises a fundamental but underexplored question: Why do employees differ systematically in their use of GenAI, even under common formal access? We argue that this divide reflects a broader challenge for IS research: explaining the use of systems that are probabilistic rather than deterministic, and general-purpose yet uneven and evolving in their capabilities. In workplaces where employees remain accountable for task performance, these properties make the use of GenAI not a routine adoption decision but a judgment process in which employees assess the system’s credibility and determine where task-relevant expertise lies. Drawing on molecular genetics and metacognition research, we theorize that genetic propensity, indexed by the educational attainment polygenic score (PGS)—an aggregate genetic index associated with educational attainment—helps account for systematic variation in these judgments and, ultimately, persistent differences in workplace GenAI use. Specifically, we develop a parsimonious two-pathway model linking educational attainment PGS to GenAI use: a cognitive pathway involving formal educational attainment and perceptions of GenAI credibility, and a metacognitive pathway involving strategic mindset and metaknowledge of where task-relevant expertise lies. We test our model using a multisource dataset combining primary genetic data, survey measures, and 21 months of objective usage logs. The results show that educational attainment PGS, a broad distal genetic index, is associated with both GenAI use frequency and interaction intensity and provide theory-consistent evidence for the proposed pathways. Overall, our study offers a micro-foundational account of the persistent GenAI usage divide and advances IS theory on the use of probabilistic, general-purpose systems in the workplace.

  - Published: 2026-11-13
  - Crossref journal: Information Systems Research
  - Abstract:
    Despite the steady growth of fast-charging networks under government subsidies, the impact of spatial and ownership structures of charging points on pricing strategies of charging point operators (CPOs) and consumer welfare is poorly understood. Given EVs’ limited range, the competition in a fast-charging network is inherently regional. It is unclear whether and under what circumstances CPOs’ competition can lead to regional monopoly, which may hinder the transition to electric mobility. In this paper, we develop a novel framework to examine the competitive dynamics in fast-charging networks that serve regional demand, incorporating delay costs arising from congestion and locational attractiveness of charging stations. At the core of our framework is a spatial competition model, which characterizes the strategic interactions between CPOs and consumers. To overcome tractability issues associated with large networks that arise from real-world settings, we propose a computationally efficient method to optimize CPOs’ pricing decisions. We instantiate the framework in Germany’s Deutschlandnetz, whose awarded regional network comprises 900 charging stations. Using demand projections for 2030, we conduct a series of counterfactual simulations. Our analysis generates four sets of findings. First, price exhibits a strong negative correlation with the local competitiveness level (measured by the density of charging stations), but shows no clear dependence on absolute demand levels. Second, CPOs’ profits are shaped by both local competitiveness and absolute demand. Third, a uniform, stringent price cap may create inefficiencies in network utilization and discourage infrastructure investment, particularly in less lucrative regions. Finally, spatial and ownership constraints in tender rules may fail to ensure competitive outcomes in large-scale charging networks, allowing CPOs to strategically select locations that strengthen their market power ex post. Taken together, these findings suggest that policymakers should enable regionally equitable access to fast charging while ensuring efficient network utilization.

    Employees often work under incomplete contracts, performing both contracted in-role tasks and valuable but noncontracted extra-role activities. Once employees have completed in-role tasks, discretionary time remains, which they may allocate to extra-role activities or consume opportunistically. This study examines how managers’ time-reporting decisions, as a less intrusive form of control, affect subordinates’ effort-allocation decisions. I propose two opposing predictions. First, managers’ control implementations may signal distrust, resulting in more opportunism and thus less extra-role effort. Second, I identify value signaling as a novel theoretical mechanism through which time-reporting decisions may increase extra-role effort.   - DOI: 10.1287/opre.2026.2625
  - Authors: Tianyu Wang; Desheng Wu; David L. Olson
  - Affiliations: Beihang University, School of Economy and Management, Beijing, Beijing, China; UCAS, School of Economy and Management, Beijing, China; Lincoln, United States
  - Published: 2026-11-13
  - Crossref journal: Operations Research
  - Abstract:
    Scheduling pickup and delivery jobs stands as a critical and challenging problem commonly encountered in time-sensitive logistics and transportation. Often, a large number of requests involving shipments from the same location are aggregated into either a fixed number of job queues, referred to as the queueing mode, or into a fixed number of sets, referred to as the pooling mode. Due to the time-sensitive nature, the objective of scheduling jobs by location and routing drivers is to minimize customers’ waiting times. While request aggregation is widely observed in platform economies and related research, the computational complexity of scheduling pickup and delivery jobs has not been studied. This paper presents a comprehensive complexity analysis of this problem. We prove that the problem is strongly NP-hard in the queueing mode, but is polynomially solvable in the pooling mode. Furthermore, we prove that both modes become polynomially solvable when minimizing total distance, as does the queueing mode with fixed number of drivers or scaled-length edges. Finally, we provide approximation guarantees and extend the analysis to bipartite graphs, identifying solvable variants with relaxed map-size constraints to enhance the applicability.

  - DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2024.19549
  - Authors: Patricia Satterstrom; Michaela Kerrissey
  - Affiliations: New York University, Wagner, New York, New York, United States; Harvard University T H Chan School of Public Health, Health Policy and Management, Boston, Massachusetts, United States
  - Published: 2026-11-13
  - Crossref journal: Organization Science
  - Abstract:
    Leaders often strive to foster psychological safety in teams, and yet it may not emerge. This study builds grounded theory on how psychological safety emerges, or not, on teams. We draw on longitudinal ethnographic data from a matched pair case study of two teams over two years, including verbatim meeting transcripts that elicit the micro-processes affecting psychological safety and interviews that elucidate team members’ interpretations. We identify an illusion initially present on both teams in which surface markers of psychological safety existed but team members nonetheless continued to avoid interpersonal risk—what we call a psychological safety mirage. This occurred because external pressures on leaders spurred them to take self-protective actions that inadvertently reinforced interpersonal risk, despite their continued efforts to invite contributions. One team was eventually able to break from this mirage through a three-phase de-risking process that facilitated psychological safety emergence: (1) collectively recognizing the underlying risk faced by team members, (2) de-risking contributions by offering opportunities to build skills, through which safety was demonstrated and reinforced, and (3) enacting psychological safety, with all team members contributing, reinforcing, and sharing in the benefits of reduced interpersonal risk. This paper advances theory on psychological safety by conceptualizing a team-level de-risking process model that facilitates its emergence and by characterizing its mirage.

- [How Firms Respond to Carbon Taxation](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2024.19313)
  - DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2024.19313
  - Authors: Arzi Adbi; Xiaoteng Wu

  - Published: 2026-11-13
  - Crossref journal: Organization Science
  - Abstract:
    Although environmental regulations can incentivize firms to reduce emissions, how firms adapt may depend not only on their organizational characteristics but also on the physical realities of their assets. This paper examines how multinational spread, an organizational feature in which firms own and manage similar production activities across countries, and the physical asset characteristics of production facilities jointly shape firms’ responses to carbon taxation. Using global, real-time satellite data on carbon emissions from 882 steel plants across 67 countries over 96 months (January 2015 to December 2022), we analyze operational adjustments firms can make to reduce carbon emissions: producing more cleanly by reducing emissions intensity of production or producing less by reducing asset utilization. We first examine how firms adapt to Canada’s carbon tax using a difference-in-differences (DID) design and then examine the impact of carbon taxes across countries using a stacked DID design.   - Abstract:
    Abstract The emergence of distrust in organizations damages working relationships and leads to poor organizational performance. We examine how senior doctors in the UK NHS transitioned from low trust/low distrust to low trust/high distrust in their managers and employer. Drawing on qualitative data from three time periods, we show, counter‐intuitively, how a positive crisis response by doctors' leaders during COVID‐19 intensified a long‐term downward trust–distrust trajectory. This response temporarily restored confidence and elevated expectations of a renewed psychological contract among doctors. This has broader implications for the leadership of change, particularly the management of hope and expectations during crisis.

  - Abstract:
    Abstract Despite a significant amount of research on constant connectivity , defined as the opportunity to remain connected to work at any time, from any place, we assert that there remains a disconnect between how constant connectivity is studied and how it unfolds in everyday occurrences. Using the miner‐prospector continuum, we problematize the extant literature to evaluate this assertion and develop new theory. Our mining reveals that constant connectivity studies rely on notions of time and place that insufficiently demarcate work–nonwork boundaries; prescribe constant connectivity as either wholly incompatible with enacting nonwork roles or inherently interfering with specific nonwork activities; and operationalize constant connectivity with vague ‘technology use’ constructs.
