1. The Cost of Regulatory Inaction: Evidence from IFRS Nonadoption
Miao Liu; Wanrong Xu; Rachel Xi Zhang
Accounting Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.2308/tar-2023-0304
Boston College; Southern University of Science and Technology; National University of Singapore
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Although extensive research examines the costs and benefits of adopting new disclosure regulations, little addresses the consequences of regulatory inaction. We investigate whether firms in nonadopting countries incur additional information losses, beyond the forgone benefits, when their foreign peers adopt International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). Nonadopters bear an absolute liquidity cost as foreign peers adopt IFRS, consistent with negative externalities from IFRS and accompanying reforms. Exploiting heterogeneity across nonadopting countries, we observe more pronounced liquidity declines among firms with greater institutional ownership overlap with IFRS peers. This effect appears more pronounced when IFRS peers are domiciled in countries with concurrent enforcement reforms or greater comparability improvements. Further evidence shows shared intermediaries—investors and analysts—reduce information production for nonadopters as other firms in their portfolios adopt IFRS. Overall, regulatory inaction degrades information environments: resources shift to the new regime, worsening liquidity for firms in the old regime. Data Availability: Data used in this study are available from commercial sources cited in the text. JEL Classifications: G1; K22; M41; M48.
2. National Audit Oversight and Foreign Investment
James J. Blann; Phillip T. Lamoreaux; Beverly J. Larson
Accounting Review
ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.2308/tar-2024-0320
Georgia Institute of Technology; Arizona State University; University of Florida
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Public audit oversight bodies have become ubiquitous following the establishment of the U.S. PCAOB in 2002. However, despite their cost and prominence, little is known of their economic implications, if any. We examine the relation between establishment of public audit oversight bodies (POBs) and cross-border investment. Using a difference-in-differences design, we find that after a country establishes a POB, the country attracts more foreign capital. Our results suggest that total foreign portfolio investment (FPI) increases by approximately $11 billion for the average country that establishes a POB. The impact of POBs on foreign investment is dependent on the effectiveness of the POB in improving audit quality and the strength of a country’s existing institutional environment. Our study provides evidence of real economic effects of public audit oversight around the world, which adds to the literature on auditor oversight and to the broader literature on economic consequences of regulation. Data Availability: Data are available from the public sources cited in the text. JEL Classifications: M42; M48; F21; L51; G15; G18.
3. Is Informed Short Selling Concentrated in Family Firms? Evidence from a Reinvestigation
FERHAT AKBAS; MINJIE HUANG; FELIX MESCHKE
Journal of Finance
ABS 4* | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/jofi.70098
Abstract:
ABSTRACT We reinvestigate Anderson, Reeb, and Zhao's findings that informed short selling is unique to family firms and driven by information leakage from internal disputes. Using the same firm sample and more comprehensive short‐selling data, we find that short selling predicts future returns in both family and nonfamily firms. We also find no significant difference in abnormal short selling before negative earnings announcements. In extended sample periods, short selling continues to predict returns for nonfamily firms under both ownership‐based and familial‐link definitions. Overall, our evidence suggests that short sellers are broadly informed and that family firms are not uniquely vulnerable.
4. Looking the Part? How Candidates’ Race and Attire Shape Employers’ Hiring Decisions in a Low-Wage Labor Market
Tiantian Yang; Jiayi Bao; Tianna Barnes; Ming D. Leung
Administrative Science Quarterly
ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1177/00018392261493033
University of Pennsylvania; Texas A&amp;M University; Dartmouth College; University of California, Irvine
Abstract:
Racial disparities in employment persist in the United States, particularly in low-wage labor markets in which employers frequently cite self-presentation, especially attire, as a basis for hiring decisions. Yet, whether improving professional attire yields equal returns across racial groups remains largely unexamined. Drawing on status characteristics theory, we argue that professional attire functions as an ambiguous proxy for unobservable traits such as motivation and reliability, making it susceptible to racialized interpretations. We predict that professional attire will yield weaker hiring returns for Black candidates than for their White counterparts and that perceived professionalism mediates this gap. We test these arguments using data from a gig-staffing platform comprising over 1 million job applications with profile photos, supplemented by a preregistered experiment that manipulates attire within candidates, using two photos of each candidate: one in professional attire, one in casual. The results converge across field and experimental data. Professional attire improves hiring prospects overall but yields substantially smaller returns for Black candidates. Moderated mediation tests confirm that perceived professionalism drives this disparity, such that race discounts how attire translates into perceived professionalism and how perceived professionalism translates into hiring. The racial gap attenuates in customer-facing roles in which appearance is task relevant. These findings challenge supply-side accounts of hiring inequality and reveal how a demand-side interpretation of equivalent signals perpetuates racial disadvantages.

6. Rethinking Supplier Integration in Humanitarian Supply Chains: Feasibility and Constraints From an International Humanitarian Organization Perspective
Jihee Kim; Nezih Altay; Stephen Pettit; Anthony Beresford
Journal of Operations Management
ABS 4* | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1002/joom.70061
School of Management Anglia Ruskin University Cambridge UK; Department of Management and Entrepreneurship DePaul University Chicago Illinois USA; Logistics and Operations Management Cardiff University Cardiff UK
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Despite the widely recognized importance of integrative practices in commercial supply chain operations, their application in humanitarian supply chains (HSCs) remains underexplored. HSCs differ from commercial supply chains in their relational dynamics and integration processes, operating under extreme uncertainty, demand volatility, infrastructural disruption, and resource scarcity across a complex web of actors. Using an embedded single‐case design, this study assesses supplier integration (SI) in humanitarian operations in a leading international humanitarian organization (IHO), as well as the IHO's relationships with eight key suppliers. Drawing on the relational lenses of power, trust, and commitment, distinct integration patterns were identified across different supplier types. The findings highlight humanitarian‐specific contextual constraints and are used to inform a typology of SI feasibility encompassing feasible, selectively feasible, and low‐feasibility integration. This typology provides a foundation for guiding supplier relationship management in complex humanitarian contexts. This study offers a framework for understanding how relational and structural conditions shape integration in humanitarian settings and underscores the need for IHOs to adopt context‐appropriate supplier management approaches that reflect the unique dynamics of humanitarian operations.
7. Can citizen election observation increase public confidence in US elections?
Jennie Barker; Oren Samet; Susan D. Hyde
American Journal of Political Science
Not ABS-rated; grouped with ABS 4* | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/ajps.70095
SNF Agora Academy Postdoctoral Fellow, SNF Agora Institute Johns Hopkins University Baltimore Maryland USA; Assistant Professor, Department of Political Science Rice University Houston Texas USA; Robson Professor, Department of Political Science University of California Berkeley California USA
Abstract:
Abstract Nonpartisan citizen election observation plays an important role in promoting the credibility of electoral processes around the world. This article assesses whether nonpartisan observation can shape attitudes among the US public, randomizing information about nonpartisan domestic observers in a series of survey experiments fielded to nearly 10,000 respondents. These included both hypothetical vignettes and information about a real‐world nonpartisan domestic observation mission undertaken by The Carter Center during the 2022 midterm elections in Fulton County, Georgia. The results show that information about positive assessments by nonpartisan observers of the hypothetical and Fulton County elections increased respondents' confidence in electoral results and processes, and also made respondents more likely to identify the officially announced winner over the candidate they favored as the candidate who won. Alternative ways of framing observers were largely inconsequential.

Mathematics and Statistics Department, Toulouse School of Economics, Université Toulouse Capitole; School of Mathematical Sciences, Tel Aviv University; Department of Economics and Decision Sciences, HEC Paris
Abstract:
We study a strategic experimentation game with exponential bandits in which experiment outcomes are private. The equilibrium amount of experimentation is always weakly higher than with public outcomes. Moreover, in pure equilibria, experimentation is never significantly below the socially optimal level and may be strictly higher. We provide a tight bound on the extent of over‐experimentation. 12. Semistability in roommate problems
P. Jean-Jacques Herings; Yu Zhou
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6562
Department of Econometrics and Operations Research, Tilburg University; College of Business, Southern University of Science and Technology; Graduate School of Economics, Nagoya University
Abstract:
We study the roommate problem allowing for preferences with indifferences and agents remaining single. A stable outcome need not exist. We introduce semi‐stability, a novel notion that guarantees existence and restores stability through minimal policy or group interventions. We build an equilibrium foundation for semistability by showing its equivalence to a newly proposed choice‐based equilibrium concept. We show that undominated semistable outcomes are Pareto optimal and under strict preferences, every semistable outcome is Pareto optimal. Nevertheless, standard structural properties, such as the rural hospital theorem and the lattice properties, fail for semistable outcomes. We also discuss possible applications.
13. Selling information for bilateral trade
Robert Evans; In-Uck Park
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6624
Faculty of Economics, University of Cambridge; School of Economics, University of Bristol
Abstract:
We study the design and pricing of information by independent information providers for a buyer engaged in a trading relationship with a seller, and examine their welfare implications. While competitive information firms would sell full information about the value of the good, a monopolist supplies limited information in a particularly simple form—often resulting in higher total surplus than under full information, by influencing the seller's pricing decision. If only a single information structure can be offered, it takes a binary threshold form. If the provider can offer a menu of priced information structures before the seller sets a price, he offers a continuum of thresholds, inducing a unit‐elastic demand function for the seller. In both scenarios, the information firm increases total welfare when the seller's production cost is high but reduces it when the cost is low.
14. Repeated games with many players
Takuo Sugaya; Alexander Wolitzky
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6787
Graduate School of Business, Stanford University; Department of Economics, MIT
Abstract:
Motivated by the problem of sustaining cooperation in large groups with limited information, we analyze the relationship between group size, monitoring precision, and discounting in repeated games with independent, player‐level noise. The viability of cooperation under independent noise is linked to the per‐capita channel capacity of the stage‐game monitoring structure. We show that cooperation is impossible if the per‐capita channel capacity is much smaller than the discount rate. A folk theorem under a novel identification condition provides a near converse. If attention is restricted to co‐linear equilibria (a generalization of strongly symmetric equilibria), cooperation is possible only under much more severe parameter restrictions.
15. Optimal auction design with contingent payments and costly verification
Ian Ball; Teemu Pekkarinen
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6485
Department of Economics, MIT; Department of Economics, University of Vaasa
Abstract:
We study the design of an auction for an income‐generating asset such as an intellectual property license. Each bidder has a signal about his future income from acquiring the asset. After the asset is allocated, the winner's income from the asset is realized privately. The principal can audit the winner, at a cost, and then charge a payment contingent on the winner's realized income. We solve for an auction that maximizes the principal's revenue, net of auditing costs. The winning bidder is charged linear royalties up to a cap, beyond which there is no auditing. A higher bidder pays more in cash upfront and faces a lower royalty cap.
16. Informed communication equilibrium
Frédéric Koessler; Vasiliki Skreta
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6733
GREGHEC, HEC Paris – CNRS; UCL, UT Austin; CEPR
Abstract:
We consider a privately informed principal selecting a communication device to influence players' actions. In contrast to standard information design, the principal cannot commit ex ante, and the device must elicit information from all players. We define an informed communication equilibrium (informed CE) as a perfect Bayesian equilibrium outcome of this informed communication design game. We show that the set of informed CE is the subset of communication equilibria (CE) that yield principal payoff vectors bounded below by an equilibrium payoff vector of the silent game, under some consistent interim beliefs. The ex ante optimal CE may fail to be an informed CE. We provide sufficient conditions for a CE to be an informed CE and identify classes of games in which CE and informed CE coincide.
17. Information design in smooth games
Alex Smolin; Takuro Yamashita
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6143
Department of Economics, Toulouse School of Economics; Institute of Social and Economic Research, Osaka University
Abstract:
We study information design in games where players choose from a continuum of actions and have continuously differentiable payoffs. We show that an information structure is optimal when the equilibrium it induces can also be implemented in a principal–agent contracting problem. Building on this result, we characterize optimal information structures in symmetric linear‐quadratic games. With common values, targeted disclosure is robustly optimal across all priors. With interdependent and normally distributed values, linear disclosure is uniquely optimal. We illustrate our findings with applications in venture capital, Bayesian polarization, and price competition.

19. Contract terms monotonicity in matching markets
Cyril Rouault
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6473
CEPS, ENS Paris-Saclay, University of Paris-Saclay
Abstract:
We study doctor–hospital matching markets with contracts, in which hospitals can offer a range of contract terms to doctors. We show that introducing new terms can reduce doctors' welfare, whereas withdrawing terms may generate a Pareto improvement. We establish the preference domains under which welfare is preserved and show that only agent‐lexicographic preferences for all agents ensure that no doctor is worse off when additional terms are introduced. Since this condition is rarely satisfied in practice, our results imply that most real‐world markets are vulnerable to welfare losses when the set of contract terms expands.
20. Combinator dynamics: Multidimensional evolutionary learning
Srinivas Arigapudi; Omer Edhan; Yuval Heller; Ziv Hellman
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6279
Department of Economic Sciences, IIT Kanpur; Department of Economics, University of Manchester; Department of Economics, Bar-Ilan University
Abstract:
In complex strategic settings with multiple dimensions, players often simplify decisions by breaking them down into separate dimensions. In contrast to standard replicator dynamics, which assume perfect multidimensional coordination, our combinator dynamics model captures cases in which players revise actions dimension by dimension, imitate successful traits in each dimension, and then combine the selected traits into a new action. We analyze key properties of these dynamics—monotonicity, stationarity, and stability. We then extend the model to heterogeneous settings, where some players imitate entire multidimensional actions while others combine traits imitated separately in each dimension. We apply the framework to organizational decision‐making, where decentralized adoption of practices can generate coordination failures, persistent performance differences, and a trade‐off between specialized and versatile organizational traits.
21. Collaboration between and within groups
Matias Iaryczower; Santiago Oliveros; Parth Parihar
Theoretical Economics
ABS 4 | 2026-01-01 | DOI 10.3982/te6680
Department of Politics, Princeton University; Department of Economics, University of Bristol; Center of Excellence for Negotiations and Analytics, BASF
Abstract:
We study the ability of multigroup teams to undertake binary projects in a decentralized environment. The equilibrium outcomes of our model display familiar features in collaborative settings, including inefficient gradualism, inaction, and contribution cycles, wherein groups alternate taking responsibility for moving the project forward. Expected delay grows more than proportionally with project size, and some welfare‐enhancing projects are not completed, even as agents become arbitrarily patient. A team composed of two equally large groups can complete larger projects than a fully homogeneous team, even as the difference in preferences for completion among the two groups is arbitrarily small. Moreover, if the project is sufficiently large, the two‐group team always completes the project strictly faster.

Abstract:
Studies find that people systematically underestimate the likelihood of streaks in a random sequence. ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1017/s0022109026103470
Ca’ Foscari University of Venice; University of Turin, and Collegio Carlo Alberto; University of Oxford
Abstract:
Abstract We build a granular data set of residential property yields using rental and sale listings from a major German real estate platform. With more than 1.5 million property-level rent-to-price ratios, we document a novel heterogeneity puzzle. About two-fifths of yield dispersion remains unexplained after controlling for extensive observable property characteristics and postal code-level time-varying factors using fixed effects. Abstract The exchange rate predictability puzzle—that fundamentals fail to forecast short-horizon exchange rate movements out of sample—has long resisted resolution. 26. Demand‐Driven Propagation of Job Losses: Evidence from the Great Recession <sup>a</sup>
HA NGUYEN; JIAMING SOH
Journal of Money, Credit and Banking
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1111/jmcb.70106
Abstract:
Abstract This paper presents evidence for the demand‐driven propagation of job losses in the context of the United States during the Great Recession. Using county‐level tradable job losses driven by aggregate shocks as an instrument, it shows that retail and restaurant (R&R) employment fell by 0.37% for every 1% of job loss in the rest of the county's economy. The finding is not driven by the house price decline or by credit supply problems. Moreover, the spillover is more severe for R&R industries that are most affected by consumer demand shocks during the Great Recession, which strengthens the argument for the demand‐driven propagation of job losses.
27. A Conditional Shrinkage Method for Portfolio Selection
Nathan Lassance; Alberto Martín-Utrera
Review of Finance
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1093/rof/rfag052
LFIN/LIDAM, UCLouvain; Iowa State University
Abstract:
Abstract We develop a conditional shrinkage method for portfolio selection. Our approach builds on a decomposition of the mean-variance portfolio into the 1/N portfolio and an alpha component that offers large efficiency gains but is exposed to substantial estimation risk. We show that a linear shrinkage covariance matrix determines the portfolio’s relative exposure to these components, and our framework allows the shrinkage intensity to depend on a state variable that predicts their relative performance. 28. Beyond Leaderism: Recentering Leadership Science Towards Relationships in Action
Janaki Gooty; Chou‐Yu (Joey) Tsai; Tiffany Keller Hansbrough; Peter D. Harms; Sean N. Walsh
Journal of Management Studies
ABS 4 | 2026-11-04 | DOI 10.1111/joms.70176
UNC Charlotte; Binghamton University; University of Alabama
Abstract:
Abstract The historical origins of ‘Great Man’ theories of leadership, and their contemporary offshoots, have produced an accumulated literature in which the leader's behaviours are the focal unit of analysis. This Point article surfaces five theoretical assumptions underlying the leader behavioural paradigm: leaderism, individualistic attribution, behavioural universalism, democratization of leadership, and individual‐level ontology. We discuss theoretical counterfactuals to each assumption and call for a shift in the scene of scholarly conversation, from individual leaders to relationships that incorporate context and stakeholders. This shift in the focal unit of analysis from individual leader behaviour to relationships represents a foundational theoretical pivot built on multidisciplinary foundations, attention to levels of analysis, and a deep grounding in context. By questioning the assumptions underlying 85 years of leader behaviour research, we argue that the science (episteme), skill development (techné), and contextual judgement (phronesis) of leadership could be more tightly coupled by directing the spotlight towards relationships in context rather than individual leaders. In doing so, we move the focus from leaders in action to leadership as relationships in action .
29. Making agentic AI explainable and auditable
Gongtao Zhang; Jimmy Huang; Jonathan Seddon; Miao Wang
Journal of Information Technology
ABS 4 | 2026-11-03 | DOI 10.1177/02683962261508077
University of Nottingham; Audencia Business School Departement Entrepreneuriat Strategie et Innovation; University of Nottingham Ningbo China
Abstract:
Agentic AI promises operational speed, yet in finance, opaque decision paths hinder regulatory auditability. We address this autonomy paradox with a knowledge-enabled explainability (KEE) design theory that makes compliance an intrinsic, explicitly governed system property. KEE decouples probabilistic learning from an explicit, rule-governed reasoning layer, relocating epistemic uncertainty from opaque model inference to a semantic structure that can be inspected, contested, and updated.
